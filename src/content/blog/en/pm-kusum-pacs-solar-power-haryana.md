It may no longer be enough to see the country's Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS) as institutions limited only to crop loans, fertiliser-seed or farm inputs. The Ministry of Cooperation has included **"Convergence of PM-KUSUM at PACS level"** among its initiatives — in the words of the ministry's initiatives booklet (20.11.2025), farmers linked to PACS can adopt solar agricultural pumps and install solar (photovoltaic) modules on their fields. In the Haryana context this opportunity is even more interesting, because the state gives an additional state subsidy on pumps and Component-A plants are already installed.

> ⚠️ **Information checked up to 18 October 2026 — pay special attention to the scheme period.** The official site of MNRE (Ministry of New and Renewable Energy) states the period of PM-KUSUM as **"till 31.03.2026"**. According to news reports (trade press), in March 2026 the deadline only for completing already-sanctioned projects was extended to 31.03.2027 and work is going on on "PM-KUSUM 2.0" — we did not open that MNRE order. So **do not spend anything without asking DISCOM/HAREDA in writing whether new applications are open right now.** Sources for the other figures are below under "Sources".

> 💡 Here we are talking about **PACS** (Primary Agriculture Credit Society), not **CM-PACS** — the two are separate institutions (see: [What is CM-PACS](/blog/cm-pacs-kya-hai)).

## The three parts of PM-KUSUM

| Component | Scope |
| --- | --- |
| A | Decentralised, grid-connected renewable energy power plants (500 kW–2 MW; smaller than 500 kW too if the state wishes) — target 10,000 MW |
| B | Standalone solar agricultural pumps — target 14 lakh pumps |
| C | Solarisation of existing grid-connected agricultural pumps and feeders — target 35 lakh pumps |

Two of these models can be especially useful for PACS — selling electricity by setting up a solar power plant itself or through a developer (Component-A), and developing collective/cluster-based solar irrigation for members (Component-B). This article explains both in the Haryana context, including financing models, subsidy confusion and practical steps.

## Why this opportunity matters for PACS

Many PACS have struggled with weak income for years, because their earnings rested mainly on a single source — loan interest. Initiatives like PM-KUSUM can change this picture: they can give a **new source of income separate from lending** (electricity sales or land-lease income) — in Component-A the PPA with the DISCOM is for 25 years. At the same time, solar irrigation lowers irrigation cost for members, which benefits both the society and its members. In other words, this is not just a government scheme, but a possible path to make a weak PACS self-reliant — provided it is taken up with professional planning.

## Component-A: a PACS can itself become a Renewable Power Generator

According to MNRE's official site, Component-A plants can be set up by individual farmers, groups of farmers, **cooperatives**, Panchayats, FPOs and Water User Associations (these are called "Renewable Power Generator"). A PACS is a cooperative society, so it falls in this list. If these bodies cannot raise the capital themselves, they can also get the plant set up through a developer or the local DISCOM. That is, a capable PACS, if selected, can set up a solar plant and sell electricity to the DISCOM — a new revenue vertical separate from the lending business.

The DISCOM notifies how much additional capacity can be connected at each substation and invites applications. According to PIB, plants are set up **preferably within 5 km of a notified substation**, so that line cost and losses stay low. So just having land is not enough — first you need to check:

- Which notified substation the land is near
- Whether that substation has spare capacity for solar power injection
- Whether the DISCOM has invited an EOI/application for that capacity
- Whether there is any encumbrance on the land
- Whether grid connectivity is technically possible

A plant can be set up on farm land too, provided the solar modules are mounted on stilts (raised structure) so that farming can continue underneath — but there is no single fixed rule for how much land is needed; it depends on module technology, layout and site conditions.

## To whom and for how long the electricity will be sold

According to MNRE, the DISCOM buys Component-A electricity at the feed-in tariff fixed by the State Electricity Regulatory Commission (SERC), and according to PIB the selected applicant has to sign a **25-year PPA** with the DISCOM. In Haryana, for the first round this tariff is reported as **₹3.11 per kWh** — we found this figure as a reference in an order of the Punjab regulatory commission (PSERC) and in news reports; we did not open the original HERC order, so treat it as "matching pending".

> ⚠️ No new tariff figure should be assumed to apply automatically — in every new EOI it is necessary to re-verify the notified tariff, bidding conditions and HERC's latest applicable order.

## Will a PACS get a 75% subsidy? — this is where the confusion is greatest

**No, the 75% subsidy that is given in Haryana on solar pumps (Component-B) does not apply to Component-A.** According to MNRE and PIB, in Component-A there is no capital subsidy for the one setting up the plant; the DISCOM buys the electricity at the regulator-fixed tariff, and the incentive goes to the **DISCOM** — 40 paise per unit or ₹6.6 lakh per MW per year (whichever is lower), for 5 years from COD. It would be wrong to make a financial plan assuming the government will pay 75% of the plant cost.

## Where will the money come from — three financing models

| Model | When suitable |
| --- | --- |
| **PACS sets up the plant itself** | Has enough own funds/reserves, bye-laws and cooperative law allow such an investment, bank financing can also be arranged |
| **PACS gives land, developer sets up the plant** | Not enough equity — predictable rental income by leasing out the land |
| **Collective solar irrigation for members (Component-B)** | Members are mainly small farmers — the aim is not to sell electricity but to lower irrigation cost |

Banks have financing frameworks for projects based on PM-KUSUM Component-A, but a loan does not come automatically — the bank will look at project viability, the PPA, promoter contribution, land, approvals and cash flow. RBI has placed this component under priority sector lending (PIB, 2021). On the other hand, in the land-lease model a PACS can get lease income without taking the full capital risk — PIB's 2021 factsheet estimated earnings of up to ₹25,000 per acre per year by leasing land to a developer, and up to ₹65,000 per acre per year by setting up the plant oneself. These were government estimates, not guarantees — the actual lease rent depends on the agreement with the developer.

## Component-B: a more direct route for small farmers

In the national framework, the central financial assistance (CFA) on a standalone solar pump is **30%** of the benchmark cost, the state gives at least 30%, and the farmer at most 40%. **In Haryana**, according to HAREDA (Department of New and Renewable Energy), pumps of 3 to 10 HP get a **total subsidy of 75%** — 30% centre and 45% state, so the farmer's share is 25%.

Besides individual farmers, this component also covers Water User Associations and community/cluster-based irrigation. Under MNRE's 2020 amendment (PIB, 13.11.2020), for pumps installed and used by a **WUA, FPO or PACS**, or in cluster irrigation, central assistance is available even on pumps larger than 7.5 HP — taking up to 5 HP for each member of the group. That is, a PACS can form an irrigation cluster of its member farmers and consider a shared solar pump.

> ⚠️ **Before spending on any project**, it is necessary to verify in writing from UHBVN/DHBVN/HAREDA whether a fresh allocation, EOI or application window is open at present in the relevant component — the scheme's deadlines, subsidy pattern and empanelled vendors keep changing over time.

## What ground experience teaches

Experience from the first round shows that the road from sanction to commissioning is long — according to news reports, in 2026 HERC approved PPAs some of which had been signed in 2020, and the scheme deadline had to be extended again and again. That is, a solar project is not just a matter of buying and installing panels. It needs professional planning of everything — land, grid connectivity, project finance, PPA, EPC execution, statutory approvals, metering, commissioning and O&M. So before choosing an ownership model, a detailed project report (DPR) and techno-financial appraisal must be done.

## What a PACS should do first

1. **Preliminary resolution of the managing committee** — give formal permission to get a feasibility study done.
2. **Identify the land** — society-owned or willing members' land, and get details of the nearest substation from the DISCOM.
3. **Ask the DISCOM** — Component-A fresh capacity, spare capacity, when the EOI is open, applicable tariff, PPA terms.
4. **Ask HAREDA/District Renewable Energy Officer** (for Component-B) — whether a PACS/group application is allowed now, the current subsidy pattern, empanelled vendors.
5. **Get a DPR prepared** — only after all this, with techno-financial appraisal; get all three models compared.

## The accounting side — a new revenue vertical

If a PACS adopts a solar plant or lease-income model, this is a completely new kind of income for it, which needs separate and accurate accounts — electricity-sale/lease income, plant cost and depreciation, loan repayment, and O&M expenses. This is different from traditional loan-interest accounting, so it is better to handle it as separate project accounts, so that its real profitability is clearly visible and the audit stays smooth. A good digital accounting system helps track this new income stream separately from the rest of the business, in a transparent way.

## The three models — in summary

To make the decision easier, look at all three routes together: **Setting up the plant yourself** — highest possible return, but the most capital, risk and responsibility (suitable: strong, capital-capable PACS). **Leasing out land** — low risk, steady rental income, but limited return (suitable: one that has land but no capital). **Solar irrigation for members (Component-B)** — not selling electricity, but lowering members' irrigation cost (suitable: PACS with mostly small farmers). The right model is the one that matches your PACS's capital, land, member profile and risk capacity — so it is wise to have all three compared in the DPR.

## An important legal caution

Getting eligibility under PM-KUSUM does not by itself give a PACS internal permission to set up a large solar project. It is necessary to separately check your registered bye-laws, the state cooperative Act/Rules, the powers of the General Body/Managing Committee, borrowing limits and investment permissions — especially if society-owned land is to be given on a long lease of 20-25 years.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is PM-KUSUM only for farmers?** — No; in Component-A cooperatives (including PACS), FPOs, Panchayats and Water User Associations are also eligible, and in Component-B there is a special provision for pumps of PACS/FPO/WUA.
**Is there a 75% subsidy on Component-A?** — No; Haryana's 75% subsidy (30% centre + 45% state) is on standalone solar pumps (Component-B). In Component-A the DISCOM buys electricity at the tariff and the incentive goes to the DISCOM.
**Is the scheme running now?** — MNRE's site states the period as till 31.03.2026; according to news reports only already-sanctioned projects can be completed by 31.03.2027 and work is going on on PM-KUSUM 2.0. Ask DISCOM/HAREDA in writing whether new applications are open.
**How much risk will a PACS have to take?** — It depends on the model chosen — setting up the plant yourself means more risk and more possible return, leasing out land means less risk and steady rental income.
**What is the very first step before starting?** — Written confirmation from DISCOM/HAREDA of the current scheme window and capacity, and a preliminary resolution of the managing committee.
**Can a PACS set up the project as soon as it gets eligibility?** — No; it must first also check its bye-laws, the cooperative Act, borrowing/investment limits and the powers of the General Body/Board.
**Why is PM-KUSUM important for PACS?** — It gives a new and steady source of income separate from loan interest, which can help make a weak PACS self-reliant.
**Main difference between Component-A and Component-B?** — In A, income by selling electricity (at the DISCOM tariff); in B, lowering irrigation cost for members with solar pumps (with subsidy).
**Which financing model to choose?** — If you have capital, your own plant; if you have land but no capital, lease; if mostly small farmers, Component-B — get them compared in the DPR.
**Is it necessary to keep separate accounts for a solar plant?** — Yes; it is a new income stream, and keeping it in separate project accounts is better for transparency and audit.

## Three things to remember

- ⚡ PACS and CM-PACS are separate institutions — do not mix them up; this initiative can open a new income source for PACS.
- 💰 Haryana's 75% subsidy (30% centre + 45% state) is on solar pumps (Component-B), not on Component-A (power plant).
- 📝 The scheme period was till 31.03.2026 — before any spending, ask DISCOM/HAREDA in writing whether new applications are open.
- 🧮 Choose the right model (own plant / land lease / Component-B) according to your capital, land and member profile; get them compared in the DPR.

## Finally

PM-KUSUM is a real opportunity for a PACS to go beyond its traditional role — by becoming a power producer, through land lease, or by lowering members' irrigation cost. But caution is just as necessary with this opportunity: it is not just a matter of buying and installing panels, but of professional planning of land, grid, finance, PPA and approvals. So the right path is — first a managing committee resolution, then written confirmation from DISCOM/HAREDA, then a DPR and techno-financial appraisal — and only after that, investment. The biggest lesson is this: eligibility is a start, success lies in preparation, and verifying every figure/window before spending is a must.
