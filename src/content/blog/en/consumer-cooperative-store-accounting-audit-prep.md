# How should a consumer cooperative store keep its accounts? Audit preparation for purchases, sales, cash and stock

For example, suppose that on the last day of the month, in a consumer store, the stock register says there should be 120 bags of sugar, but when counted in the godown there are 112. The same evening, the counter's sales total is ₹41,300, but the safe holds ₹40,650. And a member is saying he repaid his full credit last week, while the register still shows a balance due.

All three mistakes look small, but to the auditor all three say the same thing: **purchases, sales, cash and stock are running in four separate registers and do not match each other.** A consumer store has hundreds of small transactions every day, so keeping accounts here demands more discipline than in any other society. This article is a map of that discipline: what to match daily, what weekly, what at year-end, and which papers to keep ready before the audit.

> 📘 **In short:** The whole account of a consumer store rests on one equation: **opening stock + purchases − purchase returns − (cost of goods sold) = closing stock.** This is what produces the Trading Account and the gross profit. So keep purchases, sales and stock not separately but **in one chain**, split each day's sales into cash/bank-UPI/credit and match them with cash, and match the register with a physical count at least once a year. Under Sections 95(3) and 97(1) of the Haryana Act, the auditor and the inspector have access to **stock**, so the match between the count and the register is the first question of the audit.

The depth of stock, that is, the quantity formula, weighted average and counting method, is covered in detail in [Inventory and stock management](/blog/inventory-and-stock-management). This article is the **view from above**: where stock sits in the full accounting cycle of a consumer store, and how it connects with the other three things (purchases, sales, cash).

## Why a consumer store's accounting is different

In an agricultural credit society, money moves mainly as loans and deposits. In a consumer store, money moves **as goods**, and a few things make it special:

- **Many small transactions:** dozens of sale bills every day, each a small amount. A single day's slip becomes a heap the next day.
- **Heavy movement of cash:** cash, UPI and credit all run at the counter.
- **Stock is the biggest asset:** usually the largest value in the balance sheet. A mistake in counting or valuing stock shakes the whole profit.
- **Credit to members:** each member's dues, recovery and limit are a separate account.
- **Shelf life and breakage:** room for expiry, damage and theft.
- **Tax:** if GST applies, it has to be accounted for in every purchase and sale. (Details: [GST for cooperative societies](/blog/gst-for-cooperatives).)

That is why the rule here is: **a small daily check of everything, so that there is no surprise in the big year-end check.**

## The one equation everything rests on

A consumer store's profit comes straight from this question: *at what cost was the sold stock bought?* It comes from four things:

> **Cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases − purchase returns − closing stock**
> **Gross profit = net sales − cost of goods sold**

A hypothetical example *(all figures are only for explanation)*:

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Opening stock (start of the year) | ₹2,40,000 |
| (+) Purchases during the year | ₹10,60,000 |
| (−) Purchase returns (returned to supplier) | ₹20,000 |
| **Goods available for sale** | **₹12,80,000** |
| (−) Closing stock (value of physical count) | ₹2,80,000 |
| **Cost of goods sold** | **₹10,00,000** |
| Total sales | ₹14,00,000 |
| (−) Sales returns | ₹30,000 |
| **Net sales** | **₹13,70,000** |
| **Gross profit** | **₹3,70,000** |

Now see why this matters: if the closing stock count comes out ₹50,000 short (the goods are there, but were not counted), the cost of goods sold will *go up* by ₹50,000 and the gross profit will go down by the same amount. The other way round, if stock is over-counted, profit will rise falsely. **The truth of closing stock is the truth of profit,** and that is why it is tested first in the audit. (The Trading Account link: [Year-end and FY lock](/blog/year-end-closing-and-fy-lock).)

## 1. Purchase checklist

**Before and when the goods arrive:**

- [ ] **Purchase Order** with a fixed supplier, rate and quantity, especially for large purchases.
- [ ] **Goods receipt (GRN):** count the goods that arrived and check quality and expiry date; match all three: **order, challan and the actual goods.**
- [ ] Record goods that arrived damaged or short in writing **the same day** and inform the supplier.

**After the bill arrives:**

- [ ] The bill's rate, quantity and GST should **match the order**; if there is a difference, settle it before payment.
- [ ] The bill should be **posted in the books as a purchase** the same day and the goods **added to stock**: both together, not separately.
- [ ] Match the **supplier account** (how much is still payable) with the supplier's statement once a month.
- [ ] Payment within the fixed period, with approval and supporting documents. ([What is a voucher](/blog/voucher-narration-and-documents), [Payment methods](/blog/bank-payment-methods-cheque-dd-upi).)
- [ ] If there is a **purchase return**, a separate voucher for it, a reduction in stock and an adjustment in the supplier account: all three.

> 💡 **For the secretary/accountant/store manager:** The most common purchase slip is that the bill was posted in the books but the goods were not added to stock (or the other way round). In SahakarLekha, the voucher made from a **purchase** and the stock run in one chain. The **Purchase Orders (PO/GRN)** page records orders and goods receipts, and the **Purchase Return** page posts purchase returns to both stock and accounts. Checking whether the bill is correct remains your job.

## 2. Sales and member-credit checklist

**Daily sales:**

- [ ] A **serially numbered bill/cash memo** for every sale; no gaps or duplicates in bill numbers.
- [ ] At the end of the day, split sales into **three parts: cash, bank/UPI and credit.** The total of the three should equal total sales.
- [ ] Approval and record of **discounts** and **rate changes** (member rate vs retail rate).
- [ ] **Sales returns:** which bill, how much, in what form it was returned (cash refund, bank refund or adjustment in credit), and whether the goods came back into stock or turned out damaged.
- [ ] Goods whose **stock is zero or low** should not be sold; otherwise the register and the actual goods go apart.

**Member credit:**

- [ ] The **credit limit** should be fixed by the society's policy and recorded member-wise. (This is not a legal limit but the society's own policy; maximum limits are fixed by the bye-laws and the general body's decision.)
- [ ] **A receipt for every recovery** and an immediate entry in the account; this is what prevents the "member says he has repaid" problem above.
- [ ] **Age of dues (ageing):** the list of dues over 30, 60, 90 days goes before the managing committee once a month.
- [ ] The **list of defaulters** at the AGM (Section 25(3)) is made from this same register. (See: [AGM preparation](/blog/agm-preparation-checklist).)

> 💡 **For the counter in-charge/accountant:** Splitting daily sales into three parts and matching them with cash is the most tiring job by hand. SahakarLekha's **Retail Counter (POS)** records sales as cash, bank or member credit, does not allow sales beyond stock, and makes choosing a member compulsory for credit. If the member's credit limit is recorded and a new credit sale crosses it, a **warning** appears, but the sale is not stopped; whether to stop it or not is the secretary's/committee's judgement. The **Consumer Registers** page shows the counter summary and the dues register, **Member Credit** keeps credit sales, recoveries and the adjustment of sales returns, **Price Lists** hold separate retail, category and member rates, and **Sales Return** records returns as cash, bank or credit adjustment. Fixing the limit, what to do on a warning and when to tighten recovery is the society's decision.

## 3. Cash checklist

The most common source of cash trouble in a consumer store is: **not matching the sales total and the safe total every day.**

**At the end of the day (daily):**

- [ ] **Counter cash sales** (as per the register/POS) − cash refunds − cash expenses = cash that should be in the safe. Match this with a **physical count.**
- [ ] If there is a difference, find the reason **the same day**: short (shortage) or more (excess); record it in writing.
- [ ] Check whether **bank/UPI sales** reached the bank: UPI amounts reach the bank statement in two or three days; keep a list of **pending UPI.**
- [ ] **Deposit in the bank** whatever cash is above the limit of the bye-laws/higher body; keep the deposit slip.

**Weekly/monthly:**

- [ ] A surprise cash check in front of the chairman or another office-bearer.
- [ ] **Monthly BRS** (bank reconciliation). ([How to do BRS](/blog/bank-reconciliation-guide).)
- [ ] If there are separate counters or cashiers, **a separate total for each**, so that it is known with whom a difference arose.

The full cash-handling checklist is in [Cash handling and verification](/blog/cash-handling-and-verification), and from the treasurer's point of view in [The treasurer's accounting work](/blog/treasurer-accounting-work-checklist).

## 4. Stock checklist

Stock is where the consumer store's money "sleeps". Handle it in three layers:

**Daily/weekly layer:**

- [ ] A **master** for each item: name, category, unit, barcode/code, and its **sales account** (which category of sales it goes to).
- [ ] A small weekly count (cycle count) of **fast-selling** and **expensive** items.
- [ ] **Expiry/breakage:** remove goods that are no longer fit for sale from stock with **written approval**; do not let them disappear quietly.
- [ ] A list of goods that are **selling slowly** and a decision on them (discount, return, stop buying).

**Monthly layer:**

- [ ] Matching **register vs godown** for selected items, and the reason for any difference.
- [ ] A report of **stock value.** Whatever valuation method (FIFO or weighted average) you have chosen, keep it **the same every year**; changing it again and again makes profit comparisons meaningless. (Comparison of methods: [Inventory and stock management](/blog/inventory-and-stock-management).)

**Year-end layer:**

- [ ] **Full physical count:** on the last date of the year, with the signatures of both the counter and the checker on the count sheet.
- [ ] The **difference** between the count and the register listed, with reasons, and approval for whatever is a loss.
- [ ] The **value of closing stock** should come from this count, and the same figure should go into the Trading Account, the Balance Sheet and other reports, the same everywhere.

> 💡 **For the store manager/accountant:** At year-end, different stock figures (one thing in the register, another in the report) are the biggest headache. In SahakarLekha, **Inventory**, **Stock Valuation** (FIFO or weighted average) and the **Closing Stock Report** (with columns for opening stock, purchases, returns etc.) are all made from the same stock data. The **Expiry & Damage** page writes off goods no longer fit for sale from stock, and **Barcode Labels** lets you print labels for items. Doing the physical count, deciding the reason for differences and approving losses is your job, not the software's.

## Audit preparation: what the law says, what the practice is

| Point | Source | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Auditor's access | Section 95(3) | Open access for the person auditing to books, papers, vouchers, **stock** and other property; power to verify cash and securities |
| Scope of audit | Section 95(2) | Valuation of assets and liabilities, examination of the Balance Sheet and profit and loss, overdue loans, verification of cash and securities |
| Inspection | Section 97(1) | Access for the Registrar/authorised person to books, vouchers, securities, **stock** and property; power to verify cash |
| Every officer to give information | Section 95(4) | Committee members, manager, employees to give the auditor the information asked for |
| Incomplete accounts | Section 95(6),(7) | If accounts are incomplete at the time of audit, the Registrar can get them written up; cost from the society or the responsible person |

**What I did not find in the law:** in the Haryana Act 1984 I did not find separate accounting rules for consumer stores, how often or how stock should be counted, any compulsory method of stock valuation, or a provision on cash limits. So in the list below, counting, valuation and limits are **good practice**; if your society has any instruction from the Registrar, the higher body or the bye-laws, that comes first.

**Keep ready before the audit:**

- [ ] **Purchase file:** PO, GRN, supplier bills, payment vouchers, purchase-return papers.
- [ ] **Sales file:** serially numbered bills/cash memos, daily sales summary (cash/UPI/credit), sales-return register.
- [ ] **Cash:** record of the daily count, note of cash shortage/excess, bank deposit slips, monthly BRS.
- [ ] **Stock:** item-wise register, the year-end **signed count sheet**, statement of differences, approval for expiry/damage, stock valuation report.
- [ ] **Member credit:** member-wise dues, recovery receipts, ageing analysis.
- [ ] **Resolution of previous audit objections** and the managing committee's resolution.
- [ ] **Custody list:** which register is with whom. (Full audit preparation: [Audit preparation checklist](/blog/audit-preparation-checklist), [How a cooperative society audit works](/blog/how-cooperative-society-audit-works).)

> ⚠️ **For PACS readers:** If your PACS also runs the activity of selling consumer goods, fertiliser-seed or other goods and its entries are made on the prescribed ERP/portal (e-PACS), make those entries as per that prescribed portal's process and the higher body's instructions. This article's checklist is not a replacement for it; it is for understanding it. (On e-PACS: [e-PACS and day-end](/blog/e-pacs-haryana-erp-day-end-audit-explained).)

## A short hypothetical example

*(Hypothetical: not of any real store.)* Suppose a store goes on assuming "all is well" at the end of every month, and when it counts at year-end it finds goods ₹68,000 less than the register. When they sit down to find the reason, out come credit given without bills, member dues not cleared even after repayment, expired goods quietly thrown away, and two purchase bills posted in the books but not added to stock, one by one, but now nobody can tell which mistake happened when.

If the same store did just three things every week: a small count of fast items, matching the daily sales total with cash, and written approval for expired goods, this same difference would have been caught in the first month, when the reason is still remembered.

## Common mistakes

1. **Not matching the sales total and the safe total every day.** The difference piles up over months and looks large.
2. **Posting the purchase bill in the books but not adding the goods to stock** (or the other way round).
3. **Counting stock only once a year, without signatures.**
4. **Removing expired/damaged goods without approval.** The loss stays hidden.
5. **Not giving a receipt for member recoveries** or not posting them in the account at once.
6. **Recording sales without stock:** a difference between the register and the actual goods.
7. **Changing the valuation method every year.** Profit comparisons become meaningless.
8. **Different figures for closing stock:** one in the register, another in the Trading Account.
9. **Not returning goods to stock on a sales return** or not identifying damaged goods separately.
10. **In PACS, running your own system in place of the prescribed portal's process.**

## Three things to remember

- 🔗 Purchases, sales, cash and stock are one chain: cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases − returns − closing stock, so the truth of closing stock is the truth of profit.
- 📆 Small daily checks (sales total vs cash, goods receipt, approval of expiry) prevent big year-end trouble; a signed physical count at year-end is a necessary habit.
- ⚖️ The law gives the auditor and the inspector access to stock (Sections 95(3), 97(1)); the counting method, valuation and cash limit come from the bye-laws/higher body's instructions, and in PACS the prescribed portal is final.

## Frequently asked questions

**How should a consumer cooperative store keep its accounts?** — Keep purchases, sales, cash and stock in one chain: the purchase entry and the addition to stock from the goods receipt, splitting daily sales into cash/UPI/credit and matching them with cash, and matching the register with a physical count during the year.
**How is the cost of goods sold worked out?** — Opening stock + purchases − purchase returns − closing stock. This gives the gross profit (net sales − cost of goods sold).
**When should the physical count of stock be done?** — A full count at year-end, and in between small counts of fast-selling and expensive items. I did not find the frequency of counting in the law; it depends on the bye-laws/higher body's instructions and good practice.
**What does the auditor look at in stock?** — As per Section 95(3), the auditor has open access to stock. In practice he looks at register vs count, the signatures on the count sheet, the approval of differences, the valuation, and whether closing stock matches the Trading Account.
**What is the legal limit on credit to members?** — I did not find any fixed legal limit on member credit for consumer stores. It is fixed by the society's policy and the bye-laws/general body's decision.
**How should expired or broken goods be recorded?** — Remove them from stock with written approval and post them in the books as a loss; do not let them disappear quietly, so that the reason for the loss shows in the audit.
**What to do if there is a difference between the cash count and the sales total?** — Find the reason the same day; if it is not found, record it in writing as a cash shortage or excess and tell the secretary/chairman.
**Is there any compulsory method of stock valuation?** — I did not find a compulsory method in the Haryana Act. Whatever method (FIFO or weighted average) is chosen, keep it the same every year and check the auditor's/higher body's instructions.
**How should a PACS keep accounts of consumer sales?** — If its entries are made on the prescribed portal (e-PACS), follow that portal's process and the higher body's instructions; this article is a supplement, not an alternative.
**Which papers should be kept ready before the audit?** — Purchase and sales files, daily sales summaries, cash counts and BRS, signed stock count sheets, approval of expiry, ageing analysis of member credit, and the resolution of previous objections.

## In the end

A consumer store's biggest strength is its daily crowd, and so is its biggest challenge. A store that makes small daily checks a habit finds that its year-end accounting is a job of "matching", not "searching", and in front of the auditor the count, the register and the books all tell the same story.
