A member of a cooperative society, or an outsider, often wonders — can we ask our society for information directly under the RTI (Right to Information) Act? In most cases the answer is "no, not directly" — but one reliable alternative route always stays open: **through the Registrar, Cooperative Societies.** This article explains in simple language why the society itself is not treated as a public authority, and how and where the right information can be sought.

## Section 2(h): what is a "public authority"?

The RTI Act applies only to a "public authority" — an RTI application cannot be made directly to a private body. Under Section 2(h), a public authority is a body formed by the Constitution, by a law of Parliament or a state legislature, or by a government notification/order — and it also includes a body that is **owned**, **controlled** by the government, or **substantially financed** by government funds (directly or indirectly) — any one of the three tests is enough.

### The three tests — in detail

Understand the three tests of Section 2(h) one by one:

- **Ownership:** Is the body owned by the government? Most cooperative societies are owned by their members, not by the government.
- **Control:** Does the government have deep, pervasive control over the management? Generally not — management is done by the elected managing committee.
- **Substantial financing:** Is the body run substantially on government funds? This is hard to prove, and the burden is on the applicant.

If even one of these fully applies, the body can be treated as a public authority — but for most ordinary societies, none of the three clearly applies.

## Why the society itself is not treated as a public authority

There is a common misunderstanding here that "it is registered under the government, so it is governmental". But merely being registered under a cooperative Act does not make a society a government body. In **Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank v. State of Kerala** (7 October 2013; (2013) 16 SCC 82; Justices K.S. Radhakrishnan and A.K. Sikri), the Supreme Court made it clear (para 34) that "control" means **substantial control**; mere "supervision" or "regulation" under a statute does not make a body a public authority. The Registrar's powers under a cooperative Act are only regulatory/supervisory, because management lies by law with the managing committee. Most PACS, house building or marketing societies — managed by a committee elected by the members — do not qualify as public authorities on this test. According to the judgment (para 40), the burden of showing ownership, control or substantial financing is on **the person seeking information (or the government)**, and the Information Commission can examine this question.

> 📘 The Thalappalam judgment cited an earlier ruling which said that "deep and pervasive" control of the government over a cooperative society would be held to exist only when the tests of *Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib* (1981) are satisfied — mere general regulation is not enough.

## "Control" vs "supervision" — a fine but important difference

The root of this whole question lies in a fine legal difference — between **control** and **supervision**. When the government interferes deeply in the day-to-day management of a body (who will run it, what decisions will be taken), that is "control" — and such a body can become an "instrumentality" of the government, that is, a public authority. But when the government only watches from outside (getting audits done, approving bye-laws, holding elections) — that is mere "supervision", and it does not make the body governmental. Most cooperative societies are managed by a managing committee elected by their members, not by the government — so they fall within "supervision", not "control", and that is why they do not qualify as public authorities.

## But one route is definitely open — through the Registrar

This is where the biggest misunderstanding happens. Even if the society itself is not a public authority, **the Registrar, Cooperative Societies, is itself a public authority** (Thalappalam, para 52). The definition of "information" in **Section 2(f)** of the RTI Act also covers information "relating to any private body which can be accessed by a public authority under any other law". So, according to the judgment, the Registrar must give the information in his possession, and he can also call for information from the society, to the extent the cooperative Act allows, and give it — subject to the exemptions in Section 8. The question is not "is the society a public authority" — the question is "is this information within the Registrar's statutory reach?"

## How far the Registrar's reach goes in Haryana

The Haryana Cooperative Societies Act 1984 gives the Registrar wide reach through these provisions:

| Section | Power |
| --- | --- |
| Section 48 (new in 2013) | Every society must file returns with the Registrar within 6 months of the end of the year — annual report, audited accounts, plan for distribution of surplus, list of bye-law amendments, declaration of the date of the general meeting and elections |
| Section 95 (new in 2013) | Audit every year; the auditor has full access to the books, accounts, cash and property |
| Section 97 | The Registrar or a person authorised by him can inspect the society and see all its records |
| Section 98 | The Registrar on his own, or on the application of a majority of the committee or one-third of the members, can hold an inquiry into the constitution, working and financial condition of the society |
| Section 50 | Search and seizure where records may be tampered with or destroyed |
| Section 51 | Inquiry against a custodian who does not hand over records, and an order to hand them over |

So things like the audit report, annual returns, Balance Sheet, bye-law amendments and election-related information can usually be sought from the Registrar through RTI — provided that information is legally within the Registrar's reach and does not fall under any exemption in Section 8.

> 💡 **Another direct route for a member — Section 120:** After the 2013 amendment, Section 120(1) of the Haryana Act says every society shall keep the Act, the rules, its bye-laws and the **list of members** open for **free inspection** at its registered address. Under Section 120(2), a society may give each of its members access to the books, information and accounts kept in the regular course of transactions with that member (on a fee fixed by the Registrar). That is, for information about your own account or transactions, make a written request directly to the society before going for RTI.

## How to file an RTI with the Registrar — general steps

Since the route is through the Registrar (who is a public authority), address the application to him. The general RTI procedure is as follows:

1. **Choose the right public authority:** the Registrar/Assistant Registrar (ARCS) office concerned, under whose jurisdiction the society falls.
2. **Write the application:** on plain paper, with clear and specific questions — what information is needed, and for which period.
3. **Send it to the PIO:** to the Public Information Officer (PIO) of that office, with the fee fixed in the state's RTI rules. Under Section 7(5), no fee is charged from applicants below the poverty line (BPL).
4. **Wait for the reply:** under Section 7(1), the PIO must give the information or refuse it with reasons within **30 days** (48 hours where life or liberty is involved). If no reply comes in time, it is treated as a refusal (Section 7(2)), and if there is delay, the information is given free (Section 7(6)).
5. **Appeal:** if there is no reply or you are not satisfied, file a first appeal within **30 days** with a senior officer of the same department (First Appellate Authority), and then a second appeal within **90 days** with the State Information Commission (Section 19).

> ⚠️ The fee amount and the application format are in the state's RTI rules — confirm with the office concerned or the portal before applying.

## What information can be sought — examples

Information within the Registrar's statutory reach that can generally be sought:

- The society's **audit report** and audit objections.
- **Balance Sheet and annual accounts** (which are filed with the Registrar).
- Copies of **general body/board proceedings (minutes)** — only when they are with the Registrar (for example in an inquiry or inspection file).
- The society's **annual return** (Section 48: annual report, audited accounts, surplus plan).
- **Bye-laws and their amendments**.
- **Election-related records**.

On the other hand, the society's internal information that is outside the Registrar's reach, and the private information of members, is generally not given (see below).

## There are limits too

Keep three big limits in mind: (1) information that the Registrar cannot obtain under the cooperative Act at all will not be available through RTI either. In Thalappalam (para 52), the court said no provision had been shown under which the Registrar could call for details of members' bank accounts in a cooperative bank. (2) The private information of an individual member is not given under **Section 8(1)(j)** unless the PIO is satisfied that a larger public interest justifies it (Thalappalam, para 53). (3) If you claim that the society itself is a public authority (directly, without the Registrar), the burden is on you to prove that the society cannot run at all without government help — this is not easy.

> ⚠️ This is general legal information, in the Haryana context; in other states the section numbers may differ, and the final decision depends on the interpretation of the court/Information Commission — take expert advice in a specific case.

## An example — how it works

Suppose a member of a PACS wants to see last year's audit report because he doubts the accounts, but the society is not showing it to him. He cannot file an RTI directly on the society (it is not a public authority). But he can file an RTI application with the PIO of his district's **Assistant Registrar (ARCS)** and ask for that society's audit report available with the Registrar — because audit is within the Registrar's statutory reach (Section 95 in Haryana). In this way, even though the society is "not a public authority", the member gets the information through the right, lawful route. This is the practical essence of this whole article.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can we file an RTI application directly with a cooperative society?** — Usually not, unless the society is owned/controlled/substantially financed by the government.
**Then how do we get the information?** — By filing an RTI application with the Registrar, Cooperative Societies (and the ARCS in the district) — he is a public authority.
**What information can be obtained?** — The audit report, annual returns, Balance Sheet, bye-laws and election-related information that is legally within the Registrar's reach (subject to the exemptions in Section 8).
**Can another member's private information be obtained?** — Normally not; it is protected under Section 8(1)(j), without a larger public interest.
**Where and how to file an RTI application?** — With the PIO of the Registrar/ARCS office concerned, with clear questions and the prescribed fee; appeal if not satisfied.
**Why does the difference between "control" and "supervision" matter?** — Control can make a body a public authority; mere supervision (audit/elections) does not.
**What if no reply comes?** — If no reply comes within 30 days, it is treated as a refusal; then a first appeal within 30 days, and after that a second appeal within 90 days to the State Information Commission (Sections 7 and 19).
**Can a member get his own information directly from the society?** — Yes, in Haryana under Section 120: the list of members, bye-laws, the Act and the rules can be inspected free, and the books/accounts of one's own transactions on a fee fixed by the Registrar.
**Can a society ever be a public authority directly?** — Yes, as an exception — if government ownership/control or substantial financing is proved; but the burden is on the applicant.
**What is the difference between "control" and "supervision"?** — Deep interference in management = control; only audit/elections/approval of bye-laws = supervision (this does not make it a public authority).

## Three things to remember

- ⚖️ The society itself is not a public authority (usually) — but the Registrar, Cooperative Societies, is, so the route is through him.
- 📂 Information within the Registrar's statutory reach (audit report, annual returns, accounts, bye-laws, election-related information) can be sought through RTI.
- 🔒 Another member's private information stays protected under Section 8(1)(j).
- 📝 Apply to the PIO of the Registrar/ARCS; if not satisfied, the route of first and second appeal is open.

## Can a society itself be a public authority?

Yes, in some situations — but this is the exception, not the rule. If a society is under the direct ownership/control of the government, or is so financed by government funds that it cannot run without them, it can be treated as a public authority and RTI can apply to it directly. But the burden of proving this is on the applicant, and merely receiving some government grant/assistance is not treated as "substantial financing". So in practice, in most cases the most reliable route remains through the Registrar — compared with making a claim directly against the society, it is easier and more successful.

## Transparency — the member's real tool

RTI is not just a legal process but a powerful tool of transparency and accountability in members' hands. If a member feels the society's accounts are not being kept properly, audit objections are not being settled, or elections/meetings are not being held as per rules — then RTI through the Registrar is a lawful, peaceful way to find out the truth. But another truth is this — if the society itself keeps its accounts transparent, up to date and open to members, the need for RTI rarely arises. So the best society is one that shares its accounts even before being asked — that is the real cooperative spirit.

## In the end

So the gist is this — most cooperative societies are not themselves "public authorities", so RTI does not apply to them directly; but **the Registrar, Cooperative Societies, is a public authority**, and any citizen can seek information within his statutory reach (audit report, annual returns, accounts, bye-laws, election-related information) through RTI. And in Haryana, a member can also ask the society directly for his own information under Section 120. The right question is not "is the society a public authority or not" but "is this information within the Registrar's reach or not". This knowledge empowers members — and for transparent societies it is no threat, but a confirmation of trust.
