# Financial Ratios: A Society's Health at a Glance

If you want to understand a society's health quickly without reading long reports, **financial ratios** are the fastest way. They are simple divisions that give meaning to one figure by relating it to another. This article explains some of the most useful ratios, with their formulas, examples and the right way to read them, in simple language.

## Why ratios are useful

A single figure is incomplete — whether "₹5 lakh profit" is good or bad becomes clear only when you relate it to capital or sales. Ratios make exactly this link, and make year-to-year comparison easy. ₹5 lakh profit on ₹50 lakh capital is 10% (fine), but on ₹5 crore capital it is only 1% (weak) — this is the difference a ratio shows.

Ratios have three big uses: **comparison** (with your own previous year, or with similar societies), **trend** (is the society improving or slipping), and **early warning** (if a ratio starts to worsen, you can act in time). That is why both management and auditors watch ratios.

## Useful ratios

1. **Liquidity (Current Ratio) = current assets ÷ current liabilities.** Shows how well the society can pay its short-term liabilities. ~1.5–2 is considered healthy.
2. **Recovery ratio (Recovery %) = amount recovered ÷ amount due.** The most important one for a society that gives loans — high = strong.
3. **NPA ratio = non-performing loans ÷ total loans.** The lower, the better; a rising NPA is a warning.
4. **Cost-to-Income ratio = expenses ÷ income.** Lower = efficient operation.
5. **Reserve ratio = reserve fund ÷ capital.** A strong reserve = the strength to absorb shocks.

> 💡 The real value of a ratio is in the **trend** — do not look at one year's figure, look at the direction over three years. A falling recovery ratio or a climbing NPA is a signal to act early.

## Each ratio — with an example

### 1. Liquidity (Current Ratio)

Suppose the society's current assets are ₹6,00,000 and current liabilities are ₹3,00,000. Liquidity = 6,00,000 ÷ 3,00,000 = **2.0** — that is, ₹2 of assets for every ₹1 of short-term liability. This is healthy. If it goes below 1, the society may face difficulty in short-term payments. A very high figure (like 5) is not always good either — it can mean money is lying unused.

### 2. Recovery ratio (Recovery %)

If the total due was ₹10,00,000 and ₹8,50,000 was recovered, then recovery = 8,50,000 ÷ 10,00,000 × 100 = **85%**. For a credit society this is the most important indicator — a high recovery percentage shows a strong society. A falling percentage is a signal for an immediate recovery drive. (See: [Loan recovery](/blog/loan-recovery-before-year-end).)

### 3. NPA ratio

If total loans are ₹20,00,000 and ₹2,00,000 of that is non-performing (heading towards bad debt), then NPA = 2,00,000 ÷ 20,00,000 × 100 = **10%**. The lower, the better. A rising NPA directly affects both the society's earnings and its health, so watch it regularly.

### 4. Cost-to-Income ratio

If total expenses are ₹7,00,000 and total income is ₹10,00,000, then cost-to-income = 7,00,000 ÷ 10,00,000 × 100 = **70%** — that is, ₹70 spent for every ₹100 of income. A lower ratio = more efficient operation. A rising ratio signals that expenses are growing faster than income.

### 5. Reserve ratio

If the reserve fund is ₹8,00,000 and capital is ₹20,00,000, then reserve ratio = 8,00,000 ÷ 20,00,000 × 100 = **40%**. A strong reserve gives the society the strength to absorb a bad year or an unexpected shock. (See: [Profit distribution and reserves](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves).)

## Some more useful ratios

Apart from the five above, these ratios also help, depending on the type of society:

- **Return on Capital = surplus ÷ capital × 100.** Shows how much the members' capital is earning. For example, ₹2,00,000 surplus on ₹20,00,000 capital = 10%.
- **Credit-Deposit Ratio = loans given ÷ total deposits × 100.** In a banking-type society it shows how much of the deposits is lent out — too high = liquidity risk, too low = money lying idle.
- **Business per member = total business ÷ number of members.** A measure of the society's reach among members and their activity.
- **Expense growth rate = (this year's expenses − last year's expenses) ÷ last year's expenses × 100.** If expenses are growing faster than income, this is a warning.

Even from these, pick the ones most relevant to your society — you do not need to look at all of them together.

## A "health card" example

Putting several ratios in one place gives a one-glance "health card" of the society:

| Ratio | Value | Signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liquidity | 2.0 | 🟢 Good |
| Recovery % | 85% | 🟡 Fine, room to improve |
| NPA % | 10% | 🔴 High, needs attention |
| Cost-to-income % | 70% | 🟡 Room to improve |
| Reserve ratio | 40% | 🟢 Strong |
| Return on capital | 10% | 🟢 Fine |

From this card the managing committee sees at once that the society's foundation (liquidity, reserves) is strong, but loan quality (NPA 10%) needs immediate attention, and there is room to improve recovery and costs. This is the power of ratios — the whole health picture on one page.

## Trend — the real story is here

One year's ratio is a photograph; three years of ratios are a story. So look at every ratio year by year:

| Ratio | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recovery % | 88% | 85% | 80% | 🔴 Falling |
| NPA % | 6% | 8% | 11% | 🔴 Rising |
| Liquidity | 1.8 | 1.9 | 2.0 | 🟢 Stable/good |

In this table liquidity is fine, but recovery is falling and NPA is rising — a clear warning that loan management needs immediate attention. Year-3 figures alone do not tell such a clear story; the trend does.

## A caution

Ratios **point**, they do not decide. An unusual ratio means investigate, not panic — the cause is often a one-time event.

> ⚠️ Benchmark levels change with the type of society and state rules — the "good" ratios of a purchase-sale society and a credit society are not the same.

## Limits of ratios

Ratios are very useful, but it is important to understand their limits too, so that you do not trust them blindly:

- **They tell "what", not "why".** A falling recovery ratio shows there is a problem, but you have to find the cause (drought, local slowdown, or slack recovery) yourself.
- **They depend on quality.** Ratios are only as correct as the books; messy figures give wrong ratios.
- **One-time events can distort them.** A large one-off income or expense can make a year's ratio look unusual.
- **Context is needed.** A ratio alone, without comparison (previous year/similar society), is incomplete.

So treat ratios as a starting point — they tell you "where to look", they are not a substitute for a full check.

## How to use ratios

The best use of ratios is to make them part of your regular MIS/review, not to look at them only at audit time. Some practical tips:

- **Pick the 3–4 ratios most relevant to your type of society** — recovery% and NPA for a credit society; liquidity and cost-to-income for a trading one.
- **Work them out every quarter/year** and compare with the previous one.
- **Compare correctly** — with your own previous year, or with a society of similar type and size; not with a different kind of society.
- **If something looks unusual, find the cause**; do not jump to conclusions.

(See: [How to read financial reports](/blog/how-to-read-financial-reports) and [MIS reports](/blog/mis-reports-for-management).)

## Which ratios for which society

Not every society needs every ratio — some are more relevant depending on the type:

| Type of society | Most useful ratios |
| --- | --- |
| Loan/credit society | Recovery %, NPA %, credit-deposit ratio |
| Purchase-sale/consumer | Liquidity, cost-to-income, stock turnover |
| Common to all | Reserve ratio, return on capital |

Picking 3–5 ratios suited to your type of society and watching them regularly is better than ten scattered ratios. Fewer but right — that is the rule here too.

## Common mistakes

- **Looking at one year's ratio** → look at the three-year trend.
- **Wrong comparison** → comparing with a society of a different type/size misleads; compare with a similar one.
- **Panicking at an unusual figure** → first check the cause (it may be a one-time event).
- **Treating a benchmark as universal** → "good" changes with type/state.
- **Looking only at profit** → recovery, NPA and liquidity are just as important.
- **Treating a ratio as a decision** → ratios are pointers; decide after seeing the context.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are financial ratios?** — A division of two figures that gives a pointer to the society's health (liquidity, recovery, NPA etc.).
**Which ratio is most important?** — For a credit society, recovery % and NPA; for purchase-sale, liquidity and cost-to-income.
**What is a good current ratio?** — Generally ~1.5–2, but it varies by type; very high is not always good either.
**Is one year's ratio enough?** — No; look at the three-year trend — that tells the real story.
**Why is NPA important?** — A rising NPA directly affects both earnings and health; keeping it low is essential.
**What should ratios be compared with?** — With your own previous year, or with a society of similar type and size; not with a different kind of society.
**Does an unusual ratio mean something is wrong?** — Not necessarily; it is a signal to check, the cause may be a one-time event.
**What does return on capital tell?** — How much the members' capital is earning (surplus ÷ capital × 100).
**What is the biggest limit of ratios?** — They tell "what", not "why"; you have to check the cause and context yourself.
**How many ratios should we watch?** — Not all; pick the 3–5 most relevant to your type of society.
**When should ratios be worked out?** — As part of a regular (quarterly/yearly) MIS review, not just at audit time.

## Three things to remember
- 📊 Ratio = a division of two figures that gives meaning to a single figure.
- 📈 Look at the trend (3 years), not one year's figure; compare correctly with a similar society.
- 🎯 Recovery %, NPA, liquidity, cost-to-income, reserve — the five most useful (benchmark as per state/type).

## In the end
Financial ratios are the "thermometer" of a society's health — a few simple divisions that give the essence of a big report at a glance. Their best use is to pick a few key ratios for your type of society, work them out regularly, look at the three-year trend, and compare correctly. Remember, ratios point, they do not decide — if something looks unusual, find the cause, do not panic. With this understanding, ratios become a reliable, quick guide for the managing committee.

One last tip — do not look at ratios alone; read them together with [financial reports](/blog/how-to-read-financial-reports) and [MIS](/blog/mis-reports-for-management). Then ratios are not just numbers but the essence of the society's whole story — and every decision of the managing committee gets a strong foundation of figures.
