# Basic accounting assumptions: going concern and prudence

Every set of accounts is built on some unspoken assumptions. The two most important of these are **going concern** and **prudence**. Once you understand them, you know on what basis the accounts are made and what points auditors stress. This article explains them, and some other related basic concepts, in simple language with examples.

## Going Concern

This is the assumption that the society will keep running normally in the time ahead. Its accounts are made on this basis — it is not assumed that the society is about to close down or sell off its assets.

> 💡 Because of this assumption, assets are shown at their normal (not "closure") value.

> ⚠️ If there is serious doubt about the society continuing to run, it must be shown clearly — this is an important audit point.

### An example

Suppose the society built a godown for ₹5,00,000. Under the going concern assumption it is shown in the accounts at its use value (reducing depreciation every year), assuming the society will use it for years. If instead it were assumed that the society is closing tomorrow, the godown would have to be shown at the value it would "fetch if sold today" (perhaps much lower). This is the difference going concern makes — the value of a normal, running organisation, not the closure value. (See: [Depreciation](/blog/depreciation-explained).)

## Prudence

Prudence means being careful — not overstating income or assets, and **recording a possible loss or liability as soon as it can be estimated**.

> 💡 Rule of thumb: when uncertain, take the safe side — count income only when it is certain, but make a provision in advance for a possible loss.

### An example

Suppose the society gave ₹10,00,000 in loans to members, and some loans look like they are going bad. Prudence says — even if the money is not "gone" yet, make a **provision** for the possible loss as soon as it can be estimated. On the other hand, if the society expects some big income next year, do not count it now — record income only when it is actually realised. That is, losses early, income late (when certain) — this caution stops the society's picture from being overstated.

## What the two give together

| Assumption | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Going concern | Accounts at normal value; flag it if there is a sign of closure |
| Prudence | Losses recorded early, income when realised — a true, safe picture |

Together these two make the accounts **realistic and reliable** — for both members and the auditor.

> 🔍 A common misunderstanding — "prudence means showing everything lower". No; it means caution, not deliberately understating.

## Some more basic concepts

Besides going concern and prudence, some more assumptions are also at the foundation of every set of accounts — knowing them helps you understand reports in depth:

- **Accrual:** Income and expenses are recorded when they *happen*, not when the money comes in or goes out. For example, March's electricity bill will be counted as an expense in March itself, even if it is paid in April.
- **Consistency:** Keep a method once adopted (like the depreciation rate) the same every year, so that year-on-year comparison stays correct. If it must change, show it with the reason.
- **Matching:** Show a period's income together with the related expenses of the same period, so that the correct profit/loss comes out.
- **Business Entity:** The society's accounts are kept separate from the private accounts of its members/office-bearers — the society is a separate "entity".
- **Money Measurement:** Only what can be measured in money is recorded in the accounts.
- **Materiality:** Small, negligible amounts do not get as much attention as big, significant amounts.

Together these make a reliable, comparable accounting system. (See: [Accounting basics](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics) and [The accounting equation](/blog/accounting-equation-explained).)

## Accrual vs cash basis — an example

Understand the accrual assumption with an example. Suppose the society used ₹8,000 of electricity in March, but the bill was paid in April. There are two ways:

- **On cash basis:** The expense would be recorded in April (when the money went) — but then March's profit would look overstated.
- **On accrual basis:** The expense is recorded in March itself (when it was used), even though payment is in April — this gives March's correct profit.

Accounting generally follows the accrual basis, because it shows the correct profit/loss of each period. This is why adjustments like "outstanding expenses" and "income received in advance" are made at year-end — so that every item is recorded in the correct year. (See: [Year-end and FY lock](/blog/year-end-closing-and-fy-lock).)

## Matching — an example

The matching assumption says to show a period's income together with the related expenses of the same period. For example, if the society sold goods worth ₹50,000 this year, the purchase cost of those goods should also be counted in this year's expenses — not in the year the goods were bought. In the same way, depreciation is spread over the years an asset is used, not all in one year. Without matching, a year's profit/loss becomes misleading — so it is an important condition for a true picture.

## Consistency — why change carefully

Suppose the society kept charging depreciation at a fixed rate every year, but one year suddenly changes the rate — then that year's profit will look artificially higher or lower, and comparison with earlier years will be spoiled. The consistency assumption says to keep the adopted methods the same every year, so that comparison stays meaningful. If some change is really necessary (such as when rules change), show it clearly with the reason — not hidden.

## Why these assumptions matter in audit

Auditors pay special attention to these assumptions, because they are the foundation of the truth of the accounts. For example, the auditor checks whether there is any serious doubt about going concern (and if there is, whether it has been shown); whether a provision has been made for possible losses under prudence; whether methods were followed consistently; and whether the society's accounts were kept separate from the private accounts of its office-bearers. A society that follows these assumptions has a smooth audit, and members' trust in its reports grows. (See: [Audit preparation](/blog/audit-preparation-checklist).)

## Their special importance in a cooperative society

In a cooperative society these assumptions become even more important, because members' money and trust are directly involved here. The business-entity assumption makes sure that the society's money does not get mixed with any office-bearer's private money — which is basic to the transparency of cooperation. Prudence stops the society from overstating its health (which could mislead members or lead to a wrong distribution). And consistency and accrual make sure that every year's report is correct and comparable, so that members and the auditor can see the society's real progress. That is, these are not just technical rules — they are principles that protect members' trust.

## Common misunderstandings

- **"Prudence = showing everything lower"** → No; it is caution, not deliberately understating.
- **"Going concern = the society will never close"** → No; it is an assumption, which can change if circumstances change.
- **"Record income as soon as it can be estimated"** → No; count income only when it is realised (prudence).
- **"Private and society money together"** → No; under the business-entity assumption keep the two separate.
- **"The method can change every year"** → No; keep consistency, and if you change, show the reason.

## Materiality and money measurement — practical meaning

Two more concepts show up in everyday accounts. **Materiality** means that every small thing does not get as much attention as big, significant amounts — for example, treating a ₹50 stationery item as an "asset" for years and charging depreciation on it is pointless; treating it directly as an expense is practical. **Money measurement** means that only what can be measured in money is recorded in the accounts — so valuable but unmeasurable things like "the society's management experience" or "members' enthusiasm" do not come into the accounts, even though they matter a lot to the society. These concepts keep the accounts practical and objective.

## Assumption, policy and estimate — the difference

There is often confusion between these words. An **assumption** is the basic thinking on which all of accounting rests (like going concern). A **policy** is the specific method the society chooses (like which depreciation rate). And an **estimate** is where an exact figure is not available and a careful guess has to be made (like a provision for bad loans). All three are different, but work together — assumptions give the foundation, policies the method, and estimates help where there is uncertainty (this is where prudence guides).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is going concern?** — The assumption that the organisation will keep running; accounts are made on this basis and assets are shown at normal value.
**What does prudence mean?** — Caution — record possible losses early, but count income only when it is realised.
**Does going concern mean the society will never close?** — No; it is an assumption that can change if circumstances change.
**What is accrual?** — Income/expenses are recorded when they happen, not when the money comes in or goes out.
**Why is consistency necessary?** — So that accounts are made by the same method every year and year-on-year comparison stays correct.
**What is the business-entity assumption?** — Keeping the society's accounts separate from the private accounts of its members/office-bearers.
**Why do these assumptions matter in audit?** — They are the foundation of the truth of the accounts; auditors check that they are followed.
**What is materiality?** — More attention on big, significant amounts; handling very small amounts simply.
**What is the difference between assumption, policy and estimate?** — Assumption is the basic thinking, policy the chosen method, estimate a careful guess under uncertainty.
**Why are these special in a cooperative society?** — Members' money and trust are directly involved here; these assumptions protect transparency and a true picture.

## Three things to remember
- 🏛️ Going concern = the organisation will keep running; accounts on this basis.
- ⚖️ Prudence = losses early, income when realised.
- ✅ Accrual, consistency, matching, entity and materiality — together they make realistic, reliable accounts.

## In the end
These assumptions are the "thinking" behind the accounts. Going concern, prudence, accrual, consistency, matching and business entity — together they decide when and how income and expenses are recorded, at what value assets are shown, and how true and careful the picture is. Once you understand them, both reports and audit feel more meaningful — and you gain the confidence that the society's accounts stand on a solid, accepted foundation.
