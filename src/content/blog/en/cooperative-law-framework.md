# The framework of cooperative law: which law applies to a society

"Under which law does our society run?" — every secretary asks this at some point. The answer comes in a few layers. This article explains the **framework of cooperative law** in simple language — which law applies when, the role of the Registrar, the constitutional background, and how all of this connects to accounting.

## What a cooperative society is — in the eyes of the law

Legally, a cooperative society is a voluntary association of persons, formed for their mutual economic interest, run democratically (one member–one vote), and based on cooperative principles. This "cooperative" character is what sets it apart from an ordinary company — here it is not capital but members and service that are at the centre, and profit is not the aim but a means for the members' interest. (See: [Cooperative principles and values](/blog/cooperative-principles-and-values).)

Because of this distinct character, separate laws were made for cooperative societies — the state Cooperative Societies Acts instead of the Companies Act. The purpose of these laws is not just regulation, but also protecting cooperative values (democracy, equality, mutual help).

## Cooperation — mainly a state subject
In India, cooperation is **mainly a state subject**. So most societies run under their **state's Cooperative Societies Act** (such as the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act 1984, Maharashtra 1960) and its rules.

In the Constitution, cooperative societies are a subject in the State List, so every state has the power to make its own cooperative law. That is why there is no single uniform "cooperative law" for the whole country, but each state has its own Act. Only when a society spreads over more than one state does the Centre's Multi-State Act, 2002 apply. Understanding this basic division is the key to the whole framework.

## Three main layers
1. **State Cooperative Societies Act** — for societies working within one state (the most common).
2. **Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002** — when the society works in more than one state.
3. **The society's bye-laws** — the society's own rules within the scope of the Act. (See: [What bye-laws are](/blog/cooperative-society-byelaws).)

| Layer | When it applies |
| --- | --- |
| State Act | A society of one state |
| Multi-State Act 2002 | A society of several states |
| Bye-laws | Each society's own rules |

Understand the three together like this: the **state Act and Rules** are the "big law" that applies to every society in the whole state; the **bye-laws** are your society's own rules within that same scope. In any conflict the higher layer prevails — that is, the bye-laws cannot conflict with the Act, and the state Act cannot conflict with the Constitution/central framework. This clear order keeps the whole of cooperative governance orderly.

## Each state has its own Act

Since cooperation is a state subject, each state has its own Cooperative Act and Rules — so the details differ from state to state:

| State | Act (example) |
| --- | --- |
| Haryana | Haryana Cooperative Societies Act, 1984 + Rules 1989 |
| Maharashtra | Maharashtra Co-operative Societies Act, 1960 |
| Multi-state | Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002 |

That is why the minimum number of members, reserve rates, audit rules and forms can change from one state to another. So for any exact provision, always look at **your own state's** Act/Rules, not another state's. For detailed information on Haryana, see: [Haryana Act 1984 and Rules 1989: complete guide](/blog/haryana-cooperative-act-1984-rules-1989-guide).

## Main functions of the Registrar

The Registrar (RCS) is the pivot of the cooperative framework. Broadly, the Registrar's functions are:

- **Registration:** registering a new society and its bye-laws.
- **Oversight:** watching over the audit, annual returns and overall compliance.
- **Elections:** ensuring timely and fair elections, so that democratic control is maintained.
- **Dispute settlement:** resolving member–society and inter-society disputes.
- **Action:** on serious violations, steps like inquiry, supersession or winding up, which are taken only according to the procedure laid down in the law.

The aim of these powers is not to punish the society, but to keep it rule-abiding and in the members' interest.

## The role of the Registrar and the Centre
- The **Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RCS)** looks after registration, audit oversight and compliance.
- The **97th Constitutional Amendment (2011)** gave cooperatives **constitutional recognition** and stressed democratic, timely elections and professional management.
- The **Ministry of Cooperation (2021)** was formed to strengthen cooperation at the central level — through policy coordination, digitisation and initiatives like "Sahakar se Samriddhi". It does not take away the states' powers, but gives a dedicated institutional structure at the central level, which was earlier scattered across the Finance/Agriculture ministries.

> ⚠️ Note: the exact provisions, sections and applicability depend on the **state Act, Rules and judicial interpretation** — for any legal decision, confirm with your RCS/an expert.

### An important update on the 97th Amendment

In July 2021 the Supreme Court (Union of India vs Rajendra N. Shah) gave an important ruling: **Part IX-B**, added by the 97th Amendment, was held **struck down** to the extent it applies to **cooperative societies under the states** — because, cooperation being a state subject, it needed ratification by half the states (Article 368(2)), which did not happen. However, the same Part IX-B remained valid for **multi-state cooperative societies** (and societies of Union Territories). So the core spirit of the amendment lives on, but the actual regulation of state societies comes from their **state Act** alone. This ruling affirms the federal structure of the Constitution — where a central amendment on a state subject needs the consent of the states.

> 💡 Practical meaning: for an ordinary state-level society (PACS, dairy, consumer), the final word is that of its **state Cooperative Societies Act** — Part IX-B of the 97th Amendment does not apply to them directly.

## The Registrar's hierarchy: from RCS to ARCS

The "Registrar" is not one person but a whole administrative structure. The state Act gives the state government the power to appoint a Registrar and to delegate the Registrar's powers to subordinate officers — this creates a hierarchy:

| Level | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Registrar (RCS) | The highest cooperative officer at the state level, head of policy and oversight |
| Additional/Joint Registrar | Supervision and policy implementation for a special subject or at division level |
| Deputy Registrar (DRCS) | District/division-level oversight and hearing appeals, reviewing ARCS decisions |
| Assistant Registrar (ARCS) | The most practical level — registration, complaints and day-to-day matters, where most societies deal directly |

An ordinary member or secretary deals most with the **ARCS** level — applications for registering a new society and member–society disputes are often referred here first. So it is useful to know which ARCS office your society comes under, and what work to contact it for — this keeps everything smooth, from registration to annual compliance. If you are unhappy with a decision, the Act also provides a route of appeal to the higher level (DRCS/Joint Registrar).

> 💡 "RCS" (the state's Registrar) and "CRCS" (Central Registrar, for multi-state societies) are two separate bodies — an ordinary PACS/CM-PACS/dairy society comes under the state's RCS, not the central CRCS.

This distinction matters in practice: if someone tells you that your local society is under a "central law", be careful — unless it actually works in several states, it is under the RCS of its own **state**. Knowing the right Registrar matters because registration, audit and disputes are all linked to that office.

## What the law asks of a society

After registration, the law asks the society for some regular compliance — these differ a little from state to state, but the basic structure is the same:

- **Books of account:** keeping the prescribed books (cash book, ledger, member and share registers) correctly.
- **Annual audit:** by the cooperative department or an authorised auditor.
- **General body:** the annual general meeting and elections on time.
- **Annual returns:** the prescribed returns/reports to the Registrar.
- **Fund rules:** reserve fund and profit distribution as per rules, in the set order.
- **Member records:** keeping the member and share registers up to date.

Meeting these on time is what keeps the society "in compliance" and saves it from action by the Registrar.

A good habit is to make a "compliance calendar" at the very start of the year — when the general meeting is, when the audit is, when which return is due. This way no deadline is missed and there is no year-end rush. Most problems come from "forgetting" compliance, not from "being unable" to do it — so a simple calendar avoids half the trouble.

## The link with accounting
The law decides how much reserve fund to keep, how the audit is done, when returns are filed, and what the loan limit is (such as Section 32-type limits). So the society's accounts must be made in line with these rules. In other words, the law decides "what to do" and accounting "shows it in the record" — the two must be in step, otherwise an audit objection arises. (See: [Basics of cooperative accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

That is why software made for cooperatives is better than a general accounting tool — it is built with these legal needs (reserve fund, member register, audit formats) in mind from the start, so compliance stays easy on its own and the room for error shrinks.

## Frequently asked questions
**Under which law does a cooperative society run?** — Mainly under its state's Cooperative Societies Act and its bye-laws.
**Which law applies to a multi-state society?** — The Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002.
**What did the 97th Amendment do?** — It gave cooperatives constitutional recognition and stressed democratic working; in 2021 the SC struck down its part relating to state societies and upheld it for multi-state ones.
**Is cooperation a state subject or a central one?** — Mainly a state subject; central in multi-state matters.
**What is the difference between RCS and CRCS?** — RCS is the state's Registrar (state societies); CRCS is the Central Registrar (multi-state societies).
**Which level does an ordinary secretary deal with?** — Mostly the ARCS (Assistant Registrar) level — registration, complaints, day-to-day matters.
**What does the law ask of a society?** — Books, annual audit, general meeting/elections, annual returns and following the fund rules.
**Why was the Ministry of Cooperation (2021) formed?** — To strengthen and coordinate cooperation at the central level.
**Why does each state have a separate law?** — Cooperation is a State List subject, so each state makes its own Cooperative Act.

## Conclusion

The framework of cooperative law is not complex, just layered — at the top the state Act (or the 2002 Act for multi-state societies), then the Rules, then your bye-laws. The Registrar's structure (from RCS to ARCS) enforces it, and steps like the 97th Amendment and the Ministry of Cooperation give it constitutional and institutional strength. For a secretary, the practical point is just this: know your state Act and bye-laws, keep up regular compliance, and when in doubt confirm with your ARCS/an expert.
