# New Financial Year: The Right Start and the Annual Budget

April — a new financial year. Last year's accounts are closed, now a fresh start. This is the time when correct **opening balances** and a clear **budget** set your whole year in order. This article explains the right start to the new year, how to make a budget, and the rhythm for the whole year — all in simple language.

## The start of the year — linked to last year

The new year is not cut off from last year — it is its direct continuation. The cleaner last year's year-end closing (final accounts + FY lock), the easier the start of the new year. So preparation for the new year really begins at the end of last year — if the closing is correct and locked, the opening comes out correct automatically. (See: [year-end and FY lock](/blog/year-end-closing-and-fy-lock).) Think of this article as the next link in that series — where the year closes, the new one begins.

## Why a fresh start matters

The start of the year is the foundation on which the whole year's accounts stand. If the opening is correct and the budget is clear, the whole year stays in order; if the start itself is wrong, every month's report carries the same error forward. So the first few days of April — when the opening is set and the budget is made — are among the most important days of the year. A little care here, relief for the whole year.

## First task: correct opening balances
Last year's **closing balance** is this year's **opening balance** — it should come automatically, with no need to enter it again by hand. Make sure Dr = Cr is tallying. (Full details: [opening balance guide](/blog/opening-balances-new-society).)

> ⚠️ The most common slip of the new year — a wrong/incomplete opening. Get this right and the whole year is right.

### A simple way to check the opening

In the very first week of the new year, do this check: (a) do the balances of all asset and liability accounts match last year's closing; (b) is total Dr = total Cr; and (c) was last year's FY lock done properly. If all three are "yes", your foundation is solid. If you see a difference anywhere, fix it now — otherwise this difference will keep coming back in every report of the whole year. (Year-closing process: [year-end and FY lock](/blog/year-end-closing-and-fy-lock).)

## Second task: make the annual budget
A budget means **the estimated income and expenses for the whole year** — so you know where to spend how much and where income will come from.

| Item | Estimate (example) |
| --- | --- |
| Estimated income (interest, sales, fees) | ₹5,00,000 |
| Estimated expenses (salary, rent, others) | ₹3,50,000 |
| **Estimated savings** | **₹1,50,000** |

> 💡 A budget is not just a formality — comparing actual vs budget month by month keeps expenses under control.

### How to make a budget — steps

1. **Look at last year's figures:** Last year's actual income and expenses are the best basis for the budget.
2. **Estimate income:** Interest, sales, fees, commission etc. — be realistic, not inflated.
3. **Estimate expenses:** Salary, rent, electricity, repairs, interest payments — both known and likely expenses.
4. **See the difference:** Estimated income − expenses = estimated savings/deficit; if a deficit shows, plan for it before the year starts.
5. **Approval:** Get the budget approved by the managing committee/general body, so that it becomes a collective direction and not just one person's.

A good budget is realistic — neither so optimistic that it cannot be met, nor so loose that there is no discipline at all. It is a "financial map" for the whole year.

### A budget example — month by month

A budget is not just one figure for the year — it is more useful to split it into months. Suppose the year's estimated income is ₹5,00,000; split it into months according to season/activity (e.g., more in the crop season). Similarly, keep regular expenses like salary equal every month, but put expenses like repairs/events in the month they are expected. With such a monthly budget, at every month-end you can see whether that month's income and expenses stayed within budget — and correct in the middle of the year if needed, without waiting for year-end.

### What the opening entry looks like

Technically, at the start of the new year the balance of every account comes in as an "opening entry" — Dr balances of asset accounts and Cr balances of liability/capital accounts, which match last year's closing. For example: cash/bank/stock/member loans (assets) Dr; member deposits/share capital/reserve fund (liabilities/capital) Cr — and total Dr = total Cr. A digital system makes this entry automatically, but it is useful to know that this is what the "opening" really is — last year's picture, becoming the start of the new year.

## Third task: monitor budget vs actual

Making a budget and forgetting it is useless — the real benefit comes when **actual figures are compared with the budget** every month/quarter. Where expenses are going above budget, they can be handled in time; where income is low, the reason can be looked into. This "budget vs actual" analysis makes the budget a living management tool, not a paper exercise at the start of the year. (See: [budget vs actual analysis](/blog/budget-vs-actual-analysis).)

**Example:** Suppose in some month the electricity expense came out 40% above budget. In the monthly comparison this will show up at once, and the society can check the reason (wrong bill? more usage?) and correct it. Without this comparison, the extra expense will be found only at year-end — when nothing can be done. This is the difference between a "living budget" and a "paper budget".

## Fourth task: set the rhythm of the year
- Daily entries, month-end reconciliation, and timely tax compliance — build the habit from the first month itself.
- Adopt a compliance calendar with all the important dates of the year recorded. (See [quarterly compliance calendar](/blog/quarterly-compliance-calendar).)

Good habits built from the first month run through the whole year — and the year-end rush reduces on its own. On the other hand, if entries and reconciliation stay loose in the early months, that same pile becomes a big problem at year-end. So the best new-year gift you can give yourself is — discipline from day one.

Making a compliance calendar is the most practical step — with the important dates of the whole year (audit, annual return, general body meeting, tax filing) recorded in advance. With this no deadline is missed, and every task keeps happening on time. This small plan keeps the whole year stress-free. (See: [quarterly compliance calendar](/blog/quarterly-compliance-calendar).)

## Start of the new year — a checklist

- ☐ Checked opening = last year's closing (Dr = Cr).
- ☐ Last year's FY lock completed.
- ☐ Annual budget made and approved.
- ☐ Compliance calendar fixed (audit, return, general body meeting dates).
- ☐ Habit of daily entries and monthly reconciliation fixed.
- ☐ Last year's audit objections settled (if any are left).
- ☐ Member/share register up to date and matched with the books.
- ☐ First budget-vs-actual review fixed for the end of the first quarter.

Complete this checklist in the first week of the new year — and the foundation of the whole year stays solid.

## Why a budget — not just a formality

Many societies treat the budget as "paperwork", but in fact it is the most useful management tool of the whole year. A budget gives three things: **direction** (where to spend how much), **control** (a warning if expenses go outside the budget), and **accountability** (at year-end, comparing budget vs actual shows how right the plan was). Without a budget, the society runs "with eyes closed" — where the money is going is found only at year-end, by which time it is too late. So every society, small or large, should surely make a simple budget.

## Keep the budget realistic

A good budget is neither too optimistic nor too loose. If income is inflated and expenses underestimated, the budget will give false comfort and go wrong mid-year; if it is kept too loose, there will be no discipline at all. The best basis is — last year's actual figures, with known changes added (such as a salary increase, a new activity). A budget made this way stays reliable, and comparing it with actuals is meaningful.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering the opening by hand / it coming wrong** → let it come automatically from the last closing, and check Dr=Cr.
- **Not making a budget** → without a budget, expenses are uncontrolled and directionless; surely make a simple budget every year.
- **Forgetting the budget** → compare with actuals every month/quarter; the budget is a living tool, not paper.
- **Starting habits late** → daily entries, monthly reconciliation and compliance calendar from the first month itself.
- **Leaving past objections hanging** → settle them at the start of the new year itself, so that the next audit grade improves.

## The first quarter of the new year — what to watch

The first three months set the rhythm of the year. During this time: (a) make sure the opening is set correctly; (b) the budget is made and approved; (c) the habit of daily entries and monthly reconciliation has started; and (d) at the end of the first quarter, look at the first "budget vs actual" — to know whether the year is going in the right direction. If the first quarter was disciplined, the whole year usually stays in order; if there was slackness at the start, that pile keeps growing. So don't take the first quarter lightly.

## Three things to remember
- ⚖️ Make sure the opening is Dr=Cr and matches the last closing — then the whole year stays correct.
- 📊 Make a realistic budget and match it with actuals every month/quarter.
- 🗓️ Right habits and a compliance calendar from the first month — ease all year.

## In the end
The new financial year is a fresh start — with a correct opening, a clear budget, and discipline from day one, the whole year stays in order and stress-free. A little care in April, and relief for the whole year — this is the habit of a healthy society. Remember, every year's chain is linked: this year's correct closing → next year's correct opening → a good budget → a disciplined year → then a clean year-end. A society that repeats this cycle every year not only does well in audit but also earns the lasting trust of its members.
