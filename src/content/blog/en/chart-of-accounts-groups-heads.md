# Chart of Accounts: How to Set Up Groups and Heads the Right Way

The whole building of a society's accounts rests on one thing — the **Chart of Accounts**. If the groups and heads are right, every report comes out clean and correct. This article explains **how to build a chart of accounts** — what account groups and ledger heads are, the five main groups, a sample chart, coding and common mistakes — all with examples.

## What is a chart of accounts

In simple words, a chart of accounts is an orderly list of all the society's accounts — a full map of which accounts sit in which group. When you make a voucher, the accounts you pick in it (cash, rent, share capital etc.) all come from this chart. That is, it is the "basic vocabulary" of all accounting — the cleaner it is, the cleaner every report will be. (Basics: [Cooperative accounting basics](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

## Account Group
An account group is the category that keeps related accounts together — such as "Assets", "Liabilities", "Income", "Expenses", or a sub-group "Bank Accounts". Reports add up and show figures according to these groups.

> 💡 Groups give the Balance Sheet and Trial Balance their structure (assets vs liabilities etc.). Right groups = correct, readable reports.

## The five main groups — every account sits in one of them

Every ledger head falls into one of these five basic groups:

| Group | Example accounts | In which report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Assets** | Cash, bank, stock, member loans, furniture | Balance Sheet |
| **Liabilities** | Member deposits, loans taken, payables | Balance Sheet |
| **Capital** | Share capital, reserve fund | Balance Sheet |
| **Income** | Sales, interest income, commission | Income & Expenditure / Profit & Loss |
| **Expenses** | Salary, rent, electricity, depreciation | Income & Expenditure / Profit & Loss |

When every account is in the right group, the Balance Sheet and Profit & Loss come out right on their own. A wrong group (such as putting member deposits under "Income") spoils the whole report. (See: [Debit-credit and double entry](/blog/debit-credit-and-double-entry).)

## Ledger Head
A ledger head is the name under which a particular account is opened in the ledger — such as "Rent", "Salary", "Bank — SBI", or a member's name. All related entries are recorded under that head.

In a way, the group is the "cupboard" and the ledger heads are the "files" kept in it. For example, the "Expenses" cupboard has separate files (heads) such as "Rent", "Salary" and "Electricity". When you make a voucher, you pick the right file (head), and it goes into the right cupboard (group) on its own — so the report adds up correctly. (Picking a head in a voucher: [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).)

## Groups and Dr/Cr behaviour

Every group has a natural "behaviour" — that is, whether it is Dr or Cr when it increases:

- **Assets and expenses** → Dr when they increase, Cr when they decrease.
- **Liabilities, capital and income** → Cr when they increase, Dr when they decrease.

So when you put an account in the right group, the system knows on its own in which direction its balance should show and which report it will go to. Choosing the wrong group means the wrong direction — and that is where the report goes wrong. (In detail: [Debit-credit and double entry](/blog/debit-credit-and-double-entry).)

## The chart is the root of the whole report chain

Everything from a voucher to the final accounts rests on this chart:

1. In a **voucher** you pick a head from the chart.
2. In the **ledger** each head gets its own page.
3. The **Trial Balance** adds up the balances of all heads group-wise.
4. The **Balance Sheet and Profit & Loss** are made according to these groups.

This means — if even one account in the chart is in the wrong group, that mistake will show throughout the chain. That is why building the right chart at the start is the most important investment. (See: [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained).)

## Sub-groups — an even cleaner structure

Splitting large groups into sub-groups makes reports even cleaner. For example:

- **Assets → Current assets** (cash, bank, stock) and **Fixed assets** (building, furniture, vehicles).
- **Bank → Bank-SBI, Bank-PNB** (each bank account a separate head).
- **Expenses → Administrative expenses** (salary, rent, electricity) and **Financial expenses** (interest paid).

Make only as many sub-groups as you need — enough that the report is easy to read, but not so many that counting and matching get tangled.

## A sample chart — a glimpse

A small society's chart may look something like this:

| Group | Ledger heads (examples) |
| --- | --- |
| Assets | Cash, Bank-SBI, stock, member loans, furniture, building |
| Liabilities | Member deposits, salary payable, suppliers |
| Capital | Share capital, reserve fund |
| Income | Fertilizer sales, seed sales, consumer goods sales, interest income |
| Expenses | Salary, rent, electricity, depreciation |

Note — sales are kept separate by category (fertilizer, consumer goods); this lets the Trading Account show a separate profit for each category. (See: [How to read reports](/blog/how-to-read-financial-reports).)

## Account coding (numbering)

Many systems give each account a code (such as assets 1xxx, liabilities 2xxx, income 4xxx, expenses 5xxx). Coding keeps accounts in order and a new account gets added in the right place. In a small society this is not necessary, but in a large society it keeps the chart organised. Whatever coding you choose, keep it consistent.

## Category-wise sales/purchase heads

If the society sells many kinds of goods, keep a separate sales head (and purchase head) for each category — such as "Fertilizer sales", "Seed sales", "Consumer goods sales". Then the report shows how much profit or loss each category has, and management can take the right decisions. Putting everything in a single "Sales" head loses this insight.

## Rules for a good chart
| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Keep standard, clear names | Arbitrary/vague names |
| Put under the right group | Putting in the wrong group |
| Reuse existing heads | A new head for every entry |

> ⚠️ Too many small heads tangle the report. Group them sensibly.

A practical balance is this: an expense/income that needs to be seen separately gets its own head; and small, similar ones are gathered into one logical head. For example "tea and water", "photocopy", "minor repairs" — instead of making three separate heads for these, one head like "Miscellaneous office expenses" is enough, provided it really is small. But don't misuse "Miscellaneous" — a large or regular expense should always have its own clear head.

## Take help from standard names
To keep account names uniform, an **account-name glossary** and an **expense dictionary** are very useful — they make the question "which expense goes under which head" easy, and keep reports comparable.

A big advantage of standard names is also that when office-bearers or the accountant change, the new person easily understands which expense goes where — because the names are already clear and uniform. On the other hand, if everyone keeps a name of their own choice ("bhada", "kiraya", "rent" — three names for the same expense), the report gets scattered and year-on-year comparison becomes impossible. So fix a standard once and follow it across the whole society.

## A large vs small society's chart

A small society can manage with a simple chart (a few dozen heads) — too many sub-groups only add confusion. A large society (many branches, many kinds of goods) needs a detailed chart and coding, so that the accounts of each activity show separately. The principle for both is the same — "as much detail as is useful, no more". Keep a balance according to your society's size and needs.

## Common mistakes

- **Account in the wrong group** → such as putting member deposits in income; this spoils both the Balance Sheet and the profit.
- **A new head for every entry** → this scatters the report across hundreds of small heads; always reuse existing heads.
- **Vague/arbitrary names** → putting everything under names like "Miscellaneous" or "Other" makes identification hard later.
- **Duplicate heads** → two separate heads like "kiraya" and "bhada" for the same expense; always keep just one.
- **Not splitting categories** → putting all sales in a single head makes category-wise profit analysis impossible.

## How to start a good chart

1. First fix the five basic groups (Assets/Liabilities/Capital/Income/Expenses).
2. In each group, list your society's real and necessary accounts.
3. Choose a standard, clear name for each account; make logical sub-groups if needed.
4. Once it is built, add a new head only when it is really new and necessary.
5. Review the chart from time to time (such as at year-end) — remove or merge unused or duplicate heads, but touch heads with active balances only with care.

Once a good chart is built, the same one serves for years — just keep it clean. In the new year the old chart simply carries on (only balances are carried forward), so there is no need to build a new chart every year — this continuity is what makes year-on-year comparison possible.

## Frequently asked questions
**Difference between an account group and a ledger head?** — A group is a category of many accounts; a head is the name of one account.
**Can I create heads with my own names?** — You can, but keeping standard names is better — the report stays clean.
**When should I create a new account?** — When it is really new; otherwise use an existing head.
**Which are the five main groups?** — Assets, Liabilities, Capital, Income and Expenses.
**Is account coding necessary?** — Not in a small society, but in a large society it keeps the chart organised.
**Why separate heads for sales?** — So that each category (fertilizer, seed etc.) shows its own profit and the right decisions can be taken.
**What happens if I choose the wrong group?** — Both the Balance Sheet and Profit & Loss come out wrong; so choose groups carefully.
**When should the chart be changed?** — As little as possible; on review, remove or merge unused/duplicate heads, but don't change it again and again.
**When should I create sub-groups?** — When a group has so many accounts that the report is hard to read; then make logical sub-groups.
**Why are standard names necessary?** — So that even when office-bearers change, everyone understands which expense goes where, and reports stay comparable.
**Does every bank account need a separate head?** — Yes; keep a separate head for each bank account, so the reconciliation (BRS) of each stays clean.

With a clean chart of accounts everything else becomes easy — entries, reports and audit. Fix the five groups, keep every account in the right group, give standard names, and create a new head only after careful thought. A little care at the start, relief all year — because every voucher, every report and every audit rests on this chart.
