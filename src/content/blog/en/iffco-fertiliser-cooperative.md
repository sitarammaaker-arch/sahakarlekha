# What is IFFCO: the farmers' own fertiliser cooperative

Reading "IFFCO" on a fertiliser bag, many farmers ask — is this a company or something else? In fact IFFCO is the farmers' **own cooperative institution** — and one of the largest cooperative institutions in the world. This article explains in simple language its introduction, history, working, figures and benefit to farmers, and also what a small society can learn from it.

## What IFFCO is

IFFCO stands for **Indian Farmers Fertiliser Cooperative Limited**. It was set up in **1967**. It is a **multi-state cooperative society**, whose members are thousands of cooperative societies across the country. Its head office is in **New Delhi**.

## Why IFFCO was created — background

In the decades after Independence, India depended to a large extent on private companies and imports for fertiliser, and farmers often got costly, uncertain and sometimes adulterated fertiliser. In the Green Revolution era there was a huge need for reliable, fairly priced fertiliser for improved farming. IFFCO was born out of this need — with the idea that **farmers themselves, in cooperative form, should make and distribute their own fertiliser**, so that the profit returns to farmers instead of private investors. This idea of "the farmer's own fertiliser company" is the foundation of IFFCO. (Its role in the Green Revolution: [Cooperatives in the Green and White Revolutions](/blog/cooperatives-in-green-and-white-revolution).)

## What it does

- **Fertiliser production:** Makes fertilisers such as urea, DAP and NPK in its large plants.
- **Distribution:** Delivers fertiliser straight to farmers through a vast network of cooperative societies.
- **Farmers' interest:** Profit goes towards the welfare of member societies/farmers instead of investors.

> 💡 The key point: IFFCO's owners are not outside investors but **cooperative societies** — this is what sets it apart from an ordinary company. (Understand the difference: [Cooperative society vs company](/blog/cooperative-vs-company-difference).)

## What being a cooperative really means

The key to understanding IFFCO is its ownership structure. The owners of an ordinary fertiliser company are shareholders/investors, and its aim is to earn profit for them. IFFCO's owners are — thousands of cooperative societies across the country, which in turn belong to crores of farmers. This means IFFCO's profit does not go into the pocket of some distant investor, but returns towards the welfare of member societies and farmers. Because it runs on the cooperative principle of "one member, one vote", farmers' interest stays at the centre even in its big decisions. This is what makes it fundamentally different from a private company.

## What being a "multi-state cooperative" means

IFFCO is a **Multi-State Cooperative Society** — that is, its area of operation is not limited to one state but spreads across the whole country, so it comes under the central Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act (instead of a state cooperative Act). Its members are not individuals but **cooperative societies** — that is, IFFCO is a "society of societies", a big example of cooperative federalism. This structure gives it the capacity to work at the national level, set up large plants and reach crores of farmers. (See: [The three-tier cooperative structure](/blog/cooperative-society-tiers-primary-central-state-apex).)

## Innovation and expansion

IFFCO has not stayed limited to traditional fertiliser — over time it has also moved towards new products and technologies, such as innovations like liquid/nano fertilisers, which aim at more effect with smaller quantities and environment-friendly farming. It has also widened its access to raw materials and production through international joint ventures (Oman, Jordan, Senegal and others). This shows that a cooperative institution is also no less than private companies in innovation and global expansion — provided its management is far-sighted. (Details of specific innovative products change over time — get the latest information from IFFCO.)

## How farmers/societies connect with IFFCO

An ordinary farmer does not become a member of IFFCO directly — he connects through his **village cooperative society (PACS)**, which may be a member of IFFCO and sells its fertiliser. In this way the farmer gets reliable fertiliser at his own society, and indirectly becomes part of IFFCO's cooperative family. For a PACS/farm-input society, becoming an IFFCO dealer/distributor is also a good source of income — provided it keeps clean accounts of stock and sales.

## What this means for your society

If your PACS or farm-input society sells IFFCO fertiliser, this is the ordinary accounting of **purchase, sale and stock** — stock goes up on purchase of fertiliser, goes down on sale, and is recorded in the right account. (See: [Inventory and stock management](/blog/inventory-and-stock-management).)

In practice a society selling fertiliser should take care of a few things: record every purchase and sale at once, keep correct accounts of stock (bags/tonnes), keep track of batch/lot and expiry, and in the case of subsidised fertiliser follow government guidelines (such as POS/iFMS entry, where applicable). Clean stock accounts not only make the audit easier, they also ensure that farmers get fertiliser at the right time, and reduce the scope for stock mismatches or losses.

## Lessons for a small society

The IFFCO story has concrete lessons for every small PACS/CM-PACS: first, **keep members' interest at the centre** — when an institution's aim is the welfare of its members (not the profit of an outsider), members stay with it. Second, **quality and trust are what build a brand** — IFFCO's name works because farmers trust its fertiliser. Third, **transparency and good management** are what make a cooperative big. A village society may not become as big as IFFCO, but by running on these same principles it can become strong, trusted and lasting in its own area.

## What farmers gain

The direct benefit of being a cooperative reaches the farmer — reliable fertiliser, fair prices, and timely supply. Since the owners are cooperative societies themselves, the profit returns towards the welfare of member societies and farmers instead of being shared among outside investors. This "of the members, by the members" cooperative model is what makes IFFCO farmer-friendly and lasting, and sets it fundamentally apart from an ordinary private company.

## IFFCO's main products and services

IFFCO's range extends beyond the main fertilisers too:

- **Main fertilisers:** Urea, DAP (Di-Ammonium Phosphate), NPK complex fertilisers.
- **New/liquid fertilisers:** Over time, innovations like nano/liquid fertilisers, aimed at more effect with smaller quantities.
- **Farm services:** Support services such as soil testing, farmer awareness and advice (depending on area and time).
- **Allied businesses:** Some units of the group have also been active in insurance, logistics, chemicals and other agriculture-related fields.

This variety makes IFFCO not just a fertiliser maker but a broad cooperative group that meets many of the farmer's needs. (Exactly which product/service is available where changes over time — see the official source.)

## Why IFFCO is an inspiration

IFFCO is not just a fertiliser maker — it is living proof that farmers, by organising in cooperative form, can build one of the largest institutions in the world. It shows that the cooperative model can succeed from the small level (the village PACS) to the huge, international level — provided it stands on members' interest, transparency and good management. There is a lesson in this for a small village society too: with the right cooperative principles, any institution can grow big.

## Impact on rural India

IFFCO's impact is not limited to the fertiliser bag. By getting reliable, fairly priced fertiliser to every farmer, it played a big role in bringing the Green Revolution to the ground, which moved the country towards self-reliance in foodgrains. Besides this, by keeping its own vast network of cooperative societies active and strong, it also supported the rural cooperative structure — because a PACS that sells IFFCO fertiliser gets both income and member service. That is, IFFCO has been useful to the farmer, the society and the country — all three at once. This wide impact makes it not just a fertiliser maker but a pillar of the rural economy.

**Who owns IFFCO?** — Thousands of cooperative societies across the country; no outside investor.
**When was IFFCO set up?** — In 1967 (3 November 1967).
**What does IFFCO make?** — Mainly urea, DAP and other fertilisers (NPK and so on).
**Why was IFFCO created?** — So that farmers themselves, in cooperative form, could make and distribute reliable, fairly priced fertiliser, and the profit returns to farmers.

**What does this mean for a society selling fertiliser?** — Ordinary purchase-sale-stock accounting; correct stock and category-wise accounts are essential.

**Does a farmer become a member of IFFCO directly?** — No; he connects through his village society (PACS), which may be a member/distributor of IFFCO.

**What should a small society learn from IFFCO?** — Members' interest at the centre, trust through quality, and transparent management — these are what make any cooperative strong.

## In the end

IFFCO is one of the biggest success stories of cooperation — the farmers' own fertiliser institution, which today is counted among the world's leading cooperative institutions. Its core message is simple: when farmers organise and meet their own need (here, fertiliser) themselves, they not only get reliable, fairly priced supply, but the profit also returns to them. From a small PACS to IFFCO — this same principle of cooperation works at every level. And for a society selling fertiliser, the practical point is this — keep clean stock accounts, so that the farmer gets the right fertiliser at the right time.

In short, IFFCO is a living example of the strength of cooperation — and for every small society, an assurance that by following the right principles a cooperative institution can reach any height.
