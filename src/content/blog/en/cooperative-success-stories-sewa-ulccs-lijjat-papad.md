The cooperative model is not limited to farmers or milk producers — women and workers from the household and unorganised sectors can also organise and build large, lasting economic models. Here are three such cooperative success stories from India — **SEWA, ULCCS and Lijjat Papad** — that prove a small start, built on the right principles, can grow into a large and lasting institution.

## SEWA — a cooperative bank and trade union of unorganised women

On 12 April 1972, the **Self Employed Women's Association (SEWA)** was founded in Ahmedabad under the leadership of Ela Bhatt — it grew out of the women's wing of the Textile Labour Association (TLA), formed in 1920. The organisation took root in meetings with street vendors, head-load carriers and home-based women workers who could not even get a fair wage. Ela Bhatt was honoured with the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 1977.

> 💡 What makes SEWA special is that it works as **both a trade union and a cooperative network** at the same time — its members come from more than 125 different trades. **SEWA Bank**, set up in 1974 as a cooperative bank for illiterate and poor women, is counted worldwide among the pioneering initiatives of the microfinance movement.

SEWA's work had an impact not only on incomes but on policy too — in 1996 the International Labour Organization (ILO) recognised basic labour standards for home-based women workers, where SEWA's campaign played a key role, and in 2014 India passed the Street Vendors Act. Today SEWA is counted among the country's largest central trade unions for women workers in the unorganised sector, with a presence that reaches beyond India to South Asia, South Africa and Latin America.

## ULCCS — Kerala's 100-year-old labour cooperative society

On 13 February 1925, inspired by the spiritual teacher Vagbhatananda, unemployed workers of Uralungal village in Kerala's Kozhikode district came together to form a "mutual help group" — the **Uralungal Labour Contract Co-operative Society (ULCCS)**. At first it took only small contracts, but its policy of putting honesty and quality above profit slowly carried it to large projects.

> 📘 ULCCS has so far completed more than 4,000 projects, with a total value estimated at over 1,000 crore rupees — including major works such as the Kozhikode Sarovaram project, the renovation of Kappad beach, bridge construction, a government engineering college and national highway construction. It also runs a training institute for the neurodivergent (special-needs) community.

The ULCCS story proves that even without large starting capital or technical expertise, a labour cooperative society can, through discipline and honest work, build the capacity to handle big infrastructure projects over the decades — here profit is used to grow the institution's capacity and for community welfare, rather than for personal gain.

## Lijjat Papad — from 80 rupees to 1,600 crore

In 1959, seven Gujarati women in the Girgaon area of Mumbai together started rolling papads — the starting capital was just ₹80. Among them, the name of Padma Shri Jaswantiben Jamnadas Popat is especially remembered.

> ⚠️ The biggest strength of **Shri Mahila Griha Udyog Lijjat Papad** is its organisational structure — there is no traditional boss-employee hierarchy here. Every woman member is treated as a co-owner of the institution and an equal sharer in the profit. Most production happens at home, while buying raw material, quality checks and distribution are handled at the organisation level.

Today more than 45,000 women are linked with the institution, making crores of papads every day, and its products are exported to more than 15 countries. Equal profit-sharing and collective ownership are seen as the biggest reasons for its success — this model shows that cooperative principles work just as well for women in the household and unorganised sector as for agriculture or big industry.

## Why these stories matter

The value of these stories is not limited to inspiration. They prove three concrete things. First, **the cooperative model is universal** — it does not depend on any one sector (such as agriculture/dairy); wherever small people want to organise and work together, it works. Second, **it is lasting** — ULCCS has run for 100 years, Lijjat for 65+ years, SEWA for 50+ years; a properly run cooperative enterprise lasts for generations. Third, **it is inclusive** — it gave economic power to people (unorganised women, daily-wage workers) whom the market often leaves behind. These three points keep cooperation relevant today, for every new initiative.

## Cooperative principles shared by all three

Although they come from different sectors (microfinance, construction, food), all three institutions run on the same basic cooperative principles that lie at the root of any successful society:

- **Collective ownership:** the members are the owners — whether it is a Lijjat woman rolling papads or a ULCCS worker.
- **Democratic control:** decisions are not imposed from above, but made with members' participation.
- **Equal/fair sharing of profit:** profit is shared among the members, not held in a few hands.
- **Reinvestment of profit:** a large part of earnings goes into strengthening the institution and community, not into personal gain.
- **Self-help and solidarity:** the spirit of "weak alone, strong together".

These principles give all three the strength to last and keep growing for decades. (See: [Cooperative principles](/blog/cooperative-principles-and-values).)

## The foundation of trust — transparent accounts

There is one more thing these three institutions share, which rarely makes the headlines but matters most — transparent, reliable accounts. When the money, profit-sharing and ownership of thousands or lakhs of members are held in common, every member needs to trust that their share is recorded correctly and will be paid on time. SEWA Bank's microfinance, Lijjat's equal profit-sharing, or ULCCS's project accounts — none of the three would have been possible at scale without transparent accounting. The same lesson holds for every small society: trust is built not by headlines, but by clean accounts.

## Beyond economics — the social impact

The impact of these institutions has not been limited to income. SEWA gave unorganised women workers identity, a voice and policy rights; Lijjat gave home-based women economic independence and confidence; and ULCCS gave daily-wage workers dignified, steady employment and community welfare. In other words, cooperation gave these people not just money but **dignity, security and collective strength** — which working alone never gives. This is the deepest, often overlooked contribution of cooperation — it brings social empowerment along with economic progress.

## The lesson from all three stories

The three institutions come from very different sectors (microfinance, construction contracts, food production), but they share one common thread — a small, resource-limited start, equal profit-sharing, and putting profit into the long-term welfare of the institution and its members rather than personal gain. This principle is just as relevant to any small PACS or CMS as it was to these large institutions.

Notice that all three were patient — none succeeded overnight. ULCCS took decades to reach large projects; Lijjat took many years to go from ₹80 to crores; SEWA won policy changes slowly. This patience is a lesson too — cooperative success is a long, steady journey, built up by transparent, disciplined work every year.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is SEWA just a bank, or more than that?** — More than that; it is both a trade union and a cooperative network at the same time, and SEWA Bank is one of its units.
**What kind of work does ULCCS do?** — Mainly construction and infrastructure contracts — roads, bridges, buildings — in the public works department, highway and irrigation sectors.
**Do Lijjat Papad members get a salary or a share in profit?** — Neither in the usual sense; every member is treated as a co-owner and an equal sharer in the profit, and there is no traditional salary structure.
**Is this model only for women?** — No; ULCCS is mainly a labour cooperative model, while SEWA and Lijjat Papad are women-centred — the principle shared by all three is collective ownership and equal profit-sharing.
**When did these three start?** — ULCCS in 1925 (Kerala), Lijjat in 1959 (Mumbai), SEWA in 1972 (Ahmedabad).

## Three things to remember

- 🤝 All three institutions grew big from a small, resource-limited start — discipline, honesty and collective ownership made more difference than capital.
- ⚖️ Equal profit-sharing and reinvesting profit into building the institution — this is the principle shared by all three stories.
- 🌍 The cooperative model is not limited to agriculture — it reaches microfinance, construction contracts and home-based production, and brings social as well as economic empowerment.

## What your society can learn from them

These big stories are inspiring, but they also hold some concrete, practical lessons for every small society/SHG:

- **Don't fear a small start** — Lijjat started with ₹80; the real capital is members' trust and discipline.
- **Share profit and reinvest** — don't distribute all of it; put some into strengthening the institution (reserves/capacity).
- **Keep quality and honesty above profit** — like ULCCS; reputation is long-term capital.
- **Treat every member as a stakeholder** — a sense of ownership keeps members engaged.
- **Keep transparent accounts** — this is the shared, invisible foundation of all of them.

So the recipe for success is not big capital or luck — it is the right principles, discipline and transparency, which any village society can adopt.

## In the end

SEWA, ULCCS and Lijjat Papad — three very different stories, but witnesses to one truth: **when people organise, share benefits equally, and hold on to honesty and transparency, even a small start can become a large, lasting institution.** This principle is not the privilege of big institutions — it is just as open to every village PACS, every women's SHG, every small society. All it needs is the right thinking, discipline, and clean accounts.
