Forming a CM-PACS and starting business is only half the work. The law also binds every society to keep the accounts of its money in a fixed, transparent way, to get them audited every year, and to compute tax correctly — whether the society is small or big. For a CM-PACS this is even more important, because it runs many kinds of business at the same time, and the accounting and tax rules for each activity are different.

## Three responsibilities — accounts, audit, tax

Understand the financial responsibilities of a CM-PACS in three parts, because all three are separate but linked:

- **Accounts:** recording day-to-day transactions in a fixed format and preparing financial statements.
- **Audit:** an independent check of those accounts, in the manner fixed by the Registrar.
- **Tax:** claiming the 80P deduction on correctly classified income, and tax on the rest.

The root of all three is the same — **clean, activity-wise accounts**. If this foundation is strong, both audit and tax become smooth; if it is weak, trouble comes in all three places. That is why this guide keeps coming back to activity-wise accounts.

## Small or big — the rules apply to all

A common misunderstanding is that a small CM-PACS gets relaxation in the accounting/audit rules. In fact, these requirements of the cooperative Act/Rules are linked not to size but to the legal status of the body — that is, even a small society has to follow the fixed format, time limits and audit. The difference is only of scale, not of requirement. So setting up the right system from the start — even if the society is small — makes every later check easier.

## How the books of account must be kept

Under **Rule 78** of the Haryana Cooperative Societies Rules 1989, every cooperative society has to keep its books of account and records in the same form and manner that the Registrar fixes by issuing orders from time to time — that is, a CM-PACS cannot adopt any accounting format of its own choice. **Rule 79** sets a clear time limit: financial statements such as the Balance Sheet, Profit & Loss Account and Trading Account have to be prepared **within 3 months of the end of the cooperative year**, in the format fixed by the Registrar.

## Who does the audit

Under **Rule 77**, the society's accounts are audited in the manner fixed by the Registrar — a CM-PACS cannot appoint an auditor of its own choice; this is decided within the scope of the Cooperation Department. Under **Section 25(3)(b)**, discussion of the audit report and the annual report in the annual general meeting (AGM) of every society is mandatory — that is, the audit is not just a paper formality but accountability placed before the members. Under **Rule 80**, every society also has to pay the government a fee for its audit every year — this fee is not fixed directly in the Act but by the Registrar's scale, details of which should be taken from the ARCS office.

> 💡 Since a CM-PACS can run many activities at the same time, such as fertiliser-seed, dairy, LPG dealership and fair price shop, **recording the income and expenses of each activity separately** is needed not only to pass the audit but also for a correct tax deduction claim — as will become clear in Section 80P below. The part of the year's profit that goes to the reserve fund should also be clearly visible (see: [Profit distribution and reserve fund](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves)).

## Transparency — accountability to members

Note that accounts and audit are not only for the government — they are also accountability to members. The audit report and annual report being placed before members in the AGM under Section 25(3)(b) shows exactly this — the society's money belongs to the members, and they have the right to know where and how it was used. So clean accounts are not just a compliance formality but the foundation of members' trust. Members of a CM-PACS that keeps up this transparency stay more active and trusting.

## Tax deduction: which activities Section 80P applies to

**Section 80P** of the Income Tax Act 1961 gives cooperative societies a tax deduction on certain specific kinds of income — but this deduction does not apply automatically to the whole income; it depends on which activity the income came from.

| Source of income | Deduction |
| --- | --- |
| Credit/banking facilities to members, cottage industry, marketing of members' produce, supply of agricultural inputs, processing without power, fisheries [Section 80P(2)(a)] | **100% deduction** |
| Interest/dividend from investment in another cooperative society [Section 80P(2)(d)] | **100% deduction** |
| Income from godown/warehouse rent [Section 80P(2)(e)] | **100% deduction** |
| Other business — fair price shop, LPG/petrol dealership, taxi, construction, general retail [Section 80P(2)(c)] | Up to ₹1 lakh for a consumer cooperative society, in other cases **only up to ₹50,000** — above this, taxable at the normal rate |

Looking at the scope of a CM-PACS, activities such as fertiliser-seed supply, custom hiring centre, grading-packaging-marketing of produce, and dairy collection and processing come close to the 80P(2)(a) list — that is, a full deduction may be claimed on them, provided they are linked to members. But if activities like an LPG dealership or taxi service earn a profit of more than ₹50,000, the remaining part will come within tax.

> ⚠️ Under **Section 80P(4)**, this whole section does not apply to a "cooperative bank" (except a PACS or a cooperative agriculture and rural development bank) — this exception applies from 1 April 2007. We could not find the official text of the CM-PACS Model Bye-laws in this check, so confirm from your society's registered bye-laws whether they include banking business. For a CM-PACS that does not provide deposit/banking services, this exception should generally not apply — but if a particular CM-PACS has really started deposit/banking services, the position will have to be checked separately. Keep in mind — this exception is linked to PACS (Primary Agriculture Credit Society), which is a completely different body from a CM-PACS.

## An example — activity-wise 80P

Suppose a CM-PACS runs three activities — fertiliser-seed supply (to members), godown rent, and an LPG dealership. If its accounts are kept activity-wise, it can show clearly — an 80P deduction claim on the fertiliser-seed and godown income (as member-related/warehouse income), and tax on the part of the LPG income above the limit. But if all the accounts were in one heap, it could neither claim the right deduction nor satisfy the auditor. This is why activity-wise accounts are not just a good habit but a direct need for tax saving and compliance. (This is only an example to explain the principle — confirm the exact deduction eligibility with a CA.)

## What it means in practice — keeping accounts separately is necessary

Since a CM-PACS can run many kinds of business at the same time, for correct tax computation the income of each activity must be recorded separately — the income of farm-related activities, the income of general business (LPG, taxi), and the income from investments/godowns must be shown not mixed together but in separate accounts; only then can the correct deduction be claimed.

> ⚠️ This information is for general understanding — before actual tax filing, it is necessary to take advice on your specific case from a chartered accountant or tax adviser, because every society's activities may be different.

## Activity-wise accounts — how in practice

Since both a correct 80P claim and passing the audit rest on activity-wise accounts, here is how to keep them in practice:

- Create **separate income and expense accounts** for each activity (fertiliser-seed, dairy, LPG, fair price, godown etc.).
- Put every sale/purchase in the account of that same activity — do not mix them up.
- Divide shared expenses (such as electricity, salary) among the activities on a fair basis.
- At year-end, work out **activity-wise profit and loss** — this is what shows which activity is eligible for the 80P deduction.

A good cooperative software makes this activity-wise split on its own, which not only makes the audit easy but also gives the CA clean figures for correct tax computation. (See: [Basics of cooperative accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

## What is usually checked in an audit

A cooperative audit usually looks at — whether the accounts are in the format fixed by the Registrar; whether every transaction has a voucher and approval; whether bank reconciliation was done; whether the reserve fund is correctly allocated; whether member records (shares/deposits/loans) are complete; and whether last year's closing matches this year's opening. So keeping clean accounts all year round is the best audit preparation — then the audit is not a crisis, just a confirmation. (See: [Audit preparation](/blog/audit-preparation-checklist).)

## Record-keeping — practical tips

- Keep the **voucher and proof** of every transaction safely.
- Entries **daily**, bank reconciliation **every month** — the year-end rush goes down.
- Follow the Registrar's **fixed format** (Rule 78) — not a format of your own choice.
- Remember the **time limit** — financial statements within 3 months (Rule 79).
- Do take **a CA's advice** before tax filing.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a CM-PACS choose its own auditor?** — No, the Registrar fixes the manner of audit (Rule 77).
**By when must the Balance Sheet be prepared?** — Within 3 months of the end of the cooperative year (Rule 79).
**Does a CM-PACS get the 80P deduction on its whole income?** — No; 100% on member-related activities, but only up to ₹50,000 on general business (LPG, taxi etc.).
**Does the banking exception of 80P(4) apply to a CM-PACS?** — Generally no, unless it is really providing deposit/banking services.
**Why are activity-wise accounts necessary?** — Because both a correct 80P deduction claim and passing the audit depend on them — the income of each activity should show in a separate account.
**What is checked in an audit?** — Fixed format, vouchers/approval, bank reconciliation, reserve fund, member records and opening-closing match.
**What to do before tax filing?** — Advice from a CA/tax adviser on your specific case — every society's activities may be different.
**Does a small CM-PACS get relaxation in audit rules?** — No; these requirements are linked not to size but to the legal status of the body — the difference is only of scale, not of requirement.
**How much is the audit fee?** — Not fixed directly in the Act (Rule 80); by the Registrar's scale — confirm with the ARCS office.
**In which format should accounts be kept?** — In the format fixed by the Registrar (Rule 78) — not a format of your own choice; financial statements within 3 months (Rule 79).
**Is the audit only for the government?** — No; under Section 25(3)(b) the audit report is placed before members in the AGM — it is also accountability to members.
**What is the common root of the three responsibilities?** — Clean, activity-wise accounts; this foundation makes accounts, audit and tax — all three — smooth.
**Does the 80P(4) banking exception apply to a CM-PACS?** — Generally no, if the society does not provide deposit/banking services (check your registered bye-laws); but if it really provides deposit/banking services, check the position separately (this exception is linked to PACS, which is separate from CM-PACS).

## In the end

The accounts and tax of a CM-PACS seem complex because it runs many businesses at the same time — but the real key is simple: **separate, clean accounts for each activity**, in the format fixed by the Registrar, on time. This one habit makes all three possible — the correct 80P deduction, an easy audit and accurate tax. And since tax rules are detailed and keep changing, treat a CA's advice before filing as mandatory. All the sections/rules given here are for general understanding — confirm your specific case from official sources and an expert.
