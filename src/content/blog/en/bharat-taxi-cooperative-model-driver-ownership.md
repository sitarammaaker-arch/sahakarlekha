Booking a taxi from a mobile phone is now an everyday thing in cities, and from the outside almost all ride-hailing apps look the same. But the real difference lies in the business model behind the curtain — whose company it is, how much is charged to the driver, and who finally gets the benefit of the economic value created as the platform grows. On exactly this question, **Bharat Taxi** has started a different experiment — a ride-hailing platform that runs on the cooperative model, where the driver is not just a service provider but can also become a shareholder.

## This is not an ordinary taxi company

Bharat Taxi is not an ordinary aggregator run by a private company, but a cooperative-led digital mobility platform operated by **Sahakar Taxi Cooperative Limited (STCL)**. STCL is a **Multi-State Cooperative Society**, and its structure is built on the idea that the "Sarathi" who drive the vehicles should not remain just service providers, but can also become members and shareholders of the cooperative.

> 📘 According to the official description, STCL was set up on **6 June 2025** by eight national-level cooperative institutions, and the Bharat Taxi platform was formally launched on **5 February 2026**. This initiative is described as linked to the Ministry of Cooperation's "Sahakar se Samriddhi" vision.

> 💡 According to the government backgrounder, a Sarathi can become a member-owner of the cooperative by taking share capital — the face value of shares is ₹100, and there is also an arrangement of shares worth ₹500 for partner ownership. Representation for Sarathi members on the Board of Directors is also part of the model.

> ⚠️ Becoming a member and personally taking every operational decision are not the same thing — in any cooperative, decisions are taken according to its registered bye-laws, General Body, Board and the set governance structure. The real value of membership will show only when drivers' participation in governance is strong in practice too.

## What is the Zero-Commission model

In the usual app-based taxi model, a commission is cut on every ride. Instead, Bharat Taxi adopts a **subscription-based participation model** — the aim is to let the Sarathi keep the full fare of the trip and make income more predictable. The platform claims to work on surge-free pricing and not to charge an extra convenience fee or surge pricing through the app (a separate service charge is mentioned for the prepaid booths run at airports).

> ⚠️ How sustainable this model is in the long run will be answered only by the operating figures that come in future — the cost of technology, customer support, insurance linkages, marketing, verification and platform maintenance will have to be met from somewhere.

## Is the government giving money to Bharat Taxi?

No. It has been made clear that no direct financial support has been given by the government for the platform's expansion — STCL works within the framework of its registered bye-laws and the **Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act, 2002**, and it would not be correct to call it a direct government taxi company.

> 📘 Yes, it has certainly got strong institutional support from the cooperative ecosystem — according to the government backgrounder, eight national-level cooperative institutions were involved in setting it up, such as NCDC, IFFCO, NABARD, KRIBHCO, Amul's Gujarat Cooperative Milk Marketing Federation, NAFED, NDDB and National Cooperative Export Limited.

## For the driver, low commission is not the only issue

A taxi driver's problem is not only how much commission the app takes — questions like uncertainty of income, risk of accidents, health protection and a voice in platform policies also matter. Bharat Taxi's declared model sets a direction of linking Sarathis with e-Shram registration and providing insurance and welfare linkages — with mention of personal accident insurance and health insurance support at nominal rates.

> ⚠️ The real usefulness of these schemes will depend on how many drivers become eligible for these benefits, how many enrol, and how smooth the system is at the time of a claim — announcing social security is a good start, but its real value will be decided by implementation on the ground.

## What the customer gets

The talk of cooperative ownership matters for the driver, but no passenger will use an app just because the company is a cooperative — they need the vehicle on time, a fair fare, a verified driver and a solution when there is a complaint. The platform includes KYC verification, background checks, ride tracking, SOS support and a grievance redressal system, along with options like bike taxi, auto, economy cab, premium sedan, SUV, rentals and outstation.

## The cooperative platform model — the wider picture

To understand Bharat Taxi, a wider question is useful — when a digital platform is cooperative, how is it basically different from a private aggregator?

- **Ownership:** a private platform is owned by investors/a company; a cooperative platform is owned (in principle) by its members (here the Sarathi).
- **Direction of profit:** in the private model, profit goes to investors; in the cooperative, (in principle) to the members/institution.
- **Decisions:** in the private model, from the top; in the cooperative, (in principle) through governance with members' participation.

> ⚠️ The words "in principle" matter here — the cooperative label does not give these benefits automatically; their real form appears only when members' participation is strong in practice through the bye-laws, General Body and Board. This is an emerging experiment, and only its operation in the coming years will be the real test.

This model is part of a wider idea called "platform cooperativism" — that is, making those who provide services in the gig economy not just "employees/contractors" but shareholders of the platform. (See: [Member rights in a Multi-State Cooperative Society](/blog/multi-state-cooperative-society-member-rights).)

## Support from eight cooperative institutions

STCL is said to have institutional support from several of India's apex cooperative institutions — according to the official description, these eight national-level institutions were involved in setting it up: **NCDC, IFFCO, GCMMF (Amul), KRIBHCO, NAFED, NABARD, NDDB and NCEL**. This is a sign that it is not anyone's solo initiative, but a collective effort of the cooperative ecosystem. Still — as said above — institutional support is no guarantee of an enterprise's success; the same tests of technology, management, capital and governance apply here too.

## Why this is special for the cooperative sector

In India, the word cooperative usually brings to mind milk, farm credit, fertiliser, sugar mills, PACS or marketing societies. Bharat Taxi changes this image a little — it shows that the cooperative model can be tried in the digital platform business too. If drivers become member-owners of the platform instead of remaining only service providers of the app, it offers a different path of ownership in the gig economy.

> 💡 This does not mean the business will succeed as soon as the cooperative label is put on — the quality of technology, professional management, customer acquisition, pricing, driver discipline, capital and governance will all be needed just as in any other technology company. The difference is only that here the person providing the service can also stay part of the institution's ownership structure.

## The role of governance and accounts in a cooperative platform

The life of any cooperative is its transparent governance and accounts — and in a large, multi-state digital cooperative this becomes even more important. When lakhs of Sarathis are member-shareholders, some questions become central: is every member's share capital recorded correctly? Is the account of profit/surplus transparent? Is members' representation on the Board real? Are General Body decisions taken as per the rules? The answer to all of this lies in strong, transparent record-keeping and a governance structure. That is, the promise of being "cooperative" comes true only when clean accounts and democratic governance stand behind it — this is equally true for everyone, from a small PACS to a large digital cooperative.

## What to watch next

This is a new experiment, so its real success will be judged by a few things (and not just by the number of registered drivers/customers):

- **Active use:** how many Sarathis and customers actually use it regularly.
- **Sustainable driver income:** does the model really give a better, stable income.
- **Real governance participation:** are members really involved in decisions.
- **Economic sustainability:** how the cost of technology/support/insurance/marketing is met.
- **Implementation of social security:** how useful the declared insurance/welfare linkages are on the ground.

> ⚠️ So the purpose of this post is not to confirm or promote any claim, but to explain the model — the facts are taken from official sources, and the assessment is left to the figures of the coming time.

## In closing

Bharat Taxi is an interesting and new experiment — it shows that the cooperative model, which so far has been linked mainly with milk, fertiliser, credit and agriculture, is now being tried in the digital platform business too, where the driver who provides the service can also become a shareholder of the institution. The idea is attractive, but its real test will come not from the label but from implementation on the ground — from sustainable driver income, real member participation, transparent accounts and strong governance. So it should be watched with interest, but conclusions should be left to the figures of the coming years. Whatever happens, it certainly opens a new direction for cooperation.
