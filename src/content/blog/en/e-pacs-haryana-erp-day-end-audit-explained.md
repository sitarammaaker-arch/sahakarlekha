The term "e-PACS" is now common in Haryana's cooperative world, but what exactly does it mean? This guide explains the technical side of [PACS computerisation](/blog/haryana-pacs-computerization-710-pacs-e-pacs-explained) — the ERP system, the day-end process and audit — in simple language.

> ⚠️ **Information checked up to 18 October 2026.** The official PACS computerisation figures are from the Ministry of Cooperation's Lok Sabha reply (2 December 2025); the source for the ERP features (22 modules, 20 languages etc.) and the August 2026 figures is a news report that cites the Minister's written reply in the Rajya Sabha — see "Sources" below. The explanation of day-end and Maker-Checker is based on general banking practice; check exactly what happens in your system in its user manual or training.

## What does e-PACS mean?

An e-PACS is a PACS that now works fully on the shared national-level ERP software instead of paper registers — that is, membership, loans, deposits, accounts and audit are all recorded and processed digitally.

**Figures:** According to the Ministry of Cooperation's Lok Sabha reply (2 December 2025), **79,630 PACS** of 31 States/UTs were sanctioned under the computerisation project, of which **60,424 PACS** had come onto the ERP software. According to a news report (Tech Observer, 12 August 2026), the Minister told the Rajya Sabha that **63,686 PACS** have come onto the ERP, and of them **54,707 have been declared e-PACS** — we have not opened this original reply.

In simple words — "e-PACS" is not a new kind of society; its legal status is the same as an ordinary PACS. The only difference is in the **way** of working: digital instead of paper. It is a single change, but its effect on the working of the whole society is deep — faster service, accurate accounts, easier audit and new digital services all open up from this one change.

## What does the ERP system include?

According to the same news report, the system has **22 modules**, covering work such as loans, deposits, accounts, audit, member management and procurement. The system runs on a centralised cloud with cyber-security measures, is available in **20 languages**, and can connect with Aadhaar services, the core banking systems of state/district cooperative banks, POS machines, Common Service Centres (CSC), the National Cooperative Database and state portals. According to PIB (4 February 2026), this shared software works through a Common Accounting System (CAS) and MIS, links PACS with NABARD through StCBs and DCCBs, and can be modified to suit each state's needs.

### What 22 modules means

"22 modules" simply means that almost every task of a PACS — adding new members, giving and recovering loans, taking deposits, daily accounts, year-end accounts, audit, stock and procurement — exists in this one system as separate parts (modules). So all the work is in one place and interlinked — for example, as soon as a member is given a loan, its effect shows automatically in the accounts and the member's account. This integration removes the biggest weakness of manual registers (separate books not tallying with each other).

## What is the "Day-End" process?

Like any banking or financial system, e-PACS also has to run a "day-end" process at the end of every working day — in it, all the day's transactions are finally closed, balances are tallied, and the system is made ready for the next day. In common banking systems, the next day's work does not start until day-end is complete — check exactly what the rule is in your ERP in its user manual.

> 💡 Make day-end a daily habit. In PACS that delay day-end, the next day's work gets held up and reconciliation at month-end becomes difficult.

### Why day-end matters so much

Day-end is not just a technical button — it is the process that makes each day's accounts "final". It has three big benefits: first, each day's balance is tallied the same day, so any error is caught immediately, not months later; second, changes to a closed day are generally made only through a set process and approval, which keeps the records reliable; and third, at year-end and audit time the accounts are already tallied and ready. That is why, as in banking, day-end must not be put off but completed every day — this discipline is the life of a digital system.

## The Maker-Checker audit system

According to the news report, the e-PACS platform has a "Maker-Checker" control — transactions go through verification at more than one level before final approval — and also an on-system audit module (one person makes the entry — the "maker", another checks and approves it — the "checker"). This greatly reduces the scope for any one person to commit fraud alone. [Read a detailed guide on how audit works here.](/blog/how-cooperative-society-audit-works)

Maker-Checker is actually an old, proven internal-control principle that has now been applied in digital form. Its core idea is simple — "two eyes are better than one." When one person creates an entry and another independently checks and approves it, not only is deliberate fraud stopped, but unintentional mistakes are also caught. In a digital system, the added benefit is that "who created it, who approved it" — everything stays on record (audit trail), which strengthens both accountability and transparency. (See: [internal control](/blog/internal-control-in-cooperatives).)

## Paper PACS vs e-PACS — a comparison

| Aspect | Paper PACS | e-PACS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Records | Handwritten registers | Shared ERP system |
| Balance | Added by hand, delays | Automatic at day-end |
| Error risk | High | Low (Maker-Checker, automatic checks) |
| Audit | Difficult, prone to objections | Smooth, with trail |
| Speed of service | Slow | Fast |
| Additional services | No | Possible through CSC link |
| Data safety | Risk of fire/water/theft | Cloud-secured, backup |

The comparison is clear — e-PACS is better than the paper system in almost every respect, provided it is run properly. This "provided" is the most important part — technology gives the opportunity, but its benefit comes only from disciplined use.

## Cyber-security and data

Since all accounts are now digital, data security becomes just as important. The system runs on a centralised cloud with cyber-security measures — that is, the data sits on a secure server, not on a single weak computer or register. Still, some responsibilities remain at PACS level: a separate login for every person, keeping passwords confidential, removing old access when staff change, and caution with suspicious links/emails. However secure the technology, access discipline is finally in the user's hands. (See: [data security and backup](/blog/data-security-and-backup).)

## What do farmers and members gain?

After a PACS becomes an e-PACS, members get services like loan sanction, deposits and account statements faster, because records are instantly available in digital form instead of on paper. Also, the link with CSC means the PACS is no longer limited to agricultural credit but can also offer other digital government services. (See: [PACS are now CSCs too](/blog/pacs-csc-haryana-common-service-centre-income).)

## Links with other systems

A big strength of e-PACS is that it is not a lone, isolated system — it is linked with several national systems: **Aadhaar** (member identity and verification), **Core Banking System (CBS)** (transactions with cooperative banks), and **Common Service Centre (CSC)** (other digital services). The practical meaning of this link is that a farmer can get many government-digital services, along with loans and deposits, at his own village PACS, in one place — that is, the PACS becomes a multi-service centre. This link is what takes e-PACS beyond just a "digital ledger" and makes it a living service platform.

## What a working day looks like

A normal day at an e-PACS goes something like this: in the morning the operator logs into the system (the previous day's day-end is done, so the new day is open); transactions are recorded through the day — a member deposits, someone gets a loan, someone pays an instalment — each entry in the right module; large/sensitive transactions go through Maker-Checker; and at the end of the day the operator checks all transactions and runs **day-end**, which makes that day's accounts final. With this disciplined routine, each day's accounts stay tallied and safe the same day — and this is the root of e-PACS's reliability.

## Essential habits for whoever runs an e-PACS

To keep an e-PACS running smoothly, the secretary/operator should adopt some habits:

- **Day-end every day:** without fail, at the end of each day.
- **Prompt and correct entry:** record transactions immediately, in the right module.
- **Follow Maker-Checker:** don't skip the approval process thinking it is a minor thing.
- **Regular reconciliation:** Trial Balance and bank reconciliation on time.
- **Backup/security:** passwords confidential, access limited; don't share logins.

These habits are not an extra burden — they are the conditions for a digital system to give its full benefit. Only a PACS that adopts them truly gets fast service, accurate accounts and easy audit.

## Three things to remember

- 🖥️ e-PACS means that all work, from membership to audit, is done digitally on a shared national ERP system (22 modules).
- 🔒 The Maker-Checker principle — two levels of independent oversight on every transaction — is the main tool for reducing the scope for fraud.
- ⏰ Completing day-end every day is a discipline, not an option — delaying it affects both the next day's work and the month's reconciliation.
- 🔗 e-PACS is linked with Aadhaar, core banking and CSC — so it is not just a digital ledger but a multi-service platform.

## In the end

e-PACS is the practical face of the modernisation of the cooperative sector — it shows how technology can make an ordinary village society fast, transparent and reliable. But it is important to remember that the system does nothing by itself; the real difference comes when those who run it do day-end daily, follow Maker-Checker, make correct entries and keep regular reconciliation. That is, the success of e-PACS lies in the combination of technology and discipline. For a society that adopts these habits, going digital becomes not just a compliance but a path to better service and easier audit.
