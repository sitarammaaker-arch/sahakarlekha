# Share capital: the difference between authorised, issued and paid-up

On hearing "share capital", three words often cause confusion — authorised, issued and paid-up. All three are different, and to keep the correct figure on the Balance Sheet it is necessary to know the difference. This article sorts out **the difference between authorised, issued and paid-up share capital** — with examples, entries and cooperative-specific points — in simple language.

## Why share capital matters

In a cooperative society share capital is not just a figure — it is the foundation of the society's own funds, the basis of membership, and often also the measure of borrowing capacity. So understanding the difference between its three forms (authorised/issued/paid-up) is necessary for every secretary and treasurer — because the Balance Sheet, the member register and profit distribution all rest on it. (Member accounting: [Member and share register](/blog/member-and-share-accounting).)

## First the basics: share and face value
- **Share:** The unit of a member's contribution to the society's capital. Holding at least one share is often a condition of membership.
- **Face Value:** The fixed value of each share (such as ₹10 or ₹100). A member's share capital = face value × number of shares.

Face value is fixed once and stays constant — unlike company shares, its "market price" does not go up and down. So share accounting in a cooperative is straightforward: number of shares × face value = that much share capital. This simplicity is what makes a cooperative share easy to understand.

## How authorised capital is fixed

Authorised capital is fixed in the bye-laws at the time of registration — it is the upper limit up to which the society can issue shares. When the need grows, it can be raised as per rules (by bye-law amendment/approval). This limit is kept so that capital growth stays planned and controlled. As long as the society is within this limit, it can keep issuing new shares; on reaching the limit, the authorised capital has to be raised first.

## The real benefit of the three — where the confusion clears

The practical benefit of keeping these three separate is that at any time you can know: the most capital the society can raise (authorised), how many shares it has allotted so far (issued), and how much money is actually in hand (paid-up). The biggest misunderstanding happens when people take "authorised capital" to be the society's real money — whereas it is only a permitted limit, on paper. The real money is only what members have paid. Once this difference is understood, reading the Balance Sheet becomes much easier.

## Three "capitals" — one after another
| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Authorised capital** | The permitted **upper limit** to raise (from rules/registration) |
| **Issued capital** | The shares **issued/allotted** to members |
| **Paid-up capital** | The amount members have **actually paid** |

> 💡 Remember the order: authorised (limit) ≥ issued (allotted) ≥ paid-up (received).

Think of this order as a "funnel" — at the top is authorised (the largest permitted amount), out of it the shares allotted are issued, and out of that the money that actually came in is paid-up. Each next level is smaller than or equal to the previous one, never bigger. If issued ever shows more than authorised — there is a mistake somewhere, because that is not legally possible.

### At a glance — all three together

| Capital | Example (₹100 face value) | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authorised | Permitted limit (10,000 shares) | ₹10,00,000 |
| Issued | 6,000 shares issued | ₹6,00,000 |
| Paid-up | Amount received | ₹5,50,000 |
| Calls in arrears | Issued − paid-up | ₹50,000 |

This table shows the relationship of the three at a glance — and the same structure applies to every society, only the figures change.

## A simple example
Suppose the face value is ₹100 and the authorised limit is ₹10,00,000:
- The society issued 6,000 shares → **issued** = ₹6,00,000
- Members have paid ₹5,50,000 so far → **paid-up** = ₹5,50,000

On the capital side of the Balance Sheet, **paid-up capital** is the real figure. Here ₹50,000 (issued − paid-up) is still to be collected from members — this is called "calls in arrears". Note that out of the authorised ₹10,00,000 the society has so far issued shares of only ₹6,00,000; the room for the remaining ₹4,00,000 is left for the future — that is, the society can issue more shares when needed, without raising the authorised limit.

> ⚠️ "Authorised capital = money the society has" — this is wrong. The money is only what has been *paid up*.

## The entry for issuing shares

When a member buys shares, a simple receipt entry is made. Suppose a member pays 10 shares × ₹100 = ₹1,000 in cash:

| Account | Dr | Cr |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash | ₹1,000 | — |
| Share capital | — | ₹1,000 |

This raises paid-up (and issued) capital by ₹1,000, and the member's share balance too. Note — if an admission fee is taken along with it, it goes in this same entry to a separate "income" account, not to share capital; never mix the two. (Voucher basics: [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).)

## Part payment (calls) — when the full money does not come at once

Sometimes the full amount of a share is taken in instalments (calls). For example, of a ₹100 face value, ₹60 now and ₹40 later. Then:

- **Now:** paid-up capital ₹60/share.
- **Calls in arrears:** ₹40/share, which is in issued but not in paid-up.

When the remaining ₹40 comes, one more receipt entry is made for it and the paid-up capital becomes full. So the difference between issued and paid-up often comes from these calls in arrears.

In most small cooperative societies the full amount of the share is taken at once, so there issued and paid-up are often equal. The arrangement of instalments (calls) is seen more in big or capital-heavy societies. But knowing the concept is necessary, so that when there are calls in arrears you can understand and record them correctly.

## Where calls in arrears go on the Balance Sheet

The amount still to be collected from members (calls in arrears) is in a way the society's "recoverable amount". Many systems show it deducted from paid-up capital, so that only the money actually received shows on the Balance Sheet. The main point is this — do not treat calls in arrears as "already received"; keep a separate eye on them and collect them on time, otherwise they can hang on for years and become a question in audit.

## Cooperative shares — how they differ from a company

The shares of a cooperative society differ from company shares in several ways:

- **One member, one vote:** However many shares, every member has only one vote (whereas in a company votes come in proportion to shares).
- **Maximum limit:** There is often a limit on how many shares one member can hold — so that no single member dominates the society and the democratic balance stays.
- **Not traded in the market:** Cooperative shares are not sold on the stock exchange; their withdrawal or transfer happens only as per the rules and bye-laws.
- **Stable value:** Shares usually stay at face value and do not go up and down with a market price.
- **Government share capital:** In some societies the government also contributes share capital (as assistance), which has separate accounting and conditions.

These features reflect the cooperative principle of "members, not capital, at the centre".

This also has a big effect on profit distribution — dividend is given in proportion to share capital, but everyone's vote stays equal. That is, a member with more shares may get more dividend, but not more control. This balance sets cooperation apart from a company, where capital gives control too. (See: [Profit distribution and reserve fund](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves).)

## Share certificate
The society gives every member a **share certificate** — showing the name, number of shares and value. It is proof of the member's ownership. (Note — cooperative shares are not traded in the market.) Keep the record of issuing certificates in the share register, and on loss/replacement issue a duplicate through the set process. This small document is the most solid proof of ownership for the member, so keeping it correct and up to date is necessary.

## Matching share capital

An essential check: **the total of all members' share balances = paid-up share capital in the Balance Sheet/Trial Balance**. If the two do not match, some share entry has been missed or gone to the wrong account. Do this matching every month — it is the bridge between the member register and the main ledger. If the total does not match, correction is easy when caught early; after months the same difference becomes a big tangle. So make it a monthly habit, do not wait for the year-end. (See: [Member and share register](/blog/member-and-share-accounting).)

## The effect of share withdrawal/refund

When a member leaves the society and their share capital is returned as per rules, both issued and paid-up capital go down by that much (the authorised limit stays the same). The entry: Share capital Dr, Cash/Bank Cr. So the capital figure is not fixed — it keeps rising with new members and falling with withdrawals.

One essential check before withdrawal — does the member owe the society any dues (loan/recovery)? If so, first adjust them against their share amount, then return the balance. This way the society suffers no loss and the accounts stay clean. Also remember that the withdrawal process and limits are set by the state Act and bye-laws, so check the rules before a big withdrawal.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating authorised as "money"** → the money is only the paid-up capital.
- **Treating issued and paid-up as the same** → they can differ because of calls in arrears.
- **Member total ≠ ledger** → match every month.
- **Putting admission fee into share capital** → the fee is income, keep it separate.
- **Mixing government share capital with member capital** → keep a separate account, because its source and conditions are different.
- **Treating calls in arrears as "already received"** → treat them as recoverable and collect them on time.
- **Not keeping a record of share certificates** → record every issued/duplicate certificate in the register.

## Frequently asked questions
**What is the difference between authorised and paid-up capital?** — Authorised = upper limit; paid-up = the amount actually paid.
**What is issued capital?** — The value of the shares issued to members.
**How to work out a member's share capital?** — Face value × number of shares held.
**Which figure goes on the Balance Sheet?** — Paid-up share capital, because that is what has actually been collected.
**What are calls in arrears?** — The part of issued capital still to be collected from members (issued − paid-up).
**Do more shares mean more votes?** — No; in a cooperative it is one member, one vote, however many shares.
**How to raise the authorised limit?** — Through bye-law amendment/the process as per rules; confirm the exact method with your Act/RCS.
**What is the effect of a share refund on capital?** — Both issued and paid-up go down; the authorised limit stays the same.

## Three things to remember
- 📊 Order: authorised ≥ issued ≥ paid-up (never the other way round).
- 💰 The real figure on the Balance Sheet = paid-up capital, not authorised.
- 🧮 Member capital = face value × number of shares; the total of all members = paid-up capital in the ledger.

## In the end
Once the difference between these three words is clear, both the share register and the Balance Sheet become clear. Remember — authorised is only a limit, issued is the shares allotted, and paid-up is what was actually collected. The Balance Sheet always shows paid-up capital, and its total must match the member register. Take this much care and share accounting will stay transparent and audit-ready all year.
