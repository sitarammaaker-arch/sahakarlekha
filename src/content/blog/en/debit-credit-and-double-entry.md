# Debit, credit and double entry: two sides of every entry

This is the first step in learning accounting — every transaction has **two sides**: a "debit" (naam, Debit) and a "credit" (jama, Credit). Once you properly understand **debit, credit and double entry**, voucher entry becomes easy, the Trial Balance matches on its own, and you can trust every report. This article explains it right from the start — with the golden rules, many examples and a source.

## What is the double entry system

In double entry (double-entry), every transaction is written in at least **two accounts** — on one side a **debit (Dr)** of an equal amount, on the other side a **credit (Cr)**. This keeps the books always balanced.

> 💡 Think — every amount *comes* from somewhere and *goes* somewhere. Write both, and the books will stay equal on their own.

This system is ~500 years old: the Italian mathematician **Luca Pacioli** gave the first systematic description of double-entry bookkeeping in his book in 1494 — that is why he is called the "father of accounting". Today accounts all over the world run on this same principle. (Source: [Double-entry bookkeeping and Luca Pacioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-entry_bookkeeping))

**An analogy:** Think that you bought a bag of grain for ₹500. On one side **grain came** to you, on the other side your **₹500 went** — two sides of the same event. Accounting records both these sides; leave out even one and the picture is incomplete. This is the soul of double entry — "every event has two sides".

## The real meaning of debit and credit

- **Debit (naam)** = the **left** side of the entry.
- **Credit (jama)** = the **right** side of the entry.

Which one to debit and which to credit depends on the *type* of account — not on "money in/out".

> ⚠️ The most common misunderstanding — "debit means money went, credit means money came". This is not always right! When cash (an asset) **increases**, it is **debited**.

## Bank "credit" versus your books — why the opposite?

This is where the biggest confusion happens. When money comes into your account, the bank SMS says *"credited"*. But in your own books the same deposit is **Dr (debit)** because **cash/bank increased**. Why is that?

Because **the bank statement is made from the bank's point of view, not yours.** For the bank your deposit is its **liability** (it has to return it to you), so when it increases the bank credits it. For you the same thing is your **asset**, so when it increases you debit it. Both are right — only the point of view is reversed.

> 💡 Remember: the bank's "credited/debited" is not the rule for your books. In your own books always look at the *type* of account.

## Golden rules — two views

There are two ways to decide what to debit and what to credit. Both reach the same result.

### 1) Traditional rules (three types of accounts)

- **Personal account (Personal — person/institution/member):** *Debit the receiver (Dr), credit the giver (Cr).* E.g. loan given to a member → member (receiver) Dr.
- **Real account (Real — assets):** *Debit what comes in (Dr), credit what goes out (Cr).* E.g. furniture bought → furniture (came in) Dr, cash (went out) Cr.
- **Nominal account (Nominal — income/expenses):** *Debit all expenses and losses (Dr), credit all income and gains (Cr).* E.g. salary (expense) Dr; interest (income) Cr.

### 2) Modern rules (based on the accounting equation)

| Account type | On increase | On decrease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset | Dr | Cr |
| Expense | Dr | Cr |
| Liability | Cr | Dr |
| Capital | Cr | Dr |
| Income | Cr | Dr |

> 💡 The modern rule is easy to remember: **assets and expenses increase → Dr; liabilities, capital and income increase → Cr.** (See: [Accounting equation](/blog/accounting-equation-explained).)

## Understand through examples

**(a) ₹5,000 interest received in cash:**

| Account | Side | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash | Dr ₹5,000 | Asset increased |
| Interest income | Cr ₹5,000 | Income increased |

**(b) Loan of ₹50,000 given to a member (cash):**

| Account | Side | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Member loan (receivable) | Dr ₹50,000 | Asset increased |
| Cash | Cr ₹50,000 | Asset decreased |

**(c) ₹8,000 salary paid in cash:**

| Account | Side | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salary (expense) | Dr ₹8,000 | Expense increased |
| Cash | Cr ₹8,000 | Asset decreased |

**(d) A member deposited ₹20,000 (deposit):**

| Account | Side | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash | Dr ₹20,000 | Asset increased |
| Member deposit (liability) | Cr ₹20,000 | Liability increased |

**(e) Goods worth ₹12,000 bought on credit (to be paid later):**

| Account | Side | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchases/stock | Dr ₹12,000 | Asset/expense increased |
| Supplier (liability) | Cr ₹12,000 | Liability increased |

In every example Dr = Cr — this is double entry. Note that in each case when one side increases, the other either decreases or a liability/income is created.

## T-account — how the sides look

Think of every account as the English letter "T" — the left part is **Dr**, the right part is **Cr**. When cash increases, write it on the left (Dr) side; when it decreases, on the right (Cr) side. At the end of the month/year the account balance comes from the difference between the totals of these two sides — whichever side's total is larger, the balance comes on that side (Dr or Cr) — and this balance goes on to make the ledger and the Trial Balance. (See: [Ledger and posting](/blog/ledger-and-posting-explained).)

## Why double entry — its benefits

- **Automatic balance:** Every Dr has an equal Cr, so the books are always equal — the [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained) matches.
- **Complete record:** Both sides of every transaction are recorded, so there is a correct picture of income, expenses, assets, liabilities — everything.
- **Catching mistakes:** An imbalance at once signals that there is a slip somewhere.
- **Reliable reports:** Profit and loss, income and expenditure and the Balance Sheet — all are built on this strong, balanced foundation, so the figures can be trusted.

## Single entry versus double entry

- **Single entry (single-entry):** Only one side (often just cash in and out) is written — like a simple cash book (rokad bahi). It does not give the full picture, no Trial Balance can be made, and catching mistakes is hard.
- **Double entry (double-entry):** Both sides of every transaction — so a correct, balanced record of assets, liabilities, income and expenses.

For cooperative societies **double entry is the only correct and auditable** system — serious accounts cannot run on single entry, and auditors do not accept it either.

## Common mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Taking "debit = expense"** — no; an asset increasing is also Dr. Avoid it: look at the account type.
- **One-sided entry** — writing only one side. Avoid it: always both sides.
- **Choosing the wrong account** — such as treating a member deposit as income (it is a liability). Avoid it: identify the type.
- **Amounts not matching** — Dr and Cr amounts different. Avoid it: keep both equal.
- **Reversing the point of view** — taking the bank's "credited" as the rule for your books. Avoid it: in your own books look at the account type.

> 💡 The good thing: in double-entry software (such as SahakarLekha) every voucher posts only with Dr=Cr, so these mistakes drop on their own.

## Correct double entry = the society's credibility

When both sides of every transaction are recorded correctly, the Trial Balance matches, the final accounts and audit stay clean, and the society's audit grading and credibility improve. On the other hand, wrong Dr/Cr spoils the picture of income/expenses and brings objections in audit. That is, this basic rule is not just "bookish" — it is directly linked to the society's health.

## Voucher types and Dr/Cr

Daily entries are usually made in four kinds of vouchers — the same Dr/Cr rule applies in each:

- **Receipt:** Money came in → Cash/Bank Dr, the source (income/member/liability) Cr.
- **Payment:** Money went out → expense/payee Dr, Cash/Bank Cr.
- **Journal:** Entries without cash → such as interest payable, adjustments, corrections.
- **Contra:** Cash ↔ Bank → bank on one side, cash on the other (both your own assets).

> 💡 Whatever the voucher, you only have to decide two things — which accounts changed, and whether each one increased or decreased. The rest is decided by the Dr/Cr rule. (See: [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).)

## An easy way to remember

- Assets and expenses **come to you / are spent** → if they increase, **Dr**.
- Liabilities, capital and income are what you **owe / receive** → if they increase, **Cr**.
- If confused, just ask two questions: "What *type* is this account, and is it increasing or decreasing?" — these two answers decide Dr/Cr on their own.

## Its use in a cooperative society

Every activity of the society runs on these same rules — share capital (Cr), member deposits (Cr, liability), loan disbursement (Dr, asset), interest income (Cr), salary/expenses (Dr). Once the basics are clear, every voucher starts to feel easy. (See: [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide) and [Basics of cooperative accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

## Three things to remember

- ➕ Every transaction has two sides — Dr and Cr, for equal amounts.
- 🧭 Which side depends on the type of account — not on "in/out".
- ✅ Correct Dr/Cr = a matching Trial Balance, a clean audit and reliable reports.

## Frequently asked questions

**What do Dr and Cr mean?** — Dr = debit (left side), Cr = credit (right side).
**Is a debit always an expense?** — No; an asset (such as cash) increasing is also a debit.
**Why are both equal in every entry?** — That is the rule of double entry, which makes the Trial Balance match.
**How many golden rules are there?** — Three in the traditional system (personal, real, nominal); equation-based rules in the modern one.
**Should a member deposit be treated as income?** — No; it is the society's liability (it has to be returned), so it goes in Cr as a liability.
**Who discovered double entry?** — Its first systematic description was given by Luca Pacioli in 1494 — that is why he is called the "father of accounting".
**The bank said "credited", but I put Dr in my books — is that right?** — Yes; the bank credits it from its point of view (your deposit is its liability), you debit it as your asset increases. Both are right.
**Can single entry do the job?** — For petty cash maybe, but for a society's serious, auditable accounts double entry is necessary.
**What is Dr in a receipt voucher?** — When money comes in, Cash/Bank Dr, and the reason it came (income/member/liability) Cr.
**Which is better, the traditional or the modern rule?** — Both give the same result; the modern (equation-based) one often feels easier to new learners.
**Loan given to a member — what is Dr?** — Member loan (receivable, asset) Dr; Cash/Bank Cr (because cash decreased).
**A member made a deposit — how to record it?** — Cash/Bank Dr (asset increased); member deposit Cr (it is the society's liability, to be returned).
**What is the effect of wrong Dr/Cr?** — The picture of income/expenses and assets/liabilities is spoiled, the Trial Balance/reports go wrong, and objections come in audit.

Think of the two sides of every event, record both for equal amounts, and the Trial Balance will match on its own.
