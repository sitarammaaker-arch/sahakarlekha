# How to Do Voucher Entry: A Step-by-Step Guide

Every record in a cooperative society's accounting starts with one thing — the **voucher**. If the voucher is right, your cash book, ledger, Trial Balance and Balance Sheet will all come out right; if the voucher is wrong, every report is wrong. So understanding **voucher entry** properly is the most important skill. This guide explains all four types of vouchers, the step-by-step method for each, compound entries, narration and common mistakes — all with examples.

## What is a voucher?

A voucher is the written proof of a single transaction — who gave or took how much, to whom, under which head. It is the **first entry (source document)** of accounting, from which all the other books are made. In a digital system you fill in the voucher and the cash book, ledger and reports update **automatically**. (See: [Ledger and posting](/blog/ledger-and-posting-explained).)

## Four types of vouchers

Almost every transaction falls into one of these four:

| Voucher | When to use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Receipt** | Money **came in** | Share capital from a member, loan recovery, sales receipt |
| **Payment** | Money **went out** | Salary, rent, purchase payment, electricity bill |
| **Journal** | Adjustment entry without cash/bank | Depreciation, outstanding, transfer, correction |
| **Contra** | Movement between cash ↔ bank | Cash deposited in bank, cash withdrawn from bank |

> 💡 Simple rule: money came in → Receipt; money went out → Payment; only between cash and bank → Contra; all other adjustments → Journal.

Some systems also keep Sales and Purchase as separate voucher types, but from the accounting point of view they too are forms of these four — a credit sale is a journal-type entry, and a cash sale is a receipt. So these four are the foundation.

## The essential parts of a voucher

Whatever the type, a complete voucher is made of these parts — check each part while filling it:

| Part | What to fill | Why it is needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Voucher number** | Serial, automatic | To check order and completeness in the audit |
| **Date** | The day of the transaction | Decides the correct financial year and period |
| **Type** | Receipt/Payment/Journal/Contra | Decides the direction of posting |
| **Accounts (Dr/Cr)** | Which accounts | Ledger and reports are built from here |
| **Amount** | Dr total = Cr total | An unbalanced voucher should not be saved |
| **Narration** | From whom, which head, which bill | For identification later and for the audit |
| **Reference/document** | Bill/receipt/cheque number | Links the trail of proof |

> ✅ A good rule: any voucher is incomplete until the Dr total and the Cr total are equal — this is the first condition of double entry.

## 1) Receipt voucher — step by step

Suppose ₹2,000 share capital is received in cash from member Ramkumar:

1. **Voucher type** → Receipt.
2. **Date** → the same day the money was received.
3. **Amount** → ₹2,000.
4. **Received from (Cr account)** → Share capital.
5. **Where it came (Dr account)** → Cash.
6. **Narration** → "Ramkumar — share capital".
7. **Save.**

The system will automatically record **Cash Dr ₹2,000, Share capital Cr ₹2,000**, and the cash book and both accounts are updated.

## 2) Payment voucher — step by step

Suppose ₹8,000 rent is paid in cash:

1. **Voucher type** → Payment.
2. **Date, amount** → today, ₹8,000.
3. **Under which head it went (Dr account)** → Rent (expense).
4. **Where it went from (Cr account)** → Cash.
5. **Narration** → "Office rent for the month of August".
6. **Save.**

Result: **Rent Dr ₹8,000, Cash Cr ₹8,000**.

## 3) Contra voucher — cash ↔ bank

Suppose ₹50,000 cash is deposited in the bank:

1. **Voucher type** → Contra.
2. **Date, amount** → today, ₹50,000.
3. **Where it went (Dr)** → Bank.
4. **Where it came from (Cr)** → Cash.
5. **Narration** → "Cash deposited in bank".

Result: **Bank Dr ₹50,000, Cash Cr ₹50,000** — both are your own assets, only the place changed. (See: [Cash book vs bank book](/blog/cash-book-vs-bank-book).)

> 💡 How to identify it: if the transaction involves **only cash and bank** and no third account, it is a contra. It creates neither income nor expense — money only moves from one place to another.

## An example with bank/cheque/UPI

Today most transactions happen through the bank. The voucher stays the same, only the **Bank** account comes in place of cash, and write the cheque/UTR number in the reference.

**Example (purchase payment by cheque):** ₹25,000 paid to a supplier by cheque:

1. **Type** → Payment.
2. **Dr** → Supplier (creditor reduced) or Purchase account.
3. **Cr** → Bank.
4. **Narration** → "M/s XYZ — cheque #004512".

Result: **Supplier/Purchase Dr ₹25,000, Bank Cr ₹25,000**. Writing the cheque number makes bank reconciliation easier later.

## 4) Journal voucher — non-cash adjustments

A journal voucher is used where cash/bank is not involved — such as depreciation, outstanding items, or corrections.

**Example (depreciation):** ₹5,000 depreciation on furniture:
- **Depreciation (expense) Dr ₹5,000**
- **Furniture (asset) Cr ₹5,000**

**Example (outstanding salary):** at month-end ₹10,000 salary is due but not yet paid:
- **Salary (expense) Dr ₹10,000**
- **Salary payable (liability) Cr ₹10,000**

## Compound voucher — many accounts at once

When a single transaction has more than one Dr or Cr, use a compound voucher.

**Example (salary, with TDS and EPF):** gross salary ₹20,000, from which TDS ₹1,000 and EPF ₹1,800 were deducted, and the rest paid in cash:

| Account | Dr ₹ | Cr ₹ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salary (expense) | 20,000 | — |
| TDS payable (liability) | — | 1,000 |
| EPF payable (liability) | — | 1,800 |
| Cash | — | 17,200 |
| **Total** | **20,000** | **20,000** |

> 🔍 When a sale/purchase has many item categories or GST, a compound voucher also shows each category separately — the report comes out clean.

## Sales and purchase vouchers — cooperative examples

In cooperative societies (such as consumer stores or PACS) sales and purchases happen daily. Record these with vouchers too:

**Cash sale (receipt-type):** fertiliser worth ₹3,000 sold for cash:
- **Cash Dr ₹3,000**
- **Fertiliser sales (income) Cr ₹3,000**

**Credit purchase (journal-type):** goods worth ₹40,000 bought on credit:
- **Purchase Dr ₹40,000**
- **Supplier (liability) Cr ₹40,000**

Later, when you pay the supplier, a **payment voucher** will be made: Supplier Dr, Bank/Cash Cr. This way the credit and the payment show clearly through two separate vouchers.

> 🔍 When sales/purchases are in many categories (fertiliser, consumer goods, seed), choose each category's own account — this way the Trading Account and Income & Expenditure show a separate picture for each category.

## One day's vouchers — a glimpse

Suppose a normal day of a PACS is recorded like this:

| # | Type | Narration | Dr | Cr |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Receipt | Member share capital in cash | Cash | Share capital |
| 2 | Receipt | Loan instalment recovered in cash | Cash | Member loan |
| 3 | Payment | Staff salary | Salary | Bank |
| 4 | Contra | Day's cash deposited in bank | Bank | Cash |
| 5 | Journal | Month-end depreciation | Depreciation | Asset |

At the end of the day all these vouchers together build the cash book and bank book automatically, and at the end of the month/year these same entries reach the Trial Balance.

## Easy mode vs Expert mode

- **Easy mode:** one Dr and one Cr — straight everyday entries.
- **Expert (Compound) mode:** more than one Dr/Cr — like the salary example above.

New users should start with Easy mode; use compound when needed.

## How to decide Dr/Cr — a glimpse

If you are confused, look at the account type and direction (see: [Debit-credit and double entry](/blog/debit-credit-and-double-entry)):

- **Asset/expense increases** → Dr.
- **Liability/capital/income increases** → Cr.

In a receipt, cash/bank is usually Dr; in a payment, cash/bank is Cr — the other side is decided by the account type.

## Why a good narration is necessary

Write a clear narration in every voucher — from/to whom, under which head, which reference (bill/receipt number). A good narration makes it easy to identify the entry months later and in the audit, and keep the supporting documents (bill, receipt) attached. (See: [Voucher narration and documents](/blog/voucher-narration-and-documents).)

> 💡 A vague narration like "Miscellaneous expense ₹500" becomes an audit objection; a clear narration like "Stationery — bill #212" gives confidence.

## Approval and cancelling the right way

- **Maker–Checker:** one person makes the voucher, another approves it — this gives transparency and audit safety.
- **Cancelling:** never **delete** a wrong voucher — **cancel** it, so that the audit trail stays; then make a new correct entry.

> ⚠️ Quietly removing or overwriting a voucher becomes the biggest audit objection. Always take the route of cancel + new correct entry.

## What happens after the voucher

As soon as a voucher is saved, this chain runs: **voucher → posting to cash book/ledger → Trial Balance → final accounts**. So one correct voucher keeps the whole chain of reports correct — and one wrong voucher spoils the whole of it. (See: [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained).)

## Common mistakes — and how to avoid them

- **Wrong date** (in a previous/wrong FY) → always check the correct financial year.
- **Reversing Dr/Cr in receipts and payments** → decide by "did money come in or go out".
- **Empty narration** → always write a clear narration.
- **Choosing the wrong account** → for example treating a member deposit as income (it is a liability).
- **Deleting a voucher** → never; cancel it.
- **Unbalanced entry** → if the Dr total and Cr total are not equal, do not save; first find the difference.
- **Leaving out the reference number** → always write the cheque/bill/receipt number, otherwise reconciliation becomes hard later.

## Three things to remember

- 🧾 Identify the four types — Receipt, Payment, Contra, Journal.
- 🔁 Decide Dr/Cr by "did money come in or go out"; if confused, look at the account type.
- ✍️ A clear narration in every entry; if it is wrong, cancel it, do not delete it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which voucher when?** — Money came in → Receipt; went out → Payment; cash↔bank → Contra; other adjustments → Journal.
**What is Dr in a receipt?** — When money comes in, Cash/Bank is Dr, and the reason it came (income/member/liability) is Cr.
**When to use a compound voucher?** — When one transaction has many Dr/Cr — such as salary (with TDS/EPF) or a sale with GST.
**How to correct a wrong voucher?** — Do not delete it; cancel it and make a new correct entry, so the audit trail is kept.
**Why is narration necessary?** — To identify the entry later and in the audit; a vague narration becomes an objection.
**What is Maker–Checker?** — One person makes, another approves — a system to prevent mistakes and fraud.
**Are separate vouchers needed for sales/purchases?** — No; a cash sale is a receipt, a credit purchase is journal-type. Some systems give them separate names for convenience, but the accounting is the same.
**What changes in cheque/UPI transactions?** — Only the Bank account comes in place of cash; write the cheque/UTR number in the reference so bank reconciliation is easy.
**Why should an unbalanced voucher not be saved?** — The condition of double entry is Dr total = Cr total; an unbalanced entry spoils every report after it.

## In the end

Voucher entry is the first brick of all accounting. Identify the four types, decide Dr/Cr by "did money come in or go out", use compound when needed, write a clear narration in every entry, and if there is a mistake, cancel — do not delete. If you take care of this much, all your books and reports will come out right on their own.
