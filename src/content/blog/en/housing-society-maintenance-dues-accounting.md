# How to keep accounts of maintenance and dues in a housing cooperative society?

For example, suppose the secretary of a cooperative housing society of 120 flats opens the books at year-end. One member says, "I already paid the maintenance for the last three months," while the register shows ₹12,000 outstanding against his name. On the other side, a flat has been sold and the new owner says, "Why should I pay the old owner's dues?" And the auditor asks, "Why was the sinking fund money shown as income?"

The accounts of a housing society are different from other societies, because here money is first **demanded** and **comes in** later. Every month 120 bills are made, every member has his own running dues, some money is the society's income and some belongs to funds for the future, and if a flat with unpaid dues is sold, questions arise. This article is a map of this cycle: the charge schedule, monthly bills, collection, ageing of dues, late interest, funds, flat transfer and audit preparation.

> 📘 **In short:** A housing society's accounts run on the cycle of **demand → receivable → collection**. As soon as a bill is made, the member's **receivable** goes up (**Dr member receivable, Cr the charge-head accounts**), and when payment comes it goes down (**Dr cash/bank, Cr member receivable**). Keep a separate account for every member, split charges into **income, fund and pass-through**, make an **age-wise list** of dues, and charge interest only at the fixed rate, **on the principal dues, once**. I did not find the maintenance rate, late interest or fund percentages in the Haryana Act or Rules; these depend on the **bye-laws and the decision of the general body/committee**. The legal routes for recovering dues are in the Act (Sections 55, 102, 104), detailed below.

> ⚠️ **An important clarification:** This article is for **housing societies** registered under the Cooperative Societies Act. If your colony runs as an RWA or an apartment owners' association, a different law and its own bye-laws may apply to it, and the legal references in this article will not apply directly. First confirm your organisation's type of registration.

## Demand → receivable → collection: the basic cycle

In agricultural societies money mostly comes and goes in cash. A housing society runs on the **accrual basis**, which means an amount becomes **due** even before the money comes in:

| Stage | What happens | Entry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Demand (bill)** | At the start of the month, a bill is made for every flat | **Dr** member receivable (maintenance due) / **Cr** the account of each charge head |
| **2. Collection** | The member pays | **Dr** cash/bank / **Cr** member receivable |
| **3. Dues** | Whatever is not collected is the receivable balance | Member-wise balance |
| **4. Interest (if fixed)** | On delay, at the fixed rate | **Dr** member receivable (interest) / **Cr** interest income |

As soon as a bill is made, the society's **income** (or fund) goes up, even if the money has not come yet. That is why a correct account of dues is essential: if bills are made but not collected and no dues register is kept, the books show income but there is no money in the bank.

One thing checked first in the audit: **does the total of members' receivables match the "member receivable" account in the books?** This is the reason for keeping a separate account for every member. (Method of monthly matching: [monthly reconciliation](/blog/monthly-reconciliation-checklist-cashbook-bank-trial-balance).)

## Charge schedule: which head, in which account

Every society's monthly heads are different, and they are fixed by the bye-laws or the decision of the general body/committee. Usually these heads come:

| Head | Nature | In which account |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maintenance/service charge | **Income** | The society's income (in the Income & Expenditure account) |
| Sinking fund contribution | **Fund** | Sinking fund (liability/fund) |
| Repair fund contribution | **Fund** | Repair fund |
| Parking charge | **Income** | Parking income |
| Non-occupancy (rented/not self-occupied) charge | **Income** | Non-occupancy charge income |
| Property tax, water, electricity (common) | **Pass-through** | Liability, to be deposited onward |

The difference between the three natures is important:

- **Income:** the society's own earning, which goes to the Income & Expenditure account.
- **Fund:** money taken from members for some future need (repairs, big expense). This is not the society's income; it is the **corpus of the fund**.
- **Pass-through:** money the society takes from the member and passes on to some authority (like property tax); it is not the society's income, it is a **liability**.

**How the charge is worked out:** in many societies the rate is **fixed per flat**, in many **per square foot**. If a head does not apply to a flat (for example, to someone who has not taken parking), keep a way to make that head zero for that flat.

A hypothetical bill *(all amounts only to explain)*:

| Head | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Maintenance (service charge) | ₹3,000 |
| Sinking fund contribution | ₹500 |
| Repair fund contribution | ₹300 |
| Parking | ₹200 |
| **Total bill** | **₹4,000** |

Its entry:

| Account | Dr | Cr |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Member receivable (of this flat's owner) | ₹4,000 | — |
| Maintenance income | — | ₹3,000 |
| Sinking fund | — | ₹500 |
| Repair fund | — | ₹300 |
| Parking income | — | ₹200 |
| **Total** | **₹4,000** | **₹4,000** |

**Crediting the fund contribution directly to the fund**, not to income, is an accounting policy considered correct, but some societies first take the contribution to income and later transfer it to the fund. The results of these two methods are different (the surplus in the Income & Expenditure account), so **fix one policy after asking your auditor/CA and keep it the same every year.**

## Monthly bills and collection

**The order every month:**

- [ ] **Bill date and last date** as per the bye-laws/committee decision; members should know in advance.
- [ ] **Bills for all eligible flats** made together; check no flat is missed, especially a new or vacant flat.
- [ ] **Copy of the bill to the member:** with head-wise amounts, previous dues and total payable.
- [ ] **Collection receipt** on the same day, serially numbered; mode of payment (cash/bank/cheque/UPI) and reference number.
- [ ] **Part payment:** clear against which bill it was adjusted. Keep a fixed policy on old dues first or the current bill first.
- [ ] **Advance payment:** what a member pays in a lump sum for future months is a **liability (advance maintenance)**, not income; adjust it when the bill is made.
- [ ] **Bounced cheque:** reverse the receipt, levy the charge (if the bye-laws allow) and put the dues back.

**Collection entry** *(on full payment of the above bill)*:

| Account | Dr | Cr |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bank | ₹4,000 | — |
| Member receivable (of that flat) | — | ₹4,000 |

When payment comes, signing the receipt and handing it to the member is your job.

## Dues: age-wise list and late interest

The older the dues, the harder the recovery. So make an **age-wise list** every month:

| Age | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 0–30 days | Recent, just a simple reminder for now |
| 31–60 days | Written reminder |
| 61–90 days | Committee discussion, **demand notice** |
| Above 90 days | Serious dues: managing committee decision and legal steps |

These categories are **accounting practice**; your bye-laws and the committee's decision should fix the ladder of action.

**Late interest:** many societies charge interest or a penalty on dues after a fixed period. **The rate and period are the bye-laws' or the general body/committee's decision**; I did not find any such fixed rate in the Act or Rules. The straightforward method of calculation, **simple interest, only on the principal dues**:

> **Interest = principal dues × annual rate × (days ÷ 365)**

A hypothetical example: a bill of ₹4,000 paid 45 days late, fixed rate 12% per year:
4,000 × 12% × 45 ÷ 365 = **₹59.18**.

**Rules of interest:**

- [ ] **Only on the principal dues,** no interest on interest (unless the bye-laws clearly allow it).
- [ ] **Interest for one period only once:** when the calculation is run again, the same period should not be charged twice. Each time, keep track of the date "interest charged up to here".
- [ ] **Separate interest entry:** Dr member receivable (interest), Cr interest income, so that principal and interest show separately in the member's statement.
- [ ] **The rate should be told to members in advance:** interest without notice becomes a dispute.

> 💡 **For the secretary/accountant:** SahakarLekha's **Outstanding Register** shows the list of dues by age of 0–30, 31–60, 61–90 and 90+ days. On the same page you can enter an annual rate and **Accrue interest**: this is simple interest on the principal dues, and the same period is not charged again (the calculation runs only from the date of the interest charged earlier). By choosing a member, a **Demand Notice (PDF)** is also made, which has the outstanding bills and the details so far. What the rate should be and from when it applies is the committee's decision, not the portal's.

## Recovery of dues: what the law gives

When reminders and the demand notice do not work, the Haryana Act 1984 has these routes. This information is general, not legal advice on any case:

| Route | Source | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Charge and set-off on shares, deposits and dividend payable** | Section 55 | The society has a charge on the member's **share, interest in capital, deposits and dividend, bonus or profit payable on them** for his dues, and it may adjust the dues from them |
| **The dispute route: Registrar's arbitration** | Section 102(1), 102(2)(a) | A society's **claim against a member for any debt or demand due**, whether admitted or not, is treated as a "dispute touching the management or business", which is referred to the **Registrar's arbitration**, and no court can hear it |
| **Recovery certificate** | Section 104 | The society may give an **application** to the Registrar for recovery of dues from members/past members, attaching a **statement of accounts**; after a hearing the Registrar may issue a recovery **certificate**, which is **final and conclusive proof** of the dues and is recovered as per Section 110 |

**The order in practice:** reminder → written demand notice (with date and proof of receipt) → managing committee resolution → where applicable, adjustment from share/deposit/dividend under Section 55 → application under Section 104 or arbitration under Section 102. **Confirm which route to take with your Registrar/ARCS or legal adviser,** because the facts of every case are different, and I cannot say whether every maintenance due will come under these sections or not.

**One caution:** whether harsh steps like stopping a service such as water, electricity or lift, or publicly pasting a list of defaulters, are lawful in themselves — I did not find any direct provision on this in the Act. Do not adopt them without the bye-laws and legal advice. (Full picture of arbitration and dispute settlement: [where to go for a dispute in a society](/blog/cooperative-society-dispute-resolution-haryana).)

## Flat transfer and dues

When a flat is sold, most disputes are about dues. Keep a clear policy:

- [ ] **No-dues before transfer:** no transfer without paying the outstanding maintenance and interest. This is a matter of bye-laws/practice; I did not find it written separately in the law.
- [ ] **Resolution:** transfer by a resolution of the managing committee (or general body), with resolution number and date.
- [ ] **Transfer fee and premium:** a separate entry for whatever is taken as per the bye-laws. Usually the **fee goes to the society's income** and the **premium to a fund**, but this depends on the bye-laws.
- [ ] **Sale, nominee or legal heir:** the basis of transfer recorded; in the case of a deceased member, the nominee's record.
- [ ] **The new owner should get the next bill in his own name;** the old owner's dues are settled in his own account.
- [ ] **Membership and shares:** the new owner becomes a member, transfer of shares. (Member shares: [member and share register](/blog/member-and-share-accounting).)

> 💡 **For the secretary:** SahakarLekha's **Transfer Register** records the transfer of flat ownership (sale/nominee/legal heir, with resolution number and date) and **stops the transfer if dues are outstanding**; on transfer it posts the fee to an income account and the premium to a fund, and the next bill is made in the new owner's name. The committee should decide the no-dues rule and the amount of the fee.

## Funds: sinking and repair

The sinking and repair funds are the most sensitive part of the society's money, because members' trust rests on this: "our fund money is safe and will be used only for the work it was taken for."

- [ ] **A separate account for each fund:** sinking, repair and others, not mixed with each other.
- [ ] **Investment of the fund:** in an FDR or bank, with an identity separate from the bank account; a list of maturity dates.
- [ ] **Interest on the investment** should go to the fund (as the bye-laws say), not to the society's general income.
- [ ] **Use of the fund only by a resolution of the managing committee/general body**, for that same purpose.
- [ ] **Annual statement of the fund:** opening balance, contributions, interest, use, closing balance, which should match the fund account in the Trial Balance.

**A legal limit, which applies to the reserve fund:** as per Section 86, a fund built from the society's profit **cannot be distributed** among members as bonus or dividend. Funds of *contributions taken from members*, like sinking or repair, and reserves built from profit are different things, but never treat these too as "distributable profit". (Reserve fund and profit distribution: [distribution of profit](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves).)

> 💡 **For the accountant:** SahakarLekha's **Fund Statement** shows each fund's corpus, contributions, interest and use, and its closing balance matches the fund account in the Trial Balance; crediting interest to the fund or recording its use is done from the same page, and there is also a separate entry for **FDR/investment** in the fund. Where the fund is invested and when it is used is the committee's decision.

Giving every member his **statement of account**, on request or every quarter, is the most direct tool of transparency: which bill was made when, how much was paid when, how much interest was charged, and how much is due now. This is the best cure for the "I already paid" dispute.

- [ ] **Monthly bill register,** a copy of every bill, and collection receipts.
- [ ] **Age-wise list of dues** (for every month), demand notices and proof of their service.
- [ ] **Interest calculation sheet** and the resolution fixing the rate.
- [ ] **Member-wise statements of account,** and the total of dues matched with the "member receivable" account in the books.
- [ ] **Fund statement,** list of investments (FDR) and resolutions for use of funds.
- [ ] **Transfer register:** resolution, no-dues and the entry of fee/premium.

## A short hypothetical example

*(Hypothetical: not of any real society.)* Suppose in a society bills are made every month, but there is no separate member-wise register of collection; all the money is posted to "maintenance income". The sinking fund contribution also went to income, and the interest rate is not recorded in any resolution. At year-end it turns out that the total "income" is ₹3.4 lakh more than the money that came into the bank, because that is dues, and nobody knows which member has not been paying for how many months.

If the same society had posted every bill to the member's separate account, made an age-wise list of dues every month and passed a resolution on the interest rate, the same ₹3.4 lakh would have been caught month by month, and every defaulter would have got a chance to talk at the first reminder.

## Common mistakes

1. **Not keeping a member-wise register of receivables.** The total of dues does not match the books.
2. **Posting fund contributions to income** and then forgetting to account for them.
3. **Charging interest or penalty without a resolution.** The most common root of disputes.
4. **Interest on interest, or interest twice for the same period.**
5. **Making the bill but not giving a copy to the member.**
6. **Not giving a collection receipt** or posting it late, which creates the "I already paid" dispute.
7. **Treating advance payment as income.** It is a liability.
8. **Allowing transfer without no-dues.** The dues become a clash with the new owner.
9. **Harsh steps against a defaulter** (stopping a service, pasting names) **without legal advice.**
10. **Treating the rules of an RWA/apartment association as those of the Cooperative Act.** First check the organisation's type of registration.

## Three things to remember

- 🏠 A housing society's accounts run on demand → receivable → collection: as soon as a bill is made the member's receivable goes up, and on payment it goes down; keep a separate account for every member and match its total with the books.
- 💰 Split charges into income, fund and pass-through; fund money is not the society's income; interest on the principal dues, at the fixed rate and once for one period, and the rate recorded in a resolution.
- ⚖️ For dues the Act has Section 55 (charge on share/deposit/dividend), 102 (arbitration) and 104 (recovery certificate); the maintenance rate, interest and fund percentage depend on the bye-laws, and a different law may apply to an RWA/apartment association.

## Frequently asked questions

**How to keep maintenance accounts in a housing cooperative society?** — Make bills for all flats every month (Dr member receivable, Cr the account of each charge head), reduce the receivable when payment comes (Dr cash/bank, Cr member receivable), keep a separate account for every member and make an age-wise list of dues every month.
**Is the sinking fund contribution income or a fund?** — The contribution is money taken from members for a future need, that is, a fund. Many societies credit it directly to the fund; some first take it to income and then transfer it. Fix one policy after asking your auditor and keep it the same every year.
**How much interest can be charged on delay?** — I did not find any such fixed rate in the Act or Rules. The rate and period depend on the bye-laws or the general body/committee's decision. Calculate with simple interest: principal dues × annual rate × (days ÷ 365).
**Can interest be charged on interest?** — Usually not; charge interest on the principal dues, unless the bye-laws clearly allow it. And interest for one period should be charged only once.
**How to recover from a defaulter?** — Reminder, written demand notice and managing committee resolution; then the legal routes: Section 55 (adjustment from share/deposit/dividend payable), Section 104 (recovery application to the Registrar) or Section 102 (Registrar's arbitration). Confirm which route with your Registrar or legal adviser.
**If a flat is sold, who pays the old dues?** — The usual policy is that no-dues is taken before transfer. This is a matter of bye-laws/practice; I did not find it separately in the law. The new owner should get the next bill in his own name.
**Is advance maintenance income?** — No. A lump sum for future months is a liability (advance), which is adjusted when each month's bill is made.
**Does this article also apply to an RWA or apartment association?** — No. It is for housing societies registered under the Cooperative Societies Act. A different law and its own bye-laws may apply to an RWA or apartment owners' association.
**What is checked for a housing society in the audit?** — As per Section 95(3) the auditor has access to the books and vouchers. In practice the bill register, collection receipts, matching of member receivables with the books, list of dues, the basis of interest (resolution), fund statement and transfer record are checked.
**What if there is an instruction of a prescribed portal or higher body?** — Then follow that. This article is for your understanding and your own records, not in its place.

## In the end

The trust of a housing society rests on one question of every member: "Where did my money go and how much do I still owe?" In a society that has a separate account for every member, a monthly age-wise list of dues and a clear resolution on interest, this question is settled by a document, not by argument. And a secretary who has a clear statement of funds does not have to search for the answer to "where is this money" in the audit.
