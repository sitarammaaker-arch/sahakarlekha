# Audit Classification (A/B/C/D): What a Cooperative Society's Grading Is

At the end of the audit the society gets a **grade** — A, B, C or D. This grade is the "report card" of the society's health. This article explains what this classification is, what decides it, why it matters, and how to improve your society's grade — all in simple language.

## What audit classification is
After every annual audit the auditor gives the society a category based on its **financial health, compliance and management**. Generally:

| Grade | Broad meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **A** | Very good — strong, rule-abiding, few objections |
| **B** | Good — some room for improvement |
| **C** | Weak — many shortcomings/objections |
| **D** | Very weak — serious problems |

> ⚠️ Note: the exact grade names and criteria change according to the **state's cooperative Act/Rules** — some states may even use different letters/numbers. In this article A/B/C/D is for general understanding; check the exact system that applies to your society in your state's rules/audit report.

This grade is not just a letter — it is the essence of the whole year's working, which shows the society's health at a glance. Just as a school marksheet shows how a student did over the year, the audit grade shows how the society handled its money, records and rule compliance. So it should be taken seriously — it is the public measure of the society's standing.

## What decides the grade
- **Financial position** — profit/loss, capital, reserve fund and overall strength.
- **Recovery** — how low the overdues/NPA are, how high the recovery percentage is.
- **Compliance** — audit, returns, rectification of objections, general body and elections on time.
- **Records** — the state of the books, bank reconciliation, documents and member register.

### Each criterion in a little more depth

- **Financial position:** Is the society in profit or in loss? Are the capital and reserve fund strong? Continuous losses or falling capital pull the grade down, while steady profit and growing reserves take it up. (See: [How reports are made — basics of cooperative accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)
- **Recovery:** For societies that give loans this is the most important — how much of the loans was recovered on time, how much became overdue/NPA. High recovery = a healthy society. (See: [Loan recovery](/blog/loan-recovery-before-year-end).)
- **Compliance:** Were the annual audit, returns, general body and elections held on time? Were previous objections settled? Timely compliance is a big part of the grade.
- **Records:** Are the books up to date and matching? Is bank reconciliation regular? Are documents complete? Clean records directly give a better grade. (See: [Bank reconciliation (BRS)](/blog/bank-reconciliation-guide).)

Look at these four together and the grade is no longer a "mystery" — it is a direct reflection of how much discipline the society worked with all year.

## How the grade is decided — the process

The grade is not given suddenly or arbitrarily — it is the natural last stage of the audit process. Broadly: the auditor checks the whole year's books, vouchers, bank reconciliation, recovery and compliance (see: [How a cooperative audit works](/blog/how-cooperative-society-audit-works)); looks at the society's position on each aspect; and combines all of these to decide an overall category. That is, the grade rests not on one thing but on a balance of several factors — good performance in one area can balance weakness in another to some extent, but serious shortcomings (like a big embezzlement or completely mismatched books) drag the grade down.

That is why it is most accurate to see the grade as "the average performance of the whole year" — it is the result not of one day or one transaction, but of the whole year's discipline.

## What the grade means for a member

The audit grade matters for an ordinary member too — it tells him at a glance how healthy and reliable his society is. A good grade means his money (shares, deposits) is in safe hands and the society is running as per rules. A weak grade is a warning — the member has the right to ask questions in the general body, find out the reasons and demand improvement. So members too, not just office-bearers, should pay attention to the audit report and grade. This alert membership also puts pressure on the society to get better.

## Why the grade matters
- **Standing and trust** — a good grade raises the trust of members, banks and the federation.
- **Finance/support** — a society with a high grade gets loans, refinance and scheme benefits more easily.
- **Supervision** — extra supervision by the RCS on a weak grade.
- **Motivation** — the grade gives a clear target, which motivates office-bearers to do better.

Its practical effect is big. A society with an A/B grade easily gets loans, refinance and the benefit of government schemes from the bank/federation, and members stay at ease. On the other hand, a society with a C/D grade stays under the Registrar's close watch, new facilities are hard to get, and members' trust wavers. So the grade is not just on paper — it also shapes the society's future opportunities.

> 💡 The way to improve the grade is straightforward: **clean books, timely recovery and compliance, and quick rectification of objections.**

## Common reasons for a weak grade

If a society's grade falls, it is often for some of these reasons:

- Continuous losses or falling capital.
- High overdues/NPA, slow recovery.
- Late/incomplete audit, returns and rectification of objections.
- Mismatched books, bank reconciliation not done, missing documents.
- Irregular meetings/elections.
- Weak internal control, leaving room for irregularities.
- Mismatch between member records (shares/deposits) and the books.

The good news is that all of these can be fixed — and in the next audit, improvement in these turns directly into a better grade. The point to note is that most weak-grade reasons come not from "not being able to" but from "putting off" — reconciliation put off, recovery put off, rectification of objections put off. So the biggest weapon for improving the grade is discipline and timeliness, not any special expertise.

## Plan to improve the grade — step by step

1. **Settle previous objections:** Rectify every objection in the audit report on time, with documents — this has the most direct effect on the next grade.
2. **Clean up the records:** Monthly Trial Balance and bank reconciliation; remove mismatched balances and suspense at the root, do not put it off.
3. **Increase recovery:** Regular review of defaulters, timely recovery efforts and reducing NPA — for credit societies this is the heaviest factor in the grade.
4. **Compliance on time:** Complete the audit, annual return, general body and elections all within the set time limits.
5. **Strengthen internal control:** Maker–Checker, division of duties, regular reconciliation. (See: [Internal control](/blog/internal-control-in-cooperatives).)
6. **Keep documents complete:** A bill/approval and clear narration for every big transaction, so every figure is proven in the audit.

This plan is not completed in one year — but every step takes the next grade a little higher. So improving the grade is a continuous process, not a year-end task.

## The practical face of each grade

Do not see the grade as just a letter; see what the society's picture looks like at each level:

- **A (very good):** Books matching, bank reconciliation regular, recovery high, no serious objection, all compliance on time. This is the ideal position, where the audit passes almost smoothly.
- **B (good):** Healthy overall, but some small shortcomings — like one or two minor objections or a slightly late return. With a little improvement, A is possible.
- **C (weak):** Many shortcomings together — mismatched balances, high overdues, unresolved objections. A serious improvement plan is needed here.
- **D (very weak):** Serious problems — big loss, heavy overdues, missing records or signs of irregularity. This can even lead to strict action by the Registrar.

Looking at this "face", the society can judge for itself where it stands — and what its next step should be.

## Time taken to improve the grade

One important point — the grade does not change overnight. Improvements show in the next audit period, so a society that starts improving today gets the result in the next grading. That is why improvement should not be put off till year-end — doing a little every month (reconciliation, recovery, compliance) is the fastest way. Patience and consistency — these two are the real key to improving the grade.

## An example — from C to B

Suppose a society got a C grade this year, because (a) bank reconciliation had not been done for months, (b) overdues were high, and (c) the previous two objections were unresolved. If next year it does a BRS every month, increases recovery from defaulters, and settles both objections with documents — then all three improvements will show in the next audit, and the grade can go from C to B (or better). This shows that the grade is something that "can be changed", not permanent.

## Better grade from clean books
A matching Trial Balance, timely bank reconciliation, low overdues and complete reports — these directly improve the grade. This is where cooperative-specific accounting software helps the most — because reports automatically match each other, documents stay linked to vouchers, and the audit trail stays ready, which makes the audit smooth and the grade better. ## Frequently asked questions
**What is audit classification?** — The grade (A/B/C/D type) a society gets after the audit on its financial health/compliance.
**What decides the grade?** — Financial position, recovery, compliance and records.
**How can the grade be improved?** — Clean books, timely recovery/compliance, rectification of objections and strong internal control.
**Are grade names the same in every state?** — No; they change as per state rules (letters/numbers can be different).
**What is the effect of a weak grade?** — Extra supervision by the RCS, difficulty in loans/scheme benefits, effect on members' trust.
**What is the benefit of a good grade?** — Standing, easier loans and refinance, scheme benefits and members' trust.
**Can the grade change in one year?** — Yes; if improvements show by the next audit, the grade can go up.
**What is the biggest factor in improving the grade?** — No single one; but recovery + clean records + timely compliance together make the biggest difference.
**Who decides the grade?** — The auditor, after checking the whole year's books, recovery, compliance and records.
**Should a member know the grade?** — Yes; it tells how safe his money and the society are; on a weak grade, ask questions in the general body.
**When will the improvement show?** — In the next audit period; so start improving now, do not put it off till year-end.
**What does a D grade mean?** — Serious problems; this can lead to close supervision/even action by the Registrar.

## Conclusion

Audit classification is the "report card" of the society's health — the essence of its financial position, recovery, compliance and records. A good grade brings standing, loans and trust; a weak grade brings supervision and difficulty. But the best part is that the grade is not permanent — with clean books every day, timely recovery and compliance, quick rectification of objections and strong internal control, it can be improved year after year. So see the grade not as something to fear but as a signal that shows the direction — and move one step better every year.
