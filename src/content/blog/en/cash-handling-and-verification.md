# Cash Handling and Verification: Keeping Cash Accounts Right

Cash is where the most mistakes and audit objections happen. Cash moves from hand to hand, it has no record that builds itself (like a bank statement), and so this is where there is the most room for slips and leakage. But with a little disciplined habit, the society's cash can always stay right. This article explains the correct handling, verification and control of cash in detail, in simple language.

## Cash and the cash book

Cash means the actual cash (notes and coins) the society holds. In the **cash book** all cash receipts and payments are recorded date-wise, and its balance should equal the **cash-in-hand**. That is, the cash book is not a separate account — it is the mirror of the actual cash lying in the safe/drawer.

> 💡 The golden rule — the cash book balance should equal the cash you can physically count. If the two are not equal, something is wrong somewhere.

The cash book is different from the bank book — the cash book keeps the account of physical cash only, while the bank book keeps the account of the bank account. Mixing the two up is a common mistake. (See: [Cash book vs bank book](/blog/cash-book-vs-bank-book).)

## Verification (Cash Verification)

Counting the physical cash from time to time and matching it with the cash book balance is **cash verification**. This is a common and important audit check — the auditor often comes without notice, has the cash counted and matches it with the book.

> ⚠️ If the book balance and the actual cash differ — check immediately. It is a sign of a mistake or leakage, which should not be put off.

### How to do cash verification — steps

1. **Take out the book balance:** at the time of verification, see the current balance of the cash book.
2. **Count the physical cash:** count all the cash in the safe/drawer — preferably two people together.
3. **Match the two:** book balance = counted cash? If yes, all is fine.
4. **If there is a difference, investigate:** was an entry missed? Is a receipt/bill still to be recorded? An addition mistake somewhere?
5. **Record it:** write the verification date, the amount counted and signatures in a register.

With regular (daily or weekly) verification any difference is caught while it is still small — before it grows big months later.

## Denomination sheet (note-count slip)

When counting a large amount of cash it is a good habit to make a **denomination sheet** — how many ₹500 notes, how many ₹200, how many ₹100… This keeps the counting organised and makes it easy to match the total. This slip becomes part of the verification record and is also useful in the audit.

## What to do if you find a difference (over/short)

There can be two kinds of differences in verification:

- **Cash shortage:** less cash than the book — this is serious; find the reason immediately (a payment not recorded, an addition mistake, or leakage).
- **Cash excess:** more cash than the book — this is also a mistake (some receipt was not recorded); find it and record it.

Do not ignore any difference by saying "it is small, it will do". Find and correct the root of every difference, and report large/repeated discrepancies to the managing committee.

## Two important cautions

| Point | Correct understanding |
| --- | --- |
| Negative cash | Impossible — cash is never negative; it is a mistake |
| Cash vs bank | Cash = physical cash; bank is separate (in the bank book) |

**Why negative cash is impossible:** you can pay out only as much cash as you have; you cannot pay what is not in the safe. So if on some day the cash balance in the book shows negative, it is a sure sign that either some receipt was missed, or some payment was entered on the wrong date/with the wrong amount. Correct it immediately.

## Good cash-control habits

- **Record every cash transaction in the cash book immediately** — do not put it off for later; it is easy to forget.
- **A bill/receipt with every payment/receipt** — do not give or take cash without a document.
- **Regular cash verification** — daily/weekly.
- **Deposit cash in the bank when it builds up** — do not keep a pile in the safe; make a contra entry when depositing.
- **Safety of the safe** — large cash in a safe/locker; access for limited people.

> 🔍 Maker-checker and limited access make cash handling even safer. (See: [Internal control in societies](/blog/internal-control-in-cooperatives).)

## Internal control — special for cash

Some controls on cash should be in every society, however small it may be:

- **Segregation of duties (segregation):** the person who handles the cash should not alone write the book and do the verification — two different people are better.
- **Maker–Checker:** one person makes the entry, another checks/approves it.
- **Cash limit:** do not keep more than a set limit of cash in the safe; deposit the extra in the bank.
- **Surprise verification:** now and then a sudden count without notice, so that everyone stays alert.
- **Access control:** the key/access to the safe limited to a few persons.

These controls are not distrust of anyone, but protection for everyone — they guard against both mistakes and suspicion.

## Petty cash (cash for small expenses)

For small day-to-day expenses (tea, stationery, auto fare) a small **petty cash** is kept — usually a fixed amount (imprest) is given, and as it is spent, its account is given and the same amount is filled up again. Keep a separate account and bills for petty cash too, otherwise small expenses add up to a large unaccounted amount.

## An example — catching a difference in verification

Suppose on Friday evening the secretary does a cash verification. The cash book balance shows ₹18,500, but on counting the safe ₹18,000 comes out — that is, ₹500 short. Instead of panicking, he checks in order: was some payment left unrecorded? Yes — in the afternoon a stationery payment of ₹500 was made in cash, but it was not recorded in the book. He records that payment (with the bill), and now the cash book shows ₹18,000 — equal to the actual cash. The difference is resolved. This is the strength of regular verification — the small slip was caught the same day, otherwise it would have stayed tangled for months and become a big objection in the audit.

## Discipline of depositing cash in the bank

Keeping more cash than needed in the safe is a risk — theft, discrepancies, and money lying idle without interest. So regularly deposit cash above a set limit in the bank. When cash is deposited in the bank, it is a **contra entry** — money went from one account (cash) to another (bank), and the society's total assets stayed the same. It is necessary to record this correctly, otherwise the cash or bank balance will show wrong. Keep the copy of the pay-in slip, so there is proof of the deposit. (See: [Cash book vs bank book](/blog/cash-book-vs-bank-book).)

## Warning signs (red flags)

Some things indicate that there may be a problem in cash handling, and they should be taken seriously:

- Differences coming up again and again in verification (sometimes short, sometimes excess).
- Transactions recorded late or all together (in a batch).
- Cash payments without a bill/receipt.
- Large cash lying in the safe for a long time.
- One person handling the cash, writing the book and doing the verification.
- Too many corrections/cuttings in the cash book.

If any of these shows up, alert the managing committee and tighten the controls — this is not an accusation against anyone, but protection.

## Cash accounts and the audit

In the audit, cash is the first part to be checked, because the risk is highest here. The auditor usually: matches the cash book balance with the actual cash, looks at the cash limit and deposit discipline, checks the bills for large cash payments, and looks at the verification register. In a society where verification is regular, every payment has a bill, and cash is deposited in the bank on time — its cash audit stays smooth and free of objections. So these habits are not just for good management, but also for an easy audit.

## Common mistakes

- **Recording transactions later** → the root of slips and differences; record immediately.
- **Giving/taking cash without a bill** → accounts are weak without documents.
- **Never doing verification** → a difference stays hidden for months.
- **Ignoring a difference** → a small discrepancy becomes a big one.
- **A pile of cash in the safe** → risk; deposit in the bank on time.
- **Everything on one person** → segregation of duties is necessary.

## How a digital system makes cash accounts easier

A digital cash book simplifies cash management in many ways: the balance is worked out automatically with every entry (no room for addition mistakes); an impossible situation like a negative balance is caught immediately; at verification time the book balance is available in one click; and the record of every transaction stays safe. Besides this, accounts like contra entries (cash→bank deposit) and petty cash also stay organised. That is, the same discipline that needed effort in a hand-written register stays in place almost automatically in a digital system — the secretary just has to make entries on time.

## Practical cash control in a small society

Big control frameworks can seem hard to apply in a small society — where perhaps one person does most of the work. But even there a few simple steps make a big difference: at the time of cash verification keep another member (such as the treasurer or a managing committee member) with you; the consent of one extra member on every large payment; and regularly place a report of the cash position in the managing committee meeting. This way, even without a big framework, the principle "two eyes are better than one" is applied — and this is the backbone of cash safety in a small society. (See: [MIS reports](/blog/mis-reports-for-management).)

## Frequently asked questions

**What is cash-in-hand?** — The physical cash present at any time, which should equal the cash book balance.
**What is cash verification?** — Counting the actual cash and matching it with the book balance; a common and important audit check.
**Can the cash balance be negative?** — No; a negative balance means some receipt was missed or a payment was recorded wrongly — it is a mistake.
**How often should verification be done?** — Regularly (daily/weekly); the more often, the sooner a difference is caught.
**What to do if a difference is found?** — Find the reason (missed entry/addition mistake/leakage), correct it, and report large/repeated discrepancies to the managing committee.
**How to keep cash safe?** — Safe/locker, limited access, cash limit, and timely deposit in the bank.
**What is petty cash?** — A fixed amount for small day-to-day expenses, for which a separate account and bills are kept.
**Why is depositing cash in the bank necessary?** — A pile of cash in the safe is a risk; deposit cash above the set limit in the bank (with a contra entry).
**What entry is depositing cash in the bank?** — A contra entry — money went from cash to the bank account; total assets stayed the same.
**What if one person handles everything in a small society?** — Keep another member with you during verification, take extra consent on large payments — "two eyes are better than one".

Cash management is no difficult science — it is the sum of small, disciplined daily habits. A society that adopts these habits never gets its cash tangled, never gets its audit stuck, and always keeps its members' trust. So start these habits today, right now — this is the most valuable and the cheapest protection.

## Three things to remember
- 💵 Cash book balance = actual counted cash.
- 🔍 Regular verification — check and correct differences immediately.
- 🚫 Negative cash = a sign of a mistake; find the root immediately.

## In the end
Disciplined handling of cash and regular verification are what protect the society from cash discrepancies and audit objections. The rules are simple — record every transaction immediately, keep a bill with every payment, verify regularly, never ignore a difference, and keep proper control over cash handling. These habits do not demand any expertise — only discipline. And through them the riskiest part of the society — cash — becomes the most reliable.
