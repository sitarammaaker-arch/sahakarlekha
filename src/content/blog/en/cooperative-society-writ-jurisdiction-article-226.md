One belief comes up often: since a cooperative society is registered under a cooperative Act, the Registrar supervises it, and it is often linked to government schemes, it must be a "government body" in some form — and so a writ petition can be filed against it directly in the High Court. A recent judgment of the Punjab & Haryana High Court has tested this belief against the law again, and the result is not as simple as it looks.

## What the case was about

> ⚠️ **Information checked up to 18 October 2026.** All the judgments below have been matched against their text (see "Sources" below).

In *Multan Singh v. State of Haryana and Others* (CWP-7283-2023), an employee filed a petition against The Pundri Cooperative Marketing-cum-Processing Society Ltd. His retirement dues were held up from 31 July 2015 to 13 February 2018, and he asked for 12% interest for this delay. At the very start, the society raised a threshold objection: can the High Court hear a writ against this society only because it is registered under the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act?

On 30 January 2026, Justice Harpreet Singh Brar dismissed the petition on this very ground — the petitioner could not prove that the society was an "instrumentality" of the government, or that it was performing any public/statutory duty that would attract writ jurisdiction.

> ⚠️ Understand one important point clearly here: the court gave no opinion on the merits of the interest claim — the petition was dismissed only on the ground of maintainability. That is, the employee's claim was not held to be wrong; it was only decided that a High Court writ was not the right forum for it.

## Two words first — what are a writ and Article 226

Two basic points before we go further. A **writ** is a powerful order of the High Court/Supreme Court that tells someone to do or not do something (for example mandamus — an order to perform a duty). **Article 226** is the provision of the Constitution that gives the High Court the power to issue such writs — mainly against the "State" or bodies performing public duties. The question is whether a cooperative society falls within this scope — and the answer, as we will see below, is "not always".

## Registration alone does not make a "government body"

This judgment rests on settled principles of the Supreme Court. In *Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology* (2002) 5 SCC 111, a seven-judge bench held that to treat a body as the "State" under Article 12, one must look not at its legal form but at whether the government has **financial, functional and administrative domination** over it — and this control must be body-specific and pervasive, going far beyond ordinary regulatory supervision.

The same reasoning was applied to a cooperative sugar mill in *General Manager, Kisan Sahkari Chini Mills Ltd. v. Satrughan Nishad* (2003) 8 SCC 639 — there, even though the state government held 50% of the shares and 5 of the 15 committee members were government nominees, the Supreme Court held that the government did not have deep and pervasive control over the mill, so it was not the “State”. The real questions are: where does the funding come from, how real is the administrative control, and what is the nature of the business.

The Punjab & Haryana High Court itself accepted this principle about 45 years ago in *Pritam Singh Gill v. State of Punjab* (AIR 1982 P&H 228; decided 26 August 1981). There the Full Bench held that a registered cooperative society that is in fact an instrumentality of the government is open to writ just like the government — but the society in that case (a Mortgage Bank) was not held to be an instrumentality, and the petition was dismissed. That is, registration is neither a shield against a writ nor a ground for one; everything depends on the facts.

## Article 12 and Article 226 are not the same thing

One distinction must be understood here. Article 12 decides whether a body will be treated as the "State" in the context of fundamental rights. But the wording of Article 226 is wider — in some situations, the High Court can issue mandamus even against a private body that is not the "State" under Article 12, provided it is performing some public/statutory duty and the action being challenged is directly connected with that duty.

In *St. Mary's Education Society v. Rajendra Prasad Bhargava* (24 August 2022; Justice Aniruddha Bose and Justice J.B. Pardiwala), the Supreme Court made it clear that a private institution may perform public functions, but this does not mean Article 226 will apply to every internal decision it takes — a purely private contractual dispute (with no statutory backing) usually does not fall within writ jurisdiction.

## When the result could be different

The Multan Singh judgment does not lay down a blanket rule that "all cooperative societies of Haryana are now outside writ jurisdiction". The court only repeated the settled test — every body will be judged on its factual character. In some other body, if the government bears substantial expenditure, the key posts on the board are controlled by government nominees, major financial/administrative decisions depend on government approval, or the body performs a monopoly-like statutory role — then the result could be different.

> 📘 The court clearly wrote that the society's argument — that the government has no control over its financial/administrative affairs — remained “uncontroverted” by the petitioner, that is, it was not rebutted. So it is not enough just to write that "the society is under government control" — this has to be proved with documents.

## "Control" vs "supervision" — this is the real test

The common thread in all these judgments is a fine difference — **control** vs **supervision**. The Registrar getting the audit done, approving the bye-laws or holding elections is "supervision" — it applies to every cooperative society, but it does not make the society "government". "Control" is found when the government has deep, pervasive involvement in the society's day-to-day management, finances and decisions. Since most societies are managed by a managing committee elected by their members, they stay within "supervision", not "control" — and that is why they usually do not fall within writ jurisdiction. (The same principle applies to RTI too — see: [RTI and cooperative societies](/blog/rti-and-cooperative-societies).)

## What this means in practice for employees

If an employee's service conditions are not governed by any statutory provision and the employer society is not the "State" under Article 12, then taking a dispute about only salary, retirement dues, promotion or the service contract to Article 226 may face a maintainability objection. This does not mean the employee has no remedy — the available route will depend on the nature of the dispute: the applicable service rules, the employment contract, labour laws, or some special statutory mechanism.

There is an important fine point here: **Section 102** of the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act generally sends disputes about the constitution, management or business of a society to the Registrar's arbitration mechanism (see: [Where to take disputes in a society](/blog/cooperative-society-dispute-resolution-haryana)), but this same provision **expressly excludes disciplinary action and service matters of a paid servant**. So employees should not assume that every service dispute will go to Section 102 arbitration on its own — the right forum has to be identified first.

For members (as distinct from employees), the position is different — Section 102 brings many disputes between a member, a past member, the society and the managing committee within Registrar arbitration, so before going straight to the High Court, a member must check whether the Act already provides a special remedy for that dispute.

## The practical takeaway — what to do first

So what should an ordinary member/employee do? First, identify which category your dispute belongs to — member-related (shares/election/dues), service-related (salary/promotion), or one of constitutional rights. Then check whether the Act gives a special remedy for it (such as Section 102 arbitration) — if yes, that route has to be taken first. A direct High Court writ holds only when either the society truly is an instrumentality of the government, or it is performing a public/statutory duty to which the dispute is directly connected. So before filing a writ, do get a legal expert to check whether your case passes the test of maintainability — otherwise, even a correct claim may be dismissed only because of the wrong forum.

## Whose side this judgment does not weaken

Avoid one misunderstanding — judgments like this neither hold the member's/employee's claim to be "wrong", nor make the society "free of accountability". They only decide **which forum is the right one.** The society is still bound to fulfil its obligations towards its members/employees, and they have remedies through suitable forums (arbitration, service rules, labour laws, or the High Court in a suitable situation). That is, this is not "denial of justice" but a pointer towards the "right door". Going to the right forum gets the case heard faster and more effectively.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a writ petition be filed in the High Court against a cooperative society?** — Not always; it depends on whether the government has real financial/administrative control over it or the society is performing a public/statutory duty.
**Did the Multan Singh judgment reduce the Registrar's powers?** — No. This judgment is only about the scope of Article 12/226; the Registrar's statutory powers such as registration, audit and elections remain separate under the Act.
**Which forum is right for an employee's service dispute?** — It depends on the applicable service rules, the contract and the nature of the dispute; Section 102 arbitration does not cover service matters of a paid servant.
**Does this judgment apply to the whole country?** — No, it is a judgment of a Single Judge of the Punjab & Haryana High Court, not a final ruling of the Supreme Court — but it only applies principles that were already settled.
**What is the difference between Article 12 and Article 226?** — Article 12 defines the "State" (for fundamental rights); Article 226 is the High Court's wider writ power, which in some situations applies even to a private body with a public duty.
**What is the difference between "control" and "supervision"?** — Deep involvement in management/finances = control (writ possible); only audit/elections/bye-law approval = supervision (usually no writ).
**What should be done before filing a writ?** — Identify the category of the dispute, check the special remedy in the Act, and get maintainability checked by an expert.

## Three things to remember

- ⚖️ Registration under the Act alone does not make a society the "State" under Article 12 — the real test is the government's actual financial/administrative control.
- 🧾 A claim may be correct, yet the right forum is a separate question — maintainability and merits are two different things.
- 📂 Employee service disputes are outside Section 102 arbitration — the right route must be identified first (this is not denial of justice, it is the right door).

## In the end

The whole topic in one sentence — "the cooperative society is registered" does not mean a High Court writ will lie directly on every dispute of it. The courts have repeated the same principle again and again: the real test is not the form of the body, but the government's actual control over it or its public/statutory duty. For a member or an employee, the practical lesson is this — however correct the claim, the right forum must be identified first, otherwise the case may be dismissed without being heard on merits. So before taking legal steps, do get the nature of the dispute and the right route checked by an expert.
