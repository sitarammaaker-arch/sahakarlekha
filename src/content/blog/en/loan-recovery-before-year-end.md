# Loan recovery: the most important job before year-end

As the end of the financial year comes closer, every society that gives loans — especially a PACS — faces one big job: **recovering outstanding loans**. Good recovery decides the society's health, its grading and how much it can borrow next year. This article makes recovery systematic — why, when, how, and how to record recovery clearly.

## Why recovery matters so much

- Low recovery = **rising NPA** = falling grade.
- Money that is recovered goes back into new loans and work.
- A correct recovery picture at year-end = correct final accounts.

Let us understand this a little more. A cooperative society has limited capital. When that money goes out as loans and does not come back on time, the society has no money left to lend to new members. So one member's delay can take away another member's right. That is why recovery is not just an accounting job — it is the condition that keeps the society's whole "loan cycle" (money goes out → comes back → goes out again) alive. The faster and healthier this cycle, the stronger the society.

Besides this, the yearly **grading / audit classification** of most lending cooperative institutions depends largely on recovery performance. Weak recovery directly hurts the society's credit standing and the loan limit it gets from the upper bank (DCCB / State Cooperative Bank).

## Before recovery: pull out the right list

First look at the **age of dues (aging)** — who owes how much, and since when:

| Age | Status | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–90 days | Normal | Polite reminder |
| 90–365 days | Needs attention | Contact + instalment plan |
| 1 year+ | Risk / NPA | Act on priority |

> 💡 You get a correct aging only when every loan and instalment is recorded correctly. (For the basics, see [loan and interest accounting](/blog/loan-and-interest-accounting) and [KCC accounting](/blog/kcc-crop-loan-accounting).)

**Why the aging list is necessary:** If you only see "total outstanding ₹10 lakh", you do not know how much of it is fresh (easy to recover) and how much is old (risky). The aging list shows exactly this difference — and puts your time and effort where it is needed most. The older an old due gets, the harder it is to recover, so ignoring it is the most expensive mistake.

## Watch two indicators every week

- **Recovery%** = recovered ÷ due × 100.
- **NPA** = dues outstanding beyond the set period, classified as per the rules.

> ⚠️ The rules and periods for NPA classification depend on the institution and the rules — confirm the norms that apply to your society with the cooperative department / NABARD.

The benefit of watching these two indicators every week is that a problem is caught while it is still small. If you keep your eyes closed all year and suddenly look at year-end, many dues will already have turned into NPA by then and recovery will become hard. The discipline of "a little, every week" greatly reduces the year-end rush.

## Recovery drive — practical steps

1. Make the outstanding list age-wise.
2. Give priority to large / old dues.
3. Keep a record of instalment plans and contacts.
4. Make the **entry immediately** as soon as money is recovered — Cash/Bank Dr, Loan/Interest Cr.

Let us expand these steps a little:

- **List + priority:** First focus on the large and old dues, where a little effort can recover a big amount. At the same time, do not forget the members who want to pay but have fallen behind by oversight — for them, one polite reminder is often enough.
- **Record of contacts:** Write down who you spoke to and when, what promise was given, and what next date was fixed. This keeps follow-up organised, and in case of a dispute the society has a record.
- **Instalment plan:** For members who cannot pay in one go, a practical instalment plan is often better than the whole due being lost — but it must always stay within the society's rules and the approval of the competent authority.

## Recording recovery correctly — an example

Suppose a member owes ₹50,000 principal loan and ₹4,000 overdue interest, and he deposits ₹20,000. In the accounts:

- **Cash/Bank account — Dr ₹20,000** (money came into the society).
- The recovery is first adjusted (as per the society's rules, usually) against **overdue interest** — **Interest account — Cr ₹4,000**.
- The remaining **₹16,000 goes to the principal loan** — **Loan account — Cr ₹16,000**.

After this, the member's remaining principal loan is ₹34,000. This order (interest first, then principal) can differ from society to society — check your bye-laws / policy. The main point: **record the recovery as soon as it happens**, otherwise the outstanding list will be wrong and the aging will go wrong.

> ⚠️ The order of adjustment between interest and principal, penal interest and waiver rules depend on the society's bye-laws and the government orders that apply — confirm them with your cooperative department / bye-laws.

## If the member still does not pay

When recovery does not happen despite polite contact, reminders and an instalment plan, the society has further steps under the rules — such as a formal notice, and the recovery procedure available under the state Cooperative Societies Act (such as the arrangement for dispute settlement / recovery certificate). But the exact procedure, time limits and authority for these steps differ in each state's Cooperative Societies Act and Rules.

> ⚠️ The legal recovery procedure (notice, dispute reference, recovery certificate etc.) depends on the state's Cooperative Societies Act / Rules — before taking any legal step, confirm the exact procedure with your cooperative department / legal advice. Only general direction is given here, not legal advice.

The lesson in practice is that a legal step is the last option — it costs both time and relationships. So the real key to better recovery lies in discipline from the start: loans only to the right member, after proper assessment, and then timely follow-up.

## Before recovery — prevention

The foundation of good recovery is laid at the time the loan is given:

- **Proper assessment:** The loan amount should be fixed only after looking at the member's capacity to repay.
- **Clear terms:** Tell the member the instalments, interest rate and dates clearly at the very start.
- **Timely reminders:** Reminding before the due date is better than running after the member once he has defaulted.
- **Clean records:** Every disbursement and instalment recorded immediately — so that you get the correct outstanding picture on any day.

## Understand your members — not every defaulter is the same

The biggest wisdom in recovery is not to look at every defaulter through the same lens. Broadly, there are three kinds of members:

- **Those who forgot:** They want to pay, but forgot the date or did not get the slip. For them, one polite reminder is often enough — strictness here does harm.
- **Those in hardship:** They do not have money right now (crop failure, illness etc.). For them, a practical instalment plan, a little extra time, or a recovery date linked to the crop cycle works better.
- **Those who avoid on purpose:** They have the capacity, but not the intention. This is where firmness, a formal notice and further steps as per the rules are necessary.

Treating all three the same way either annoys the good members or lets the avoiders off. So, along with the aging list, this "understanding of members" makes recovery effective and fair.

## Grading and recovery — a deep link

In the yearly grading of most lending cooperative institutions, recovery performance is a major factor. Good recovery = better grade = higher loan limit and better terms from the upper bank (DCCB / State Cooperative Bank). The opposite is also true — falling recovery presses down both the society's credit standing and its borrowing capacity, which makes lending to members even harder next year. So recovery is not just the account of the past year, it is the foundation of next year's capacity. (To understand the grading ratios: [cooperative financial ratios](/blog/financial-ratios-for-cooperatives).)

## The benefit of digital recovery tracking

Pulling out an aging list from a hand-written register is slow and error-prone. In good software, as soon as every loan, instalment and recovery is recorded, an exact outstanding list, aging and recovery% are ready automatically — on any day. The secretary does not need to guess "who owes how much"; the figures themselves tell you whom to focus on first. At year-end, this same digital discipline saves hours of work and keeps the final accounts correct.

## Link recovery to the crop cycle

For societies that give agricultural loans (such as PACS), a practical understanding is that the farmer's income is tied to the crop cycle — he has money only when the crop is harvested and sold. So if recovery dates are in step with the crop cycle, recovery is easier and the farmer does not face needless pressure. Speeding up the recovery drive around the rabi and kharif harvest and sale, and keeping only reminders before that — this is both farmer-friendly and effective. This is also the spirit of cooperation: recovery is not to break the member, but to keep the society's cycle running.

## One-time settlement — think it through

For some very old, stuck dues, societies sometimes consider an arrangement like One-Time Settlement, where a part of the due is taken from the member in one go and the case is closed. This can be a way to get back some of the lost money, but it has its own risks — such as sending the wrong message to members who repay regularly.

> ⚠️ Eligibility, procedure and approval powers for one-time settlement / waiver depend on the society's bye-laws and the government orders that apply. Take any such step only with the approval of the competent authority and within the rules — confirm with your cooperative department / auditor. This is not legal / financial advice.

## Year-end recovery checklist

- [ ] Freshly pulled out the age-wise outstanding list?
- [ ] Large and 1 year+ old dues marked and on priority?
- [ ] Every contact / instalment promise recorded?
- [ ] Every recovery made recorded immediately, in the right account?
- [ ] Latest recovery% and NPA figures placed before the management?
- [ ] Formal steps started as per the rules against those who avoid on purpose?

## Frequently asked questions

**When should the recovery drive start?** — It should run all year, but speed it up in the months before year-end, so that the final accounts come out right.
**How do I make the outstanding list?** — Age-wise (by how old the due is), so that old / large dues can be given priority.
**How do I calculate recovery%?** — Amount recovered ÷ total amount due × 100.
**How do I make the recovery entry?** — Cash/Bank Dr; Cr the recovery (as per the rules) first to overdue interest, then to the principal loan.
**When is a loan treated as NPA?** — When the due crosses the set period under the norms that apply; confirm the exact period / classification as per the rules of the cooperative department / NABARD.
**What if the member does not pay?** — First contact and an instalment plan; then a formal notice as per the rules and the state Act's recovery procedure (exact procedure from the department / legal advice).
**Should every defaulter be treated the same way?** — No; a reminder for those who forgot, an instalment plan for those in hardship, and firm steps as per the rules against those who avoid on purpose.
**How should recovery dates be fixed?** — In agricultural loans, keeping them in step with the crop cycle (rabi/kharif harvest and sale) makes recovery easier.

## Three things to remember

- 📋 Recovery always from the aging list — not from guesswork.
- 📊 Watch recovery% and NPA all year, and year-end becomes easy.
- 🧾 Record every recovery immediately — keep the accounts correct.

## In the end

Giving a loan is easy; the real skill is **recovery**. Systematic recovery before year-end keeps the society strong and audit-ready. Its secret lies not in strictness but in discipline — a clean list, regular follow-up, polite but firm contact, and an immediate, correct entry for every recovery. These four habits keep a society's borrowing capacity and credit standing strong year after year.
