Whenever a decision is taken in a Managing Committee (Board) meeting — whether it is a financial sanction, an appointment, a purchase, or any other administrative matter — it must be recorded as a formal Resolution. Many societies just write it in the Minutes Book, but what structure a proper Resolution and its Certified True Copy should have, fit to show to a bank, a government department or any third party, is often not clear. This guide explains that structure, the necessary parts, Rule 30 (conflict of interest) and the Certified Copy — all in simple language.

> ⚠️ **Information checked up to 18 October 2026.** The sections and rules mentioned in this article have been matched with the government text of the Act (amended up to October 2007), the 2013 amendment and the Indian Kanoon copy of the Rules 1989 (see "Sources" below). The Resolution structure (RESOLVED THAT etc.) is good practice, not a format fixed by any rule. This is general information, not legal advice.

> 💡 In the society's registered bye-laws, the "Board" may also be called the Managing Committee or just the Committee — there is no difference; use the correct term given in the bye-laws. Use this format only for matters that fall within the committee's powers — wherever the Act/Rules/bye-laws/departmental instructions require the approval of the General Body, the Registrar or any other authority, take that separately.

## Why a Resolution is so important

A formal Resolution is not just a paper formality — it is the legal and accounting proof of every big decision of the society. There are three big reasons for this: first, it proves that the decision was taken **properly and with authority** (in a proper meeting, with quorum); second, it becomes **evidence in audit** that an expense/appointment/purchase was approved by the right authority, within the right limit; and third, it is the official document to show to a **third party (bank/department)** (for example, to prove the authorised signatory). Without a proper Resolution, even a valid decision can later become a cause of dispute or objection.

## In which matters a Resolution is needed

Almost every decision related to the society's money, property, appointments or outside relations needs a formal Resolution. Common examples:

- **Banking:** Opening/closing an account, fixing the authorised signatory, taking a loan.
- **Financial:** Approving a big expense/purchase, passing the budget.
- **Appointment/staff:** Appointment of an employee or other staff decisions (as per rules).
- **Property/contracts:** Land/building transactions, any big contract.
- **Administrative:** Applying under a scheme, authorising someone to represent the society.

Keep in mind — the committee can decide many of these itself, but for some, the role of the General Body or the Registrar is fixed in law. Some examples:

- **Amendment of bye-laws:** A resolution in a General Body meeting at which at least two-thirds of the members are present (Rule 10), and then registration by the Registrar (Section 10).
- **The society's maximum credit limit:** Only the General Body sets this, with the Registrar's approval — Primary Credit Service Societies like PACS do not need the Registrar's approval (Rule 24).
- **Subjects of the annual general meeting:** Next year's programme, the audit report, the list of defaulters, reserves, the list of employees who are relatives of office-bearers, elections, amendment of bye-laws, and since 2013 the appointment of the auditor from the panel (Section 25(3)).

So before making a Resolution, check whether the subject is within the committee's powers or not.

## Meeting details — what should be recorded at the top

The society's name, Registration No., Registered Office, Meeting Type (Managing Committee/Board Meeting), Meeting Date, Time, Venue, total committee members, members present and quorum — this basic information should be recorded at the top, so that it can be confirmed that the meeting was called as per rules and the quorum was complete. Mentioning the quorum is important because decisions taken without enough attendance can later be open to challenge.

## How to write the RESOLUTION section

This section should record the Agenda Item No., Resolution No., Subject and Background (preamble) — that is, the background on the basis of which the decision is being taken. The Background is needed so that anyone who reads it later can understand for what reason and in what context this decision was taken. After this, the decision taken after discussion is written in two parts:

- **RESOLVED THAT** — the main decision.
- **RESOLVED FURTHER THAT** — any additional/supporting decision, if any (such as authorising someone, fixing a time limit etc.).

### A simple example structure

Suppose the society has to authorise someone to open a bank account. The Resolution will be something like this: first the Background ("The society has to open its operating account in X Bank"), then **"RESOLVED THAT** the society open a current account in the Y branch of X Bank", and then **"RESOLVED FURTHER THAT** Secretary Shri ___ is authorised to operate this account". In this way the main decision and the supporting authority are both recorded clearly and separately — and there is no ambiguity when it is shown to the bank.

## The Financial Sanction part

If the resolution relates to a financial sanction, then the Authorised Person, Designation, Amount/Financial Limit (or Not Applicable), Budget/Account Head, and whether any further Approval is Required (and from whom) — this information should be recorded separately. It is needed later in audit to prove that the expense was approved by the right authority, within the right limit.

## Voting/Decision Record and Rule 30 — Conflict of Interest

The Voting/Decision Record should record the Result (Unanimous/Majority), For, Against, Abstained, and Conflict/Recusal (if any) — so that it is clear by what majority/consent the decision was taken.

> ⚠️ **Rule 30** of the Haryana Co-operative Societies Rules, 1989 says that an officer of the society **shall not have** any interest — directly or indirectly, other than in the capacity of officer — in: (a) any contract with the society; (b) property sold, bought or leased by or to the society; (c) any other transaction of the society. The only exceptions are: investment in the society, a loan taken from the society, and housing given by the society to a salaried employee. Also, an officer cannot buy a member's property sold in recovery of dues, and these bars apply **up to 2 years after** leaving office.

Note: Rule 30 is a **bar**, not just a rule to "disclose and step aside". If a member has such an interest, just recording recusal does not fix the matter — first check whether that contract or transaction should happen at all. A person who has an interest in a contract with the society is also disqualified from contesting the committee election (Rule 27(b)), and on such disqualification arising they can be removed from office (Rule 28). Still, if a member disclosed an interest in the meeting and stayed away from the discussion, record this clearly in the voting record.

## When and why a Certified True Copy is needed

When a copy of the resolution has to be given to a bank, a government department or any third party (for example, to prove the appointment of an authorised signatory), a certified copy is given. In it, the Resolution No., Agenda Item No., Subject and both RESOLVED clauses are written again in a standalone format, issued with the name, designation, signature, date and the society's seal — it is always **subject to the original Minutes Book**.

**What the law says:** According to Section 42 of the Act, a certified copy of any entry in a book regularly kept by the society is treated as prima facie evidence of that entry in any suit or legal proceeding. The method of certifying is in Rule 32: a certificate should be written at the foot of the copy that it is a true copy of that entry and that the book is in the society's custody; it should carry **the date and the signature of an officer authorised by the committee**. So get the Certified True Copy signed by the same officer whom the committee has authorised for this.

## Minutes Book vs Resolution — understand the difference

A common confusion is that writing it in the Minutes Book is enough. The two have different roles: the **Minutes Book** is the record of the whole meeting (who came, what was discussed, which decisions were taken) — it is the original, primary document. The **Resolution/Certified True Copy** takes one particular decision out of it, in a standalone, certified format, to show to a third party. That is, the Certified Copy does not replace the Minutes Book — it is a certified extract from it, and always remains subject to the original Minutes Book. So it is necessary to keep both correct and matching.

## Digital records — ease and safety

Today many societies also keep their resolutions and minutes in digital form (along with the original signed Minutes Book). The benefit — instant search by resolution number, date and subject; a uniform, clean format; and a safe backup, so that no important resolution is lost. When a bank/department needs a Certified Copy, it can be taken out quickly and correctly from the digital record. Keep in mind — the digital copy is for convenience; legally, the original signed Minutes Book remains primary.

## Checklist before use

> 📘 The meeting was convened by the competent officer/authority as per Rule 23 and the registered bye-laws; proper notice and agenda were circulated as per the bye-laws/instructions; the quorum was present throughout the decision; the subject is within the powers of the Managing Committee/Board, and wherever the approval of the General Body/Registrar/federation/department is needed, it has been taken separately; any conflict of interest under Rule 30 or the bye-laws has been addressed and recorded; the resolution number, agenda number and Minutes Book entry — all are the same in all records; and in a financial resolution the amount, purpose, budget/account head, authorised person and approval limit are clearly recorded.

## Common mistakes

- **Just writing it in the Minutes Book and leaving it** → a third party needs a Certified True Copy.
- **Taking a decision outside the committee's powers** → where General Body/Registrar approval is needed, take it separately.
- **Hiding a conflict of interest** → if there is an interest under Rule 30, record the recusal clearly.
- **Amount/limit/authority incomplete in a financial resolution** → audit objection; record everything clearly.
- **Mismatch between resolution number and Minutes entry** → keep them the same in all records.
- **Not confirming quorum/notice** → a decision without quorum/notice can be questioned.
- **Not recording the Background** → later it is hard to understand the basis of the decision; always write the preamble.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between "Board" and "Managing Committee"?** — No difference; in the society's registered bye-laws it may be called the Managing Committee, the Board, or just the Committee — use the correct term given in the bye-laws.
**Can the Managing Committee take the final decision on every matter?** — No. Only on matters that fall within the committee's powers. Where the Act, Rules, bye-laws or departmental instructions require the approval of the General Body, the Registrar or any other authority, that has to be taken separately.
**What to do if there is a conflict of interest?** — Rule 30 bars an officer from having an interest in the society's contracts, property deals or other transactions (except investment, loans and employee housing). So first check whether the deal should happen at all; and record clearly in the voting record the member who stayed away in the meeting.
**When is a Certified True Copy needed?** — When a certified copy of the resolution has to be given to a bank, a government department or any other third party. According to Rule 32, it should carry a "true copy" certificate, the date and the signature of an officer authorised by the committee; under Section 42 such a copy is accepted as evidence.
**Who calls the committee meeting?** — According to Rule 23, the Chief Executive Officer or an officer authorised by them, on the direction of the authority named in the bye-laws. On receiving a written demand from the Registrar or from the members set in the bye-laws, the society has to call a meeting within one month (Section 26).
**Why is a Resolution necessary?** — It is the legal and accounting proof of the decision — for proper authorisation, audit evidence, and showing to a third party (bank/department).
**Difference between the Minutes Book and a Certified Copy?** — The Minutes Book is the original, primary record of the whole meeting; the Certified Copy is a certified extract of one decision from it (always subject to the original).
**What is the difference between RESOLVED THAT and RESOLVED FURTHER THAT?** — The first is the main decision; the second is a supporting decision (such as authorising someone).
**In which matters is a Resolution needed?** — On almost every important decision, such as banking, big expense/purchase, appointments, property/contracts and authorised representation.
**Is a digital record valid?** — Yes, for convenience (search/backup), but legally the original signed Minutes Book remains primary.

## Three things to remember

- 📝 Every Resolution should clearly record the meeting details (quorum), Background, RESOLVED THAT and RESOLVED FURTHER THAT, so that both the basis and the scope of the decision are clear.
- ⚠️ Rule 30 is a bar on an officer's interest, not just a recusal rule — if there is an interest, first question the deal, and record the member who stayed away.
- 📄 A Certified True Copy is always subject to the original Minutes Book — it does not replace the Minutes Book; it is only a certified extract to show to a third party.
- ✅ Before making a Resolution, check that the subject is within the committee's powers — otherwise take General Body/Registrar approval separately.

## In the end

A good Board Resolution makes the society's decision strong, provable and dispute-free on paper. Its structure is simple — meeting details (with quorum), then the Background and both RESOLVED clauses, amount-limit-authority in financial matters, and a record of recusal if there is a conflict. When a third party needs it, issue a Certified True Copy subject to the original Minutes Book. The decisions of a society that follows these basics pass smoothly in every audit and every bank/department check — because then a clear, authorised and certified record of every decision exists.
