# Basics of cooperative accounting: for new secretaries and accountants

If you have just become a new secretary, accountant or clerk in a cooperative society, it is natural to feel nervous on hearing the word "account books" (bahi-khata). But the truth is that **cooperative accounting** rests on a few clear, simple rules. Once the foundation is understood, everything else falls into place by itself. This article explains that foundation in simple language, with examples — and links onward to an in-depth guide on each topic.

## What is accounting?

Accounting means — **recording every transaction of the society in order, under the right category**, so that on any day you can tell:

- how much money/property the society has,
- who has how much claim on it (liabilities/capital),
- and how much income was earned and how much spent during the year.

> 💡 In a cooperative society, accounting is not just "keeping accounts" — it is **accountability** to the members. Whose every rupee is should be clearly visible.

### What is special about cooperative accounting?

The rules of accounting are the same for an ordinary business and a cooperative society, but the emphasis is different:

- **Members are the owners:** share capital, deposits and dividend are directly linked to members.
- **Statutory books:** the society must keep the books prescribed by the Registrar/bye-laws.
- **Distribution of profit is rule-bound:** a part of net profit goes to the compulsory reserve fund, and the rest as per the rules. (See: [profit distribution and reserve fund](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves).)
- **Annual audit:** every society is audited by the cooperative department.

## Five types of accounts

Every transaction falls into one of these five categories. This is the foundation of all accounting:

1. **Assets** — what the society has: cash, bank, stock, building, loan amounts to be recovered.
2. **Liabilities** — what the society has to pay: deposits, loans taken, dues payable.
3. **Capital** — members' share capital and accumulated funds.
4. **Income** — sales, interest, commission, service charges.
5. **Expenses** — salary, rent, electricity, interest paid, repairs.

> 🔍 A simple way to identify: ask "Is this something I have (asset), something I must pay (liability), something belonging to the owners (capital), an earning (income), or a spending (expense)?" — the answer is the category.

Choosing the right category is the most important beginner's skill, because every later entry and report rests on it. When in doubt, don't pick an account in a hurry — repeat the five questions above.

## Debit and credit: the heart of double entry

Every transaction has **two sides** — that is why it is called "double entry". One account is debited (Dr), the other is credited (Cr), and the amounts of both are always equal.

The simple golden rule to remember:

| Category | On increase | On decrease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assets and expenses | Debit (Dr) | Credit (Cr) |
| Liabilities, capital and income | Credit (Cr) | Debit (Dr) |

> 🔍 Example: ₹5,000 share capital received in cash from a member. Cash (asset) increased → **Cash Dr ₹5,000**; share capital increased → **Share Capital Cr ₹5,000**. Dr = Cr, the account is balanced.

For a deeper understanding of this rule and more examples, see: [debit-credit and double entry](/blog/debit-credit-and-double-entry).

## The accounting equation

All accounting rests on one equation, which is always true:

> 📘 **Assets = Liabilities + Capital**

Whenever your Balance Sheet "does not tally", it is actually this equation that has broken somewhere — often in the Opening Balance. The two sides of every voucher keep this equation balanced. (In detail: [accounting equation](/blog/accounting-equation-explained).)

## The accounting cycle (Accounting Cycle)

In a financial year, work runs in this order:

1. A **transaction** takes place (sale, purchase, payment).
2. A **voucher** is made (receipt/payment/journal/contra). → [voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide)
3. From the voucher, the **Day Book**, **cash/bank book** and **Ledger** are made. → [ledger and posting](/blog/ledger-and-posting-explained)
4. From these, the **Trial Balance** — where Dr = Cr is checked. → [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained)
5. Then the **final accounts** — Trading, Income & Expenditure, Balance Sheet.
6. At the end of the year, the **audit** and **distribution of profit**.

In a digital system you do only the **first step** (the correct voucher) — the remaining stages are built automatically. This is the biggest difference between manual and digital, which we looked at in detail in [why digital accounting is necessary](/blog/digital-accounting-for-cooperatives).

## Essential books — at a glance

At the start, knowing these books is enough:

| Book | What it keeps |
| --- | --- |
| **Cash book** | All cash receipts/payments, cash-in-hand balance |
| **Bank book** | Deposits/withdrawals of every bank account |
| **Ledger** | A separate page for every account (member, expense, income) |
| **Member register** | Shares, deposits, loans — member-wise |
| **Stock register** | Inward/outward movement and balance of goods |

Keeping cash and bank separate is very important — see the difference and correct use here: [cash book vs bank book](/blog/cash-book-vs-bank-book).

## A small example — from start to balance

Suppose a new society has three transactions:

1. Share capital of ₹500 each from 10 members in cash → **Cash Dr ₹5,000, Share Capital Cr ₹5,000**.
2. Furniture of ₹3,000 bought in cash → **Furniture Dr ₹3,000, Cash Cr ₹3,000**.
3. ₹2,000 cash deposited in the bank (contra) → **Bank Dr ₹2,000, Cash Cr ₹2,000**.

Now the position: cash ₹0, bank ₹2,000, furniture ₹3,000 = total assets ₹5,000; and share capital ₹5,000. That is, **Assets (5,000) = Liabilities (0) + Capital (5,000)** — the equation tallies. This same check can be repeated every day.

Note that in all three entries Dr = Cr held, and each time the equation stayed balanced by itself. If it ever does not tally, understand that one side was missed or went into the wrong account — then going back and finding that one voucher is enough. In this way, small correct steps together make correct books for the whole year.

## Financial year and accounting period

In India, accounting usually runs on a financial year from **1 April to 31 March**. The whole year's transactions are gathered in this period, and the Closing of 31 March becomes the Opening of the next year. So making entries on the right date and in the right year is very important — one entry in the wrong year spoils the reports of two years. (In detail: [financial year and accounting period](/blog/financial-year-and-accounting-period).)

## Member accounting — the life of a cooperative

Unlike an ordinary business, in a cooperative society every member has their own account — how many shares, how much deposit, how much loan outstanding. This is kept in the member register and member ledger, and it is the basis of accountability to members. When a member buys shares, makes a deposit or repays a loan, a voucher is made each time and their member account is updated. (See: [member and share accounting](/blog/member-and-share-accounting).)

## Audit — why and for whom

Every cooperative society has an annual audit, in which auditors check that the books are kept as per the rules and are correct. That is why day-to-day discipline — a clear narration, cancelling a voucher (not deleting it), month-end matching — matters so much: at audit time, this "trail" becomes the proof of your honesty. Good books make the audit easy, not hard. (See: [how a cooperative audit works](/blog/how-cooperative-society-audit-works).)

## Key terms — a glimpse

At the start, learn these terms; the rest will come by themselves:

| Term | Simple meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Voucher** | Written proof of one transaction |
| **Ledger** | A separate page and balance for every account |
| **Trial Balance** | A list of all balances, where Dr = Cr is checked |
| **Opening/Closing** | The balance at the start/end of the period |
| **contra** | Money moving between cash ↔ bank |
| **Reserve fund** | The part of profit that is compulsorily held back |

## The daily role of the secretary/accountant

A new secretary or accountant only has to do this every day:

1. Make a **voucher** for every transaction of the day (right type, right accounts, clear narration).
2. At the end of the day, tally the cash balance with a **physical count**.
3. Keep supporting documents (bills, receipts, cheque numbers) safely.
4. At month-end, do the bank reconciliation (BRS) and look at the reports.

Do this regularly, and the year-end reports, audit and profit distribution all become easy — because the foundation is already strong.

## Three habits that save the books

- 🗓️ **Make entries daily** — record transactions on the same day, don't let them pile up.
- 🔁 **Month-end matching** — tally the bank statement, cash and stock every month.
- 💾 **Regular backup** — never lose data.

## Manual vs digital — what changes

The basic rules stay the same in both; the difference is only in effort and room for error:

| Aspect | Manual (by hand) | Digital system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entry | Separately in each book | One voucher, the rest automatic |
| Calculation errors | Common | Almost zero |
| Trial Balance | Added by hand | Instant, Dr=Cr automatically |
| Reports | Made separately | In one click |
| Backup/security | Depends on paper | Regular digital backup |

This does not mean you can stop learning the rules — a digital system too will give correct reports only when your first step (the voucher) is correct. So do understand the basics, and then the tool will reduce your effort.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Mixing up categories** → such as treating member deposits as income (they are a liability).
- **Mixing cash and bank** → keep both separate, and move money between them by contra.
- **Not tallying the opening balance** → last year's Closing is this year's Opening.
- **Deleting vouchers** → if one is wrong, cancel it, don't delete it (so the audit trail stays).
- **Piling up at year-end** → doing a little every day is the easy way.
- **Leaving the narration blank** → in every entry, write from whom/which item/which bill.
- **Not taking a backup** → digital or paper, always keep a second copy of the records.

## Three things to remember

- 🧱 Identifying the five categories (assets/liabilities/capital/income/expenses) is the foundation of all accounting.
- ⚖️ One single rule — if assets/expenses increase, Dr; if liabilities/capital/income increase, Cr.
- 🔁 "Assets = Liabilities + Capital" must always tally; if it does not, check the opening.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is double entry?** — Every transaction has two sides; one account Dr, the other Cr, and both equal.
**How many types of accounts are there?** — Five: assets, liabilities, capital, income, expenses.
**Why does the Balance Sheet not tally?** — Mostly because of the opening balance or some unbalanced entry; check the equation.
**Are member deposits income or a liability?** — A liability; it is the member's money that the society has to return.
**Will all books have to be made by hand?** — In a digital system you only fill in the correct voucher; the cash book, ledger and Trial Balance are made automatically.
**Where should a new accountant start?** — With the five categories and the Dr/Cr rule; then voucher entry, then the Trial Balance.
**How is profit distributed?** — A part of net profit goes to the compulsory reserve fund, the rest as per the rules — in detail in a separate guide.
**From when to when is the financial year?** — In India usually 1 April to 31 March; transactions of this period are counted in one year.
**Are the rules different in manual and digital?** — No, the rules are the same; digital only reduces effort and calculation errors.
**Do I need to be a CA?** — No; with the five categories, the Dr/Cr rule and regular discipline, even a new secretary can keep the books correctly.

## Conclusion

Cooperative accounting is no magic — just five types of accounts, one rule of debit and credit, and one equation. This understanding makes every voucher of yours correct, and from correct vouchers all the other reports become correct by themselves. Move ahead one step at a time, record a little every day, and do month-end matching — within a few weeks the account books will become easy for you.
