# Bye-laws: the cooperative society's own rule book

Every cooperative society has its own "rule book". It is called the **bye-laws**. Most answers to how the society will run are in it. This article explains in simple language **what bye-laws are**, what they contain, the order of Act, Rules and bye-laws, how amendments are made, and how they connect to accounting, with examples.

If you are about to run a society or become a new secretary/office-bearer, reading the bye-laws is the first essential task, because almost every power and limit you have is written there.

## What bye-laws are
Bye-laws are the society's **internal rules**. Staying within the cooperative Act, they decide how the society will run its work. They are **below** the Act: no rule that clashes with the Act is valid.

In simple words, the Act is the "big law" that applies to every society in the state, while the bye-laws are only your society's own arrangement, made within that same Act. So two societies can have different bye-laws, but both have to follow the same Act. At the time of registration, these bye-laws are submitted to the RCS, and once approved they become the society's "constitution". (See: [How to register a society](/blog/how-to-register-a-cooperative-society).)

## Why bye-laws are needed

Without clear rules, every decision, big or small, can turn into a dispute: who becomes a member, how many shares they get, when the meeting is held, how profit is shared. The bye-laws answer all of these in advance and keep the society running smoothly and fairly. They also assure every member that the rules are the same for everyone and nobody can act arbitrarily. In short, the bye-laws are the foundation of the society's democratic and transparent working.

## What bye-laws usually contain
- **Membership** — who can become a member, admission fee, conditions for withdrawal and expulsion.
- **Share capital** — value of a share, maximum share limit for one member.
- **Meetings** — general body and managing committee; quorum, notice period and procedure.
- **Managing committee** — constitution, number of members, term and powers.
- **Funds** — reserve fund, profit distribution and the dividend limit.
- **Audit and accounts** — how books are to be kept and when the audit is held.
- **Dispute settlement** — how internal disputes are resolved and what the procedure is.
- **Area of operation and objective** — in which area and in which activities the society will work.

> 💡 Simple rule: whenever there is a question — "can this be done?" — look at the **bye-laws** first, then the Act.

Bye-laws of different types of societies put weight on different things. A consumer store's bye-laws focus on sales rules and member benefits, a credit society's on loan limits and recovery, and a housing society's on allotment of plots/flats. But the basic structure (membership, meetings, funds, audit) stays the same in all of them.

## Model bye-laws and amendment
- The RCS usually provides **model bye-laws**, which the society adopts as per its needs.
- To change (amend) the bye-laws, a **general body resolution + RCS approval** is usually needed.

### Why start from model bye-laws

Not every society needs to write rules from scratch. The RCS model bye-laws are already in line with the Act and Rules, so starting from them makes approval easier and reduces the chance of big mistakes. The society adapts only the parts linked to its own objective (such as area of operation, special services); the rest of the structure stays standard. That is why new societies are advised to start from the model bye-laws and make only the changes they need, rather than write completely new bye-laws.

### Amendment procedure — step by step

1. **Proposal:** the managing committee prepares the draft amendment.
2. **General body:** it is placed in the general body and passed by vote.
3. **Submit to the RCS:** the passed amendment is sent to the Registrar for approval/registration.
4. **Registration:** the amendment is treated as in force only after it is registered, and the new arrangement takes effect only from then.

> ⚠️ Remember — merely passing it in the general body does not bring the amendment into force; it must be registered with the Registrar (Section 10 in Haryana).

## An example — bye-laws in practice

Suppose a society's bye-laws say that "a member can hold at most 100 shares" and "the managing committee will have 9 members, with a term of 5 years." Now if a member asks for 150 shares, the secretary can refuse on the basis of the bye-laws. That is the strength of bye-laws. Similarly, if the society wants an 11-member committee, a bye-law amendment is needed first, otherwise that constitution will be invalid. In this way, the bye-laws keep day-to-day decisions clear and free of disputes.

## Hierarchy of bye-laws — which is above which

Three levels of rules work in running a society, and their order is clear:

> 📘 **Cooperative Act > State Rules > Society's bye-laws**

That is, the state's cooperative Act is at the top, the Rules below it, and the society's own bye-laws at the bottom. Any bye-law provision that clashes with a law above it is not valid. So whenever you make or change bye-laws, always check that they are within the Act and Rules. Once you understand this hierarchy, half the confusion clears up by itself, because in any clash the answer is always "the law above".

## What each part means — in a little more depth

- **Membership:** who is eligible, admission fee, conditions for withdrawal and expulsion. This decides whose society it is. (See: [Membership: rights and duties](/blog/membership-rights-and-duties).)
- **Share capital:** the value of one share, the maximum share limit for one member. This decides the capital structure.
- **Meetings:** general body and managing committee meetings, quorum (minimum attendance), notice period. This is the backbone of democratic control.
- **Managing committee:** number of members, term, powers and election. Day-to-day management is in their hands.
- **Funds and profit distribution:** reserve fund, dividend limit, other funds. These directly shape the accounts.
- **Audit and accounts:** how books are to be kept, when the audit is held. The guarantee of transparency. (See: [Basics of cooperative accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

## Link with accounting
Bye-laws directly shape the accounts: the % for the reserve fund, the dividend limit, share capital rules, the audit cycle. So the society's software and accounts must match the bye-laws. If the bye-laws say a fixed percentage will go to the reserve fund and dividend will be paid only up to a limit, the accounts must show exactly that, otherwise it becomes an audit objection. (See: [Profit distribution and reserve fund](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves) and [Member and share registers](/blog/member-and-share-accounting).)

## Amendment, merger and closing the society
Even after the bye-laws are settled, the society's story does not end there. Three provisions of the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act, 1984 cover the later life cycle:

| Situation | Section | Main point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bye-law amendment | Section 10 | An amendment is not valid without being registered with the Registrar — passing it in the general body alone is not enough |
| Merger of two societies | Section 15 | As soon as assets and liabilities are transferred, the old society's registration is treated as cancelled by itself, everything is absorbed into the new society |
| Formal closure | Section 105 | The Registrar can order winding up; a liquidator is appointed, who settles the assets and liabilities |

> 📘 Section 24(2): even after winding up, the liability of a former/deceased member does not end by itself; it continues up to the limit fixed by law.

Knowing these provisions matters because the bye-laws give a framework not just for "how the society runs" but also for "how the society changes, merges or closes". So before these big steps, always check the relevant section and the Registrar's procedure, so that no decision is held invalid later.

## How to read and use your bye-laws

Do not treat the bye-laws as something to lock away in a cupboard. They are an everyday working document:

- **Any dispute/question:** first look for the relevant provision in the bye-laws (membership, meeting, distribution, whatever it is).
- **New office-bearers:** read the bye-laws fully once as soon as you take charge. Your powers and limits are set here.
- **Before a meeting/election:** match the quorum, notice period and procedure with the bye-laws, so that decisions stay valid.
- **Before distribution:** check the dividend limit and fund rules against the bye-laws.

A society that knows and follows its bye-laws has fewer disputes and more transparency.

A good habit is to keep a copy of the bye-laws in the society office within everyone's reach, and to tell a new member the main points when they join. This avoids many later disputes right at the start. When office-bearers change, briefing the new office-bearers on the bye-laws is also good practice, so that continuity is kept.

## Consequences of not following the bye-laws

The bye-laws are not just a formality. Breaking them brings real trouble: invalid meetings/elections, audit objections, member disputes, and in serious cases action by the Registrar. So take every big decision only in line with both the bye-laws and the Act.

For example, if a decision is taken in the general body without enough quorum, that decision can be challenged later and cancelled, even if the intention was good. So following the procedure is as important as the decision being right. Even a small procedural slip can make the whole decision invalid.

## Frequently asked questions
**What are bye-laws?** — The society's own internal rules, within the Act.
**Are bye-laws above the Act?** — No; in a clash with the Act, the Act prevails.
**How to change bye-laws?** — By a general body resolution and RCS approval (as per state rules); in Haryana, under Section 10, an amendment is not valid without being registered.
**What are model bye-laws?** — A ready framework given by the RCS, which the society adopts as per its needs.
**What is the order of Act, Rules and bye-laws?** — The Act at the top, then the Rules, then the bye-laws; in a clash, the one above prevails.
**What do bye-laws contain?** — Membership, share capital, meetings, managing committee, funds/distribution, audit and accounts.
**How do bye-laws link to accounting?** — The reserve fund, dividend limit and audit cycle are set here, and the accounts must show exactly that.
**Is it necessary to read the bye-laws?** — Yes; every office-bearer should read the bye-laws as soon as they take charge, because their powers and limits are set here.
**What happens if bye-laws are not followed?** — Invalid decisions, audit objections, disputes and in serious cases action by the Registrar.

## Conclusion

The bye-laws are the society's own constitution: made within the Act, but the very first test for your society's day-to-day decisions. Know them, follow them, and when needed, amend them properly. A society that runs as per its bye-laws has no lasting disputes and no audit objections, and members' trust stays strong. And since the reserve fund, dividend and audit are all set here, your accounts will be right only when they match the bye-laws.
