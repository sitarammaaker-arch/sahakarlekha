A member of a cooperative society is not just a person who has deposited share money. In principle, the member is also an owner of the institution, a beneficiary, and a part of its governance. But in practice the big problem has been that many members are not clear about what information they can ask the management for, how they can question an election, or where to take a personal complaint. In the case of **Multi-State Cooperative Societies**, this system has seen important changes in the last few years.

> ⚠️ **Information checked up to 18 October 2026.** The text of the Amendment Act 2023 was matched with the Gazette of India, and the Ombudsman and Election Authority dates with the Ministry of Cooperation's Annual Report 2023-24 and the Central Registrar (CRCS) website — see "Sources" below. We have not seen the full text of the Multi-State Rules 2002 (amended in 2023), so points that appear only in the Rules have been written in softer terms.

## First understand — which societies this law applies to

In India, a cooperative society can fall under two separate legal frameworks. If a society's area of operation is limited to one state, it runs under that state's cooperative Act (for example, in Haryana, the [Act of 1984](/blog/haryana-cooperative-act-1984-rules-1989-guide)). But a society whose area of operation spreads across more than one state comes under the central law, the **Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002** — where the **Central Registrar of Cooperative Societies (CRCS)** handles registration and regulatory processes.

> ⚠️ So it would not be right to assume that the arrangements described below apply automatically to every PACS, dairy society or state-level cooperative society — first check under which law your society is registered.

It is important to recognise this difference first because all the new arrangements below come only from the central (Multi-State) law. The **Multi-State Co-operative Societies (Amendment) Act, 2023** made many amendments to the original 2002 law, and it received the President's assent on 3 August 2023. The aim was to strengthen governance, increase transparency, make the election process independent, and build a grievance-redressal mechanism for members.

## The basic rights of a member

Before going into these new arrangements, it is useful to understand the basic rights of a cooperative member — these are almost the same in every cooperative framework:

- **Right to vote:** "One member, one vote" — in electing the board and voting on resolutions.
- **Right to information:** to receive the prescribed information about the society's working and accounts.
- **Share in profit:** a fair share in the society's profit/dividend (as per rules).
- **Participation in the general body:** to ask questions, give suggestions and demand accountability.
- **Grievance redressal:** to take one's complaint to the appropriate forum.

The 2023 amendments gave these same rights — especially information, elections and grievance redressal — a stronger, institutional form in Multi-State societies.

## Elections no longer depend on the sitting board

Under the old system, the responsibility for conducting board elections stayed with the sitting board itself. The 2023 amendment provided for a **Co-operative Election Authority** for this — its job is to conduct board elections, supervise the preparation of the electoral roll and control the election process, so that the body conducting the election and the sitting management contesting it are not the same party. The government set up this Authority on 11 March 2024 — meaning it exists not just on paper but also as an institution.

Under the amended Section 45, this Authority consists of a Chairperson, a Vice-Chairperson and up to three members, appointed by the Central Government on the recommendation of a selection committee.

The 2023 amendment also changed the structure of the board — under the amended Section 41(3), the board will have **at most 21 directors** as per the bye-laws, and in a Multi-State society whose members are individuals and include members of these categories, the board will have **one SC/ST member** and **two women**.

## The legal route for a member to ask for information

Perhaps the most practical change is the arrangement of a **Co-operative Information Officer**. Under the amended Section 106:

- Every Multi-State society will appoint a Co-operative Information Officer, who will give members information about the society's working and management — but this information will be limited to the **disclosure norms set in the society's bye-laws**.
- The member will apply with the prescribed fee.
- The Information Officer must give the information within **30 days**, or reject the application with reasons.
- If rejected, the member can appeal to the Co-operative Ombudsman within **one month**, whose decision will be final.

Under Section 106A, the society's Chief Executive must keep the rules, bye-laws and the **list of members** open for free inspection at the registered address.

> 💡 The method of application (written/electronic) and whether a reason for asking for information must be given — these points will be in the Multi-State Rules, whose text we have not seen. Check with your society or CRCS before applying.

> 💡 This arrangement looks a lot like the transparency principle of RTI, but it would not be right to treat the two as fully the same — this right comes from the Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act's own arrangement, and the scope of information is tied to the society's disclosure norms and the related law/bye-laws, not every kind of document as in a general government RTI application.

According to the CRCS website, this appeal is made in **Form VII**, within one month of rejection, before the Co-operative Ombudsman.

## What the Cooperative Ombudsman looks at

According to the Ministry of Cooperation's Annual Report 2023-24, a Co-operative Ombudsman was appointed on **5 March 2024** — with jurisdiction over the whole country, for three years. Under Section 85A and the CRCS website, the Ombudsman looks at these matters:

| Nature of complaint |
| --- |
| Complaint related to a member's deposits |
| Matter related to equitable benefits from the society's functioning |
| Issue affecting a member's individual rights |
| Appeal against an order of the Co-operative Information Officer |

Under Section 85A(2), the Ombudsman must complete the inquiry and decision within **three months** of receiving the complaint; during the inquiry it can give directions to the society, which the society must follow within one month. If the society is not satisfied with a direction, it can appeal to the Central Registrar within one month, who will decide in 45 days (Section 85A(3)). During the inquiry, the Ombudsman has civil-court-like powers such as summoning witnesses and calling for documents (Section 85A(5)).

The complaint is filed in **Form VI** (Rule 30H(2)), and the CRCS website gives the Ombudsman office's address and e-mail (mscs-ombudsman@gov.in).

> 💡 Which matters are outside the Ombudsman's scope (such as matters already in arbitration), and through whom a complaint can be filed — these points will be in the Rules, whose text we have not seen. Read the conditions of Form VI carefully before filing a complaint.

## Three new institutions — at a glance

Look at the three main arrangements created by the 2023 amendment together:

| Institution | Job | What it means for the member |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Co-operative Election Authority | Conduct independent board elections | Fair elections, without interference from the sitting board |
| Co-operative Information Officer | Give information to the member | Information up to the bye-laws' disclosure norms, in 30 days |
| Co-operative Ombudsman | Grievance redressal/appeal | A forum for appeal on refusal of information and other matters |

Together, these three give a member three basic powers — fair elections, transparent information and a route for complaints. Together, these three put the cooperative principle of "the member is the owner" into practice.

## Recruitment and other changes

According to news and summary articles, the Rules amended in 2023 also contain provisions to make staff recruitment transparent. We have not seen the text of the Rules, so their conditions are not given here.

## How a member should use these rights

Rights are useful only when members use them. Practical steps: attend the general body regularly and ask questions; you can see the member list and bye-laws free of cost (Section 106A); when needed, ask for information by applying to the Co-operative Information Officer with the prescribed fee; if you don't get the information, appeal to the Ombudsman within one month; always vote in elections and keep an eye on the process; and keep records of your deposits/share of profit. An aware, active member is the real guarantee of strong cooperative governance — the law only gives the tools; picking them up is in the member's hands.

## Why these reforms were needed

To understand these changes, it helps to know what the problem was. In large Multi-State societies there were often complaints — the sitting management itself conducted elections (so fairness was questioned); members did not easily get information about the accounts; and there was no clear, independent forum for complaints. These weaknesses at places left room for mismanagement and neglect of members' interests. The 2023 amendments tried to fill exactly these three weaknesses — elections, information and complaints — with institutional solutions (Election Authority, Information Officer, Ombudsman). In other words, these reforms were born not from some abstract ideal, but from problems on the ground.

## State vs multi-state society — remember the difference again

Since this confusion is very common, it needs repeating: all the new arrangements described above (Election Authority, Information Officer, Ombudsman) apply **only to Multi-State Cooperative Societies** — that is, societies that spread across more than one state and are under CRCS. If your society is in only one state (like most PACS and dairy societies), your **state's cooperative Act** will apply to it, not this central law. The rights and complaint routes of such a state-level member must be looked up in their state Act (for Haryana: [Act 1984 guide](/blog/haryana-cooperative-act-1984-rules-1989-guide) and [Where to take disputes](/blog/cooperative-society-dispute-resolution-haryana)). So before taking any step, first make sure under which law your society is registered.

## The law alone is not enough

Creating institutions like the Election Authority, Ombudsman and Information Officer does not automatically make cooperative governance perfect. For that, members must know their rights, the society must make updated bye-laws available, annual returns and audits must be on time, election records must be correct, management decisions must be made through proper resolutions, and members must take meaningful part in the general body. If members themselves stay away from the institution's working, even the best law will give only limited results.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does this arrangement apply to every cooperative society?** — No; only to Multi-State Cooperative Societies registered with the Central Registrar. A member of a state-level society must look at their own state's Act and Rules.
**In how many days will the Information Officer reply?** — Within 30 days it will give the information or refuse with reasons (Section 106(3)); the information is limited to the bye-laws' disclosure norms.
**What to do if the information is not given?** — Appeal to the Co-operative Ombudsman in Form VII within one month of rejection; its decision is final (Section 106(4)).
**In how many days does the Ombudsman decide?** — Within three months of receiving the complaint (Section 85A(2)).
**Who conducts elections now?** — The Co-operative Election Authority (set up on 11 March 2024 — Ministry's Annual Report), not the sitting board.
**What is new in the board structure?** — At most 21 directors; in a society with individual members, the board has one SC/ST member and two women (Section 41(3)).
**What are a member's basic rights?** — Voting, information, share in profit, participation in the general body and grievance redressal.
**Why were these reforms brought?** — To fill, in an institutional way, the old weaknesses in election fairness, information transparency and grievance redressal.
**What should members of a state-level society do?** — Look up rights and complaint routes under their state's cooperative Act; this central arrangement does not apply to them.
**In which matters does the Ombudsman not hear complaints?** — This will be in the Rules (such as other proceedings already going on); read the conditions of Form VI before complaining.

## Three things to remember

- 🗳️ Board elections are now conducted not by the sitting management but by the independent Co-operative Election Authority.
- 📂 A member can ask the Co-operative Information Officer for information (reply in 30 days) — if rejected, appeal to the Ombudsman within one month.
- 🌍 This arrangement applies only to Multi-State Cooperative Societies (under CRCS), not to state-level societies — first identify your society's law.

## In the end

For Multi-State Cooperative Societies, the 2023 amendments have given members far more power than before — fair elections (Election Authority), transparent information (Information Officer) and a forum for complaints (Ombudsman). These changes are concrete steps towards putting the basic cooperative principle — "the member is the owner" — into practice. But remember the biggest point: the law only gives tools; real change comes when members are aware, use their rights and take an active part in the society's working. And also — this whole arrangement applies only to Multi-State societies; a member of a state-level society must look to their own state's Act.
