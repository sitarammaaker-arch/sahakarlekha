# The accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital

Why does the Balance Sheet always "tally"? The answer is hidden in one small equation — **Assets = Liabilities + Capital**. This **accounting equation** is the backbone of all double-entry accounting: once you understand it, the Balance Sheet stops looking frightening and becomes completely logical, and finding errors also becomes easy. This article explains it in simple language with examples, the extended form and a source.

## What the equation says

Everything the society has (**assets**) has come either from other people's money (**liabilities**) or from the members' own funds (**capital**).

> **Assets = Liabilities + Capital**

> 💡 That is why both sides of the Balance Sheet are always equal — this is not a coincidence but a rule. Behind every asset there is some "source".

## Understand the three parts

- **Asset:** valuable things the society has or is to receive — cash, bank, loans given to members (receivables), goods/stock, furniture, buildings. Simple test: "what we have or are to receive."
- **Liability:** amounts the society has to pay/return to others — members' deposits, loans taken from the bank/DCCB, outstanding expenses. Simple test: "what we have to return to someone else."
- **Capital (ownership):** the members' own funds — share capital + reserve fund (reserves) + accumulated surplus. Simple test: "what is our own." (See: [Share capital](/blog/share-capital-authorised-issued-paidup) and [Profit distribution and reserves](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves).)

## A simple example

Suppose the society has:

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Assets (cash + bank + loans) | ₹8,00,000 |
| Liabilities (members' deposits) | ₹5,00,000 |
| **Capital (balance)** | **₹3,00,000** |

Check: ₹5,00,000 + ₹3,00,000 = ₹8,00,000 ✓ — the equation tallies.

## Balance after every transaction — a running example

The equation holds not just at year-end but **after every single transaction**. See how (starting from zero):

| Transaction | Assets | = | Liabilities | + | Capital |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Members paid ₹3,00,000 share capital | +3,00,000 (cash) | = | 0 | + | +3,00,000 |
| 2. ₹5,00,000 deposits came in | +5,00,000 (cash) | = | +5,00,000 | + | — |
| 3. ₹4,00,000 loan given to a farmer | −4,00,000 cash, +4,00,000 loan | = | — | + | — |
| 4. ₹40,000 interest income received | +40,000 (cash) | = | — | + | +40,000 (income→capital) |
| **Total** | **8,40,000** | **=** | **5,00,000** | **+** | **3,40,000** |

After every row both sides stayed equal — 8,40,000 = 5,00,000 + 3,40,000 ✓. Note that in some transactions there is only an exchange within one side (as in transaction 3: cash went down but the member loan went up — both assets), yet total assets stayed the same and the equation balanced. This is what **double entry** gives us. (See: [Debit-credit and double entry](/blog/debit-credit-and-double-entry).)

## The extended equation — where do income and expenses go?

Income and expenses directly affect capital (income increases it, expenses reduce it). So the extended form is:

> **Assets = Liabilities + Capital + (Income − Expenses)**

- **Income** → capital increases (Cr nature).
- **Expense** → capital decreases (Dr nature).

**Example:** ₹40,000 interest income → capital increased by ₹40,000 as surplus; ₹8,000 salary expense → capital decreases by ₹8,000. At year-end this net surplus is shared to reserves/member benefit.

## This equation and the Balance Sheet

The Balance Sheet is the detailed form of this very equation — **assets** on one side, **liabilities and capital** on the other. Both sides are equal because the equation holds in every entry.

> ⚠️ If the Balance Sheet is not tallying, there is a **one-sided or wrong entry** somewhere — first check the [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained). (See: [How to read financial reports](/blog/how-to-read-financial-reports).)

**Sample (from the example above):**

| Liabilities + Capital | Amount | Assets | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Members' deposits (liability) | 5,00,000 | Cash/bank | 4,40,000 |
| Share capital + surplus (capital) | 3,40,000 | Member loans (receivable) | 4,00,000 |
| **Total** | **8,40,000** | **Total** | **8,40,000** |

Both sides are equal at ₹8,40,000 — this is the visible form of the equation.

## Common items of a society — where to count them

In practice the confusion is which item is an asset, which a liability, which capital. A quick look:

| Item | Type |
| --- | --- |
| Cash, bank | Asset |
| Loans given to members (receivable) | Asset |
| Stock/goods, furniture, buildings | Asset |
| Members' deposits | Liability |
| Loans taken from the bank/DCCB | Liability |
| Outstanding expenses (salary/bills payable) | Liability |
| Share capital | Capital |
| Reserve fund (reserves) and other funds | Capital |
| Accumulated surplus | Capital |

> 💡 A simple check: "Do we have this / are we to receive it?" → asset. "Do we have to return this to someone else?" → liability. "Is this our own?" → capital.

## Three forms of the equation

The same equation can be read in three ways — as needed:

- **Assets = Liabilities + Capital** (basic form).
- **Capital = Assets − Liabilities** (to find the members' real capital/net worth).
- **Liabilities = Assets − Capital** (to find total liabilities).

All three are three angles of the same truth — use the form that gives the information you need.

## How to find net worth

The society's real "capital", i.e. net worth = **Assets − Liabilities**.

**Example:** assets ₹8,40,000, liabilities ₹5,00,000 → net worth = ₹3,40,000. This is the members' own funds (share capital + reserves + surplus). Rising net worth is a sign of the society's strength; falling net worth is a worry.

## When the equation doesn't tally — an example

Suppose a deposit of ₹10,000 came in but by mistake only cash (asset) was increased, and "Members' deposits" (liability) was not recorded. Now assets are ₹10,000 higher, but liabilities are unchanged — the equation **will not tally** (and neither will the Balance Sheet). Correction: as soon as the member deposit of ₹10,000 is added to liabilities, the balance is restored. This is why recording **both sides** of every transaction is necessary.

## Parts of capital in a cooperative society

A society's "capital" is not just share capital — it includes several things:

- **Share capital** — shares taken by members.
- **Reserve Fund** — the compulsory part of profit.
- **Other funds** — education fund, building fund, etc.
- **Accumulated surplus** — the profit left over so far.

All of these together make up the society's "ownership" (net worth) — that is, what remains after subtracting liabilities from assets.

## Company vs society — the same equation

This equation applies to every organisation, only the name of "capital" changes:

- **In a company:** Assets = Liabilities + **Shareholders' equity** (share capital + reserves + retained earnings).
- **In a society:** Assets = Liabilities + **Members' capital** (share capital + reserves + surplus).

The principle is the same — the only difference is that in a society this "ownership" is shared by the members, not by outside investors. (See: [Cooperative society vs company](/blog/cooperative-vs-company-difference).)

## How the equation, double entry and the Trial Balance are related

All three are three forms of the same truth:

- **Double entry** records two sides (Dr=Cr) of every transaction.
- That is why the **accounting equation** stays balanced after every entry.
- And that is why both columns of the **Trial Balance** tally, and both sides of the **Balance Sheet**.

That is, a correct Dr/Cr → a balanced equation → a tallying Trial Balance → a tallying Balance Sheet. The whole chain is bound by one rule. (See: [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained).)

## Why the equation matters — benefits

- **Logic of the Balance Sheet:** you understand why both sides are equal.
- **Catching errors:** if it doesn't tally, it is a direct sign that an entry somewhere is incomplete/wrong.
- **Net worth:** assets − liabilities = the members' real capital — a measure of the society's health.
- **Foundation of every report:** the Income & Expenditure account and the Balance Sheet rest on it.
- **Confidence:** once this logic is clear, no new entry/report feels confusing.

## Using it day to day

Whenever a new transaction comes, just ask three questions:

1. Which accounts changed, and **what type** is each one (asset/liability/capital/income/expense)?
2. Did each one **increase or decrease**?
3. Did the equation stay equal on both sides?

If the answer to the third is "yes", the entry is correct. This habit saves you from big mistakes — and in software this check happens automatically.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Capital = liability"** — no; capital is the members' own funds, liabilities are payable to outsiders/depositors.
- **"Income is directly an asset"** — income increases capital; an asset increases when cash/a receivable comes in.
- **"The equation is only for year-end"** — no; it holds after every transaction.
- **"Balance Sheet tallied = everything is right"** — tallying is a necessary condition, but not the only one (some mistakes can still stay hidden).
- **"The reserve is a separate cash box"** — no; the reserve is a part of capital (a source), not separately kept cash — this misconception is common.

## Three things to remember

- ⚖️ Assets = Liabilities + Capital — always.
- 🔁 It holds after every transaction (thanks to double entry).
- 🧾 If it doesn't tally, there is a one-sided/wrong entry somewhere — start with the Trial Balance.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the accounting equation?** — Assets = Liabilities + Capital; it holds in every entry.
**Are capital and liabilities the same?** — No; capital is the members' own funds, liabilities are payable to outsiders/depositors.
**Why does the Balance Sheet tally?** — Because this equation holds in every entry — both sides are equal.
**Where are income and expenses in the equation?** — In the extended form: Assets = Liabilities + Capital + (Income − Expenses); income increases capital, expenses reduce it.
**How is net worth calculated?** — By subtracting liabilities from assets — this is the members' real capital.
**If the equation doesn't tally, what to check first?** — The Trial Balance; usually there is a one-sided or wrong-side entry.
**Are member deposits an asset or a liability?** — A liability; the society has to return it to the member, so it is counted as a liability.
**Share capital and reserves — are both capital?** — Yes; share capital, reserves/funds and surplus together make the society's total capital (net worth).
**What is the difference between the equation and the Balance Sheet?** — The equation is a rule; the Balance Sheet is the presentation of that rule on a particular date (assets vs liabilities+capital).
**Is rising net worth good?** — Yes; a rising net worth (assets − liabilities) shows the society's strength, a fall is a sign of worry.
**Does this equation also apply to a company?** — Yes; to every organisation — in a company "shareholders' equity" takes the place of "capital", the principle is the same.
**Is the reserve fund an asset or capital?** — Capital (a source); it is not separately kept cash, but a part of the members' ownership.
**If there is a change within one side, does the equation break?** — No; e.g. giving a loan by reducing cash (both assets) — total assets stay the same, the equation stays equal.

## In the end

This one equation is the whole secret of the Balance Sheet. Where did the assets come from — from other people's money (liabilities) or from the members' own (capital) — this is all it tells you, and it stays equal after every transaction. Identify every item as one of the three (asset/liability/capital), record both sides of every transaction, and the equation will stay balanced on its own. Once you understand this, reading reports, assessing net worth and finding errors — all three become easy, and the Balance Sheet looks logical, not frightening.
