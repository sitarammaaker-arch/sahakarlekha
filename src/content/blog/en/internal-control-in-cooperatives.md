# Internal Control: how to prevent errors and fraud

A good society does not rely on the audit alone — it **stops its own mistakes every day**. This is called **internal control**. This article explains in simple language how a few simple habits reduce both errors and fraud — what they are, why they matter, how to put them in place, and which warning signs to watch for.

## What internal control is
These are the **internal arrangements** that make sure every transaction is correct, authorised and recorded — without waiting for the auditor to come. The aim: catch errors early and reduce the room for wrongdoing.

In simple words, internal control is a "safety net" that works every day. The audit checks once a year, from outside; internal control keeps watch on every transaction, from inside. So in a society with strong internal controls, the year-end audit stays easy on its own — because most problems have already been caught and corrected. (See: [How a cooperative audit works](/blog/how-cooperative-society-audit-works).)

## Why controls matter — three reasons

- **Preventing errors:** It is natural for people to slip up; controls catch the slip early and stop it from becoming a big tangle, before it spoils the whole chain of reports.
- **Preventing fraud:** Where one person holds all the money and the records, the room for wrongdoing grows; controls reduce that opportunity and make dishonesty harder.
- **Building trust:** A transparent system strengthens the trust of everyone — members, the auditor and the Registrar — and the society's reputation grows.

That is why internal control should be seen not as "extra work" but as a basic condition for the society's health. And remember — control is not distrust of anyone; it protects everyone: it also shields an honest employee from baseless allegations, because every transaction has a clear record and approval. That is why good employees also welcome strong controls.

## Three types of control

To understand them, look at controls in three ways:

- **Preventive:** these stop a problem **before** it happens — such as dual approval on large payments, or access limits.
- **Detective:** these **catch** a problem that has happened — such as bank reconciliation, cash counts, checks on unusual figures and surprise inspections.
- **Corrective:** these **fix** a problem that has been caught — such as a correction entry, changing a weak link, or fixing responsibility.

In a good system all three work together — prevent first, catch it if it is not prevented, and fix it once caught. Of these, preventive control is the cheapest and most effective, because not letting a problem happen at all is always better than catching and fixing it later. So pay attention first to strong preventive controls (approvals, access limits).

## Four basic controls
1. **Maker–Checker (one prepares, another approves):** One person makes the entry, another approves large payments/changes. No single person should have full control.
2. **Segregation of duties:** The person who holds cash, the person who writes the books and the person who gives approval should be different.
3. **Regular reconciliation:** Reconcile bank, cash and stock from time to time — so any difference is caught at once.
4. **Access control and audit trail:** Each person gets only as much authority as needed; and a record of who changed what.

> 💡 A plain rule: "Trust is good, but a system is better." Even an honest team is safer with the right controls — and no room for suspicion is left.

### 1. Maker–Checker — two pairs of eyes, fewer mistakes

When one person makes an entry and another checks and approves it, there are two benefits — a mistake is caught by the second pair of eyes, and no single person can do wrong alone. For example, the secretary prepares the voucher, and payments above a set limit are approved by the chairman/treasurer. This small two-step process stops most big problems.

### 2. Segregation of duties — power should not be in one hand

Fraud is easiest when one person holds the cash, writes the books, and also gives the approval — because then they can commit the wrong and hide it too. So divide these three jobs among different people. Where this is not possible in a small society, the gap is filled by regular audit, surprise cash counts and the chairman's oversight. (See: [Roles of office-bearers](/blog/office-bearers-chairman-secretary-treasurer).)

### 3. Regular reconciliation — catch problems early

Reconciliation is the strongest tool of detective control: matching the bank book with the statement (BRS), cash with the physical count, and stock with the godown. The sooner (monthly) this is done, the sooner a missed entry or problem comes to light — months later the same small difference becomes a big tangle. (See: [Bank reconciliation (BRS) guide](/blog/bank-reconciliation-guide) and [Cash handling and verification](/blog/cash-handling-and-verification).)

### 4. Access control and audit trail — who can do what

Each person should get only the access their role needs — the clerk for entries, the chairman for approvals, and so on. And there should be a record of every change (audit trail) — who changed what, and when. This not only makes wrongdoing harder, but also gives an instant answer to any question. This is the "thread of accountability" that ties every transaction to the person responsible for it. (See: [Data security and backup](/blog/data-security-and-backup).)

## Where fraud grows

Fraud usually grows when three things come together — **opportunity** (weak controls), **pressure** (financial need), and **rationalisation** (telling oneself that "no one is watching"). Internal controls reduce the most controllable link among these — **opportunity**. When access is limited, there are two pairs of eyes, and reconciliation is regular, very little chance of wrongdoing is left. That is why strong controls protect honest people from suspicion and also block the room for dishonesty.

## Warning signs (red flags)

Stop and check these — they can be signs of weak controls or wrongdoing:

- 🚩 One person handling cash, books and approval — all three.
- 🚩 Bank/cash reconciliation not done for months.
- 🚩 Repeated suspense/mismatched balances that are not getting resolved.
- 🚩 Large payments made without documents or without proper approval.
- 🚩 Vouchers being erased/changed (instead of cancelled).
- 🚩 Passwords/access shared with everyone.
- 🚩 One person handling sensitive work for a long time without leave/checks.

## An example — how control protects

Suppose in a society one person keeps the cash, prepares the vouchers, and also does the bank reconciliation. Now if they make a fake receipt of ₹10,000 and take the money, with no second pair of eyes this can stay hidden for months — and since they also do the bank reconciliation, the difference will be buried too.

Now look at the same situation with controls: the secretary prepares the voucher, the chairman approves large amounts, the treasurer holds the cash, and a third person does the bank reconciliation. Here, making a fake receipt is itself hard, and even if one is made it will be caught at once in reconciliation. This is the difference — a single change (segregation of duties) changes the whole picture.

## Reviewing controls — once a year

Controls are not something to set up once and forget — over time the team, technology and work change, so the controls must too. At least once a year the managing committee should look at — which role is with whom, whether access is right, whether reconciliation is happening regularly, and whether any weakness has formed. This short review keeps the controls "alive", instead of letting them become paper rules.

## A small society can do it too
Even without a big team: the secretary makes entries, the chairman/treasurer approves large payments; bank reconciliation once a month; bills attached to large vouchers. This minimum control saves a lot. (See: [Roles of the chairman/secretary/treasurer](/blog/office-bearers-chairman-secretary-treasurer).)

In a small society the biggest challenge is "segregation of duties", because there are few people. But the answer is — make up for what you cannot separate with **oversight**: the chairman does surprise cash counts from time to time, the managing committee looks at the accounts monthly, and there is a regular audit. So where "separation" is not possible, double the "oversight".

## A minimum control checklist

Every society should do at least this much:

- ☐ Approval by two people for payments above a set limit.
- ☐ Match cash with a physical count daily; bank reconciliation (BRS) every month.
- ☐ A bill/document and a clear narration with every large voucher.
- ☐ Do not erase a wrong voucher, cancel it (so the audit trail survives).
- ☐ Separate, role-based access for every user; never share passwords.
- ☐ Physical count of stock from time to time and matching with the system.
- ☐ Regular backup.
- ☐ Surprise checks from time to time by a senior/the chairman.

Going through this checklist every month is the easiest "control habit" — in just a few minutes the society's safety position becomes clear, and any weakness is caught before it grows big.

## Control made easy with software
Role-based access (roles), Maker–Checker approval, an automatic audit trail and reports that tie with each other — these come built into software made for cooperatives, so control no longer remains manual effort. For example — an unbalanced entry cannot be saved at all, a large amount does not move ahead without approval, and every change is logged automatically. This reduces the room for human error, and control stays not something to "remember" but something "built into the system".

## Common weaknesses — and the fix

- **Everything on one person** → divide duties or increase oversight.
- **Putting off reconciliation** → treat monthly BRS and cash counts as compulsory.
- **Not keeping documents** → keep the bill/approval for every large transaction.
- **Shared passwords** → separate, role-based access for every user.
- **Erasing vouchers** → always cancel, keep the audit trail. (Also: [10 common mistakes](/blog/common-accounting-mistakes).)

## Frequently asked questions
**What is internal control?** — Everyday arrangements that prevent errors and wrongdoing, even before the audit.
**What is Maker–Checker?** — One person makes the entry, another approves it — so that no single person holds full control.
**How do you apply it in a small society?** — Divide roles, get large payments approved, reconcile regularly; where separation is not possible, increase oversight.
**How is it different from audit?** — Audit is once a year, from outside; internal control is daily, from inside.
**What are the types of control?** — Preventive (that stop), detective (that catch) and corrective (that fix).
**Why does segregation of duties matter?** — So that cash, books and approval are not in the same hands, which makes hiding wrongdoing hard.
**How is fraud stopped?** — Mainly by reducing "opportunity" — through limited access, two pairs of eyes and regular reconciliation.
**Does an honest team also need controls?** — Yes; controls prevent errors and also protect honest people from suspicion.
**What are red flags?** — Everything on one person, reconciliation being put off, repeated mismatched balances, large payments without approval, erasing vouchers, shared passwords.
**When should controls be reviewed?** — At least once a year the managing committee should check the position of roles, access and reconciliation.
**Which control matters most?** — No single one; but segregation of duties + regular reconciliation together make the biggest difference.

## Conclusion

Internal control is nothing complex or costly — just a few simple, daily habits: two pairs of eyes (Maker–Checker), division of power, regular reconciliation, and an audit trail with limited access. In a society that adopts these, errors are caught early, the room for fraud shrinks, and the audit goes smoothly. "Trust is good, but a system is better" — this is the essence of internal control, and also the mark of a healthy, trustworthy cooperative institution.
