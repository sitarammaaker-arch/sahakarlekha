# Ledger and posting: from entry to balance

The voucher is made — but how does it reach the "ledger", and how is each account's balance formed? The answer is **posting**. Once you understand **ledger and posting**, you can always answer "where did this number come from" — which is the mark of reliable accounts. This article connects the ledger, accounts, posting and balancing in simple language, with examples and sources.

## What is a Ledger

The ledger is the main book in which all the society's **accounts** are kept. Open any account — cash, bank, salary, interest income, or a member — and all its entries and its current balance show together. You can call it "the master book of accounts".

> 💡 The journal/vouchers record transactions in the order they *happen*; the ledger collects them *account by account*.

## The full flow — from voucher to report

Accounting is a chain, and the ledger is its middle stop:

1. **Voucher/journal** — every transaction recorded with Dr=Cr. (See: [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).)
2. **Posting** — the same entry goes into the relevant **ledger accounts**.
3. **Balancing** — the closing balance of every account is worked out.
4. **Trial Balance** — all balances in one place, to match Dr=Cr. (See: [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained).)
5. **Final accounts** — Profit & Loss and the Balance Sheet.

That is, the ledger is where scattered transactions take an "account-wise" form, and the figures for every report come from here.

## Posting — taking the entry to the account

Posting means — carrying the voucher/journal entry into the relevant **accounts**, so that every account collects all its transactions and its balance comes out right. Every entry goes to two places — the Dr side of the account that was debited, and the Cr side of the account that was credited.

> 💡 Good news — in SahakarLekha posting happens **automatically**. You save the voucher, and it goes into the correct accounts at once — no need to post it by hand.

## Example — posting and balance of the cash account

Suppose the cash account has some entries for the month. A ledger account looks like a "T" — left is **Dr**, right is **Cr**:

| Date | Particulars (Dr) | ₹ | Date | Particulars (Cr) | ₹ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Opening balance | 20,000 | 5 | Salary paid | 8,000 |
| 3 | Share capital received | 30,000 | 12 | Loan given | 25,000 |
| 10 | Interest received | 5,000 | | | |
| | **Total** | **55,000** | | **Total** | **33,000** |

**Work out the balance:** Dr total ₹55,000 − Cr total ₹33,000 = **₹22,000 (Dr balance)**. This is the closing balance of cash, which will become next month's opening. (In traditional language this is called "balance c/d", and next month "balance b/d".)

> 💡 The balance is on the side whose total is bigger — cash/assets usually have a Dr balance.

## Example — one member's ledger account

In a cooperative society the most useful ledger is the **member-wise account**. Suppose this is the loan account of member "Ramprasad":

| Date | Particulars | Dr (loan/interest) ₹ | Cr (recovery) ₹ | Balance ₹ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 April | Loan given | 50,000 | — | 50,000 Dr |
| 30 June | Interest charged | 2,000 | — | 52,000 Dr |
| 15 Oct | Instalment recovered | — | 20,000 | 32,000 Dr |

Now you know at a glance that ₹32,000 is outstanding against Ramprasad — this is the strength of the member ledger. With such an account for every member, recovery, dues and NPA all stay clear.

## Day Book vs Ledger

- **Day Book:** All transactions in order of **date** — "what all happened today".
- **Ledger:** The same transactions grouped by **account** — "what is the balance of this account".

| Question | Where to look |
| --- | --- |
| What all happened today? | Day Book |
| What is the balance of this account? | Ledger |
| Full history of a member/account? | Ledger account |

> ⚠️ The Day Book and the ledger are not the same thing — one is date-wise, the other account-wise; both are two views of the same entry.

## Journal vs Ledger

- **Journal (book of prime entry):** Every transaction is first recorded here with Dr/Cr, in order of date — the "original entry".
- **Ledger (principal book):** Those journal entries are grouped account by account, so that balances are formed.

In simple words — **write in the journal, carry (post) to the ledger**. The journal tells "what happened when" date-wise, the ledger "how much in which account" account-wise. Both are two forms of the same entry. In modern software the voucher itself does the work of the journal, and the ledger is formed automatically — so you do not have to post separately.

## Types of ledger — general and subsidiary

- **General Ledger:** The main collection of all the society's accounts.
- **Subsidiary Ledger:** Where there are very many sub-accounts, a separate subsidiary book — like the **member-wise account** (each member's loan/deposit/shares), or supplier-wise.

The total of the subsidiary ledger should match the "control account" in the general ledger — this is one more internal check. (See: [Member and share accounting](/blog/member-and-share-accounting).)

## Control Account — the check on the subsidiary ledger

When there are hundreds of members, each member's separate account stays in the subsidiary ledger, and the general ledger has only one **control account** (like "Member loans — control"), which shows the total of all.

**Check:** sum of the balances of all member accounts = balance of the control account. If these do not match, some posting has been missed/is wrong somewhere. This is a powerful internal-control check.

> 💡 Example: if the total loan balance of 200 members is ₹40 lakh, then the "Member loans control" account in the general ledger should also be exactly ₹40 lakh — if there is a difference, some posting has been missed or doubled somewhere; start the check from there.

## Steps of posting (manually)

1. From the voucher/journal, identify **which account is Dr and which is Cr**.
2. Post the amount and particulars on the **left (Dr)** side of the Dr account.
3. Post on the **right (Cr)** side of the Cr account.
4. Write the **cross-reference** of the source (date/voucher number) in both.
5. At the end of the month/year, **work out the balance** of every account.

> 💡 In digital software all five steps happen automatically on one "Save".

## How to check a balance

Doubt about some figure? Directly open that **ledger account** — every entry and its balance will be in front of you. The order of checking:

1. Open the **ledger** of the suspect account.
2. Look at the **source voucher** of each entry (date, amount, particulars).
3. Match the addition/subtraction and the balance.

This is the first step of audit and of finding irregularities — and in software, "drill-down" from every figure to its voucher is available in one click, so "which entries make up this balance" becomes clear at once. This transparency builds the trust of both members and the auditor.

## Benefits of the ledger

- **Correct balance of every account:** The history and current position of every account in one place.
- **Foundation of reports:** Trial Balance, Profit & Loss and Balance Sheet are all made from the ledger.
- **Accountability:** Every figure can be linked to its voucher — nothing is "in the air".
- **Recovery/dues clear:** The member-wise ledger tells at once who owes how much.
- **Easier audit:** The auditor checks directly from the ledger, so objections are fewer and resolved faster.

## Manual vs digital posting

- **Manual:** Carrying every voucher by hand into every account — it takes time, and there is room for mistakes of wrong account/wrong side/addition.
- **Digital (like SahakarLekha):** Posting happens automatically as soon as the voucher is saved, both sides correct, and drill-down from every figure to the voucher. The room for mistakes drops a lot, and the ledger always stays up to date.

> 💡 This is why digital double entry is the most reliable for serious, audit-ready accounts.

## Common ledger accounts in a society

A cooperative society's ledger usually has these accounts:

- **Assets:** Cash, bank (each bank separate), member loans, stock, furniture/building.
- **Liabilities:** Member deposits (savings/fixed), borrowings from the DCCB/bank, outstanding expenses.
- **Capital/funds:** Share capital, reserve fund, education fund, surplus.
- **Income:** Interest income, commission, membership fee, other income.
- **Expenses:** Salary, rent, stationery, interest expense, repairs, depreciation.

Every transaction touches two (or more) of these accounts — and posting takes it to the right place, so that every account keeps a correct, up-to-date balance. (See: [Debit-credit and double entry](/blog/debit-credit-and-double-entry).)

## Common mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Posting to the wrong account** — like rent into salary. Avoid: choose the correct account in the voucher.
- **One-sided posting** — only Dr or only Cr. Avoid: always both sides (automatic in software).
- **Working out the balance wrongly** — wrong total of the sides. Avoid: add again, or rely on software.
- **Subsidiary and control account not matching** — member-wise total different from the main one. Avoid: reconcile from time to time.
- **Not writing the cross-reference** — hard to find the source later. Avoid: keep the voucher reference in every posting (automatic in software).
- **Picking up the opening balance wrongly** — last year's c/d not becoming the correct b/d. Avoid: take the next opening only after the year is closed.

## Three things to remember

- 📒 Ledger = master book of all the society's accounts (account-wise).
- 🔄 Posting = from entry to account (automatic in software, both sides).
- 🔍 To check a balance, open the ledger account → go to its entries and source voucher.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is posting?** — Carrying the voucher/journal entry into the relevant accounts in the ledger, so that every account's balance comes out right.
**Will I have to do posting myself?** — No; in double-entry software it happens automatically as soon as the voucher is saved.
**What is the difference between the ledger and the Day Book?** — The ledger is account-wise (for balances), the Day Book date-wise (for the day's activity).
**How is the Trial Balance made from the ledger?** — The closing balance of every ledger account is picked up and a Dr/Cr list is made — that is the Trial Balance.
**What is a subsidiary ledger?** — A separate book where there are many sub-accounts (like member-wise); its total should match the main control account.
**How to find the source of a number?** — Open its ledger account and go from the entry → source voucher (drill-down).
**What does a control account do?** — It shows the total of a subsidiary ledger (like member-wise); the sum of all sub-accounts should match the control account — this is a check.
**How is it decided whether an account's balance is Dr or Cr?** — The balance is on the side whose total is bigger; usually Dr for cash/assets, Cr for income/liabilities.
**What do b/d and c/d mean?** — c/d = this period's closing balance (carried down); b/d = the same as the next period's opening (brought down).
**What is the difference between the journal and the ledger?** — In the journal a transaction is first recorded (date-wise); in the ledger the same is carried (posted) account-wise so that balances are formed.
**Why is a member-wise ledger necessary?** — Each member's loan/deposit/dues show separately, which keeps recovery and NPA clear.

## In the end

Once you understand ledger and posting, you can always answer "where did the number come from". Voucher → posting → balance → Trial Balance → final accounts — remember this chain. If in doubt, open the ledger account and go to every entry and its source voucher, keep reconciling the member-wise and control accounts from time to time, and pick up the opening/closing correctly. In digital software all this happens automatically, but understanding the principle lets you trust every figure fully — this is the real mark of transparent, audit-ready accounts.
