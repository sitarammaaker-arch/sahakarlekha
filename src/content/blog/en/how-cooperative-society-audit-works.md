# How a Cooperative Society's Audit Works

For every cooperative society an **annual audit is mandatory** — but how does it actually happen? Who does it, in what order, and what do you get at the end? This article explains **how a cooperative society's audit works** — why, who does it, the process, what the auditor checks, settling objections and classification — all in simple language. (The preparation checklist is separate — see [Audit preparation](/blog/audit-preparation-checklist).)

## What an audit is

An audit simply means a fair check of the society's books by an independent person (the auditor), to confirm that the accounts are correct, the rules were followed, and money was used in the right place. It is not an exercise in "catching mistakes", but a system that gives members confidence that their society is being run honestly. So do not see the audit as an enemy, see it as the society's yearly health check — like a regular check-up of the body.

## Why the audit is mandatory
A cooperative society runs on its members' money, so every year there is an independent check that the accounts are correct, the rules were followed, and money was used in the right place. This is the backbone of transparency and trust, and it is required under the state cooperative Act.

In an ordinary company the audit protects shareholders' interests; in a cooperative society it is even more important, because here the owners and the customers are often the same people (members), and the society may also be linked to government aid/schemes. That is why a cooperative audit checks not only financial right and wrong, but rule compliance, the general body, elections and fund management — everything. It is a "statutory audit", that is, mandatory by law, not voluntary. (Legal framework: [Framework of cooperative law](/blog/cooperative-law-framework).)

## Who does the audit
The auditor is generally appointed/approved by the **Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RCS)** — a departmental auditor, or a chartered accountant on the panel (as per state rules). The society cannot keep any auditor of its own choice.

This system exists so that the audit stays truly independent — if the society chose its own auditor, its fairness could be questioned. An auditor appointed from outside can check without pressure, and this is the guarantee of members' trust. The auditor's job is not to put the society down, but to place the fair truth before everyone.

## The process — broadly
1. **Appointment** — the auditor is fixed by the RCS/panel.
2. **Checking** — books, vouchers, bank reconciliation, stock, member records, compliance (GST/TDS/returns) are checked.
3. **Objections** — an audit objection is recorded for any irregularity/shortcoming.
4. **Audit report** — findings, objections and recommendations.
5. **Classification** — the society gets a grade (A/B/C/D type). (See [Audit classification](/blog/cooperative-audit-classification).)
6. **Rectification** — the society gives its reply/rectification on the objections to the RCS.

> 💡 The audit is not for "catching", it is for **correcting** — a society that keeps clean records has a smooth and quick audit.

## What the auditor actually checks

During the check the auditor mainly looks at:

- **Books and vouchers:** Is there a clear description and supporting document for every big transaction? (See: [Voucher narration and documents](/blog/voucher-narration-and-documents).)
- **Bank reconciliation:** Has a BRS been done for every bank account, and do the book balances match the statements?
- **Cash:** Does the physical cash (count) match the book balance?
- **Trial Balance and reports:** Does Dr=Cr match, and is there no suspense?
- **Member/share records:** Does the register total match the share capital balance in the books?
- **Compliance:** Were the annual return and GST/TDS done on time, and were the general body and elections held on time as per rules?
- **Funds:** Were the reserve fund and profit distribution done in the right order and as per rules?

All of these being correct is the key to a "clean audit" — and all of them come automatically from daily discipline, not from a year-end rush.

## Types of audit objections

Not every objection is equally serious. Broadly:

- **Minor (procedural):** Such as incomplete narration on a voucher, or a missing receipt — easily corrected.
- **Serious (financial):** Such as mismatched balances, big missing documents, or breach of rules — needs a detailed reply and correction.
- **Very serious:** Signs of embezzlement/fraud — these can lead to strict action, and the matter can go to inquiry/legal proceedings.

Do not panic when an objection comes — replying to every objection on time, with documents, is what rectification means. Also remember that most objections are minor or procedural, and can be prevented from next time. So treat every audit as a chance to learn — do not repeat next year the objection that came this year. This thinking makes the society better year after year, and within a few years the audit becomes almost objection-free.

## How clean books make the audit easier
- A bill/receipt with every voucher, bank reconciliation on time, a Trial Balance that matches.
- Audit-ready reports (Trial Balance, Balance Sheet, Income & Expenditure and R&P) in a single click.

## Audit trail — the backbone of the audit

An audit trail means a path to reach the root of every figure. For example: a balance in the Balance Sheet → which ledger it came from → which voucher → which document. When this chain is complete and clear, the auditor can confirm any figure in minutes. That is why it is essential to cancel a voucher, not erase it — so the trail does not break. A digital system keeps a log of every change, so the audit trail keeps building itself, which is difficult in manual books.

## Preparation — before the audit

To make the audit smooth, keep these ready in advance:

- All books up to date and matching each other (Trial Balance, cash, bank).
- Documents for every big voucher filed in voucher order.
- Final accounts (Balance Sheet, Income & Expenditure, R&P) ready on time.
- Member/share register and fund records fully up to date.
- Rectification of previous audit objections complete and documented.

The better this preparation, the quicker and calmer the audit. (Detailed list: [Audit preparation checklist](/blog/audit-preparation-checklist).)

## The member's role

The audit is not just a matter between the auditor and the secretary — members also have a role in it. The audit report is placed before the general body, where every member can ask questions on it and seek clarification. This is the strongest tool of transparency — because when office-bearers know they must give account before members every year, discipline stays all year. So members too should be active in reading the audit report and asking questions — this is the real strength of cooperative democracy.

## Audit in Haryana: three routes of appointment and the time limit
Under Chapter XI (Sections 81-95) of the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act, 1984, the auditor can be fixed in three ways — the society itself appoints one as per its bye-laws (Section 81), the Registrar gives a direction (Section 82), or if the society fails, the Registrar appoints one himself (Section 84). Under **Rule 79** it is mandatory to prepare the Balance Sheet within just **3 months** of the end of the financial year.

> 💡 Section 85: Discussing the audit report in the general body and replying to objections is mandatory — this gives members the legal right to ask questions directly. Section 86: The auditor can also be removed by a 2/3 majority — the appointment is not permanent.

## Digital audit: the national PACS ERP and the Maker-Checker system
Under the central government's national PACS computerisation project (implemented by NABARD), a common national ERP software has been built, with 22 modules including loans, deposits, accounts, member management and audit. Its audit module rests on **"Maker-Checker" control** — that is, every transaction has to pass verification at several levels before final approval, so an irregularity can be caught at the time of the transaction itself, without waiting for the yearly audit.

> 💡 This is the biggest difference between the old manual system and a digital audit — Maker-Checker control stops an irregularity before it happens or right after, not in a yearly audit years later. In software like SahakarLekha too, the same principle applies in the form of roles & permissions.

## Rectification — after the objection

The audit report is not the end, it is a new beginning. The society has to reply to every objection recorded in the report — correct things where correction is needed, and reply with documents where an explanation is enough. This rectification report has to be given to the RCS within the set time. Timely, honest rectification improves the next audit grading; putting it off weighs even heavier in the next objection. So start work on the objections as soon as the report arrives, do not wait for the last moment.

## Effect of the grade after the audit

At the end of the audit the society gets a classification (grade), which shows its financial health and compliance. A good grade raises its standing — loans and support from the bank/federation become easier; a poor grade does the opposite. So the grade is not just a letter, it is a measure of the society's reputation. (In detail: [Audit classification (A/B/C/D)](/blog/cooperative-audit-classification).)

## Audit vs internal control

The yearly audit is an external check once a year, while **internal control** (like Maker-Checker, division of duties, monthly reconciliation) is the daily defence. The two complement each other — in a society with good internal control the year-end audit is automatically easy, because irregularities have already been caught and corrected. (See: [Internal control](/blog/internal-control-in-cooperatives).)

## Frequently asked questions
**How does a cooperative society's audit work?** — An RCS-appointed auditor checks the books, records objections, and gives a report and classification; the society settles the objections.
**Who appoints the auditor?** — The RCS/panel (as per state rules), not the society itself.
**When does the audit happen?** — Generally after every financial year, yearly.
**What if an objection comes?** — Correct things on time and give the rectification report to the RCS.
**What does the auditor check?** — Books, vouchers, bank reconciliation, cash, member records, compliance and funds.
**Why does the audit grade matter?** — It shows the society's standing; a good grade makes loans/support easier.
**Can members question the audit report?** — Yes; discussion of the report in the general body and replies to objections are members' right.
**What is the difference between audit and internal control?** — Audit is a yearly external check; internal control is the daily defence — the two complement each other.
**What is an audit trail?** — The path to reach the root of every figure (report → ledger → voucher → document).
**What should be kept ready before the audit?** — Up-to-date, matching books, documents in order, final accounts and rectification of previous objections.
**What is a rectification report?** — The society's correction/reply on audit objections, given to the RCS within the set time.
**Is the auditor permanent?** — No; appointment is as per rules and the auditor can be changed/removed.

## Conclusion

A cooperative audit is a straightforward process — appointment, checking, objection, report, classification and rectification. Do not treat it as something to fear; keep clean books daily, attach a document to every voucher, reconcile monthly, and if an objection comes, settle it on time. Do just this and the audit becomes smooth, quick and something that raises your society's standing.
