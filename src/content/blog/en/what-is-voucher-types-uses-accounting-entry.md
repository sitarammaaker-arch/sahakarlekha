# What is a voucher? Types, uses and an accounting entry example

For example, suppose the auditor asked about a payment of ₹18,500, "Show me its voucher." The secretary put the electrician's handwritten bill in front of him. The auditor said, "This is a bill, where is the voucher?" The secretary thought both were the same thing. In fact they are not, and this confusion is very common in cooperative societies.

A bill comes from outside. **A voucher is made by the society itself in its own books.** The bill is its proof; the voucher is the entry that tells what effect the transaction had on which account. This article clears up the whole basics of the voucher: what a voucher is, what it contains, how many types there are, which one to choose when, and an example entry for each type.

> 📘 **In short:** A voucher is the written accounting record of a single transaction: date, which account is Dr, which is Cr, amount, description (narration) and supporting document. In accounting, almost every voucher falls into these types: **receipt, payment, journal, contra**, and in trading societies **sales, purchase, debit note, credit note**. Money came in: receipt; went out: payment; between cash and bank: contra; everything else, an adjustment: journal. Every figure in the books, that is, the cash book, ledger, Trial Balance and final accounts, is built from vouchers.

The *method of filling in* a voucher, that is, which button, which fields, step by step, is in [How to make a voucher entry](/blog/voucher-entry-guide). This article is its **concept introduction**: first understand what a voucher is, then go there and learn to fill it in.

## What is a voucher?

A **voucher** is the accounting record in which **one transaction** is recorded: its date, which account was **debited (Debit, Dr)** and which was **credited (Credit, Cr)**, how much, and the reason (narration). Usually some **supporting document** (bill, receipt, resolution) is also attached.

Think of it this way: the books are a book, and the voucher is not each page but **each line** of it. A thousand transactions means a thousand vouchers. No figure should come into the books without a voucher.

### Voucher vs bill, receipt, challan

| Thing | Who makes it | What it is | Relation to the voucher |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bill / invoice** | The seller (outside party) | "You took this much goods/service, this much is payable" | Supporting document |
| **Challan / delivery note** | The sender | Proof of delivery of goods | Supporting document |
| **Receipt** | The one who takes the money | "We received this much money" | Supporting document; also made from the society's own receipt book |
| **Bank slip / statement** | Bank | Proof of deposit/withdrawal in the bank | Supporting document |
| **Voucher** | **The society itself** | The **entry** in the books based on all these | The one that goes into the books |

That is, the bill, challan, receipt and bank slip are all **proof**, and the voucher is the society's **own entry** based on that proof. Where there is a bill but no voucher, the account has not been posted in the books. Where there is a voucher but no bill, there is no proof. Both are needed. (How to attach proof is in [Voucher narration and documents](/blog/voucher-narration-and-documents).)

## What does a voucher contain?

Whatever the type, a complete voucher has these parts:

| Part | What | Why necessary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Voucher number** | Serial, without gaps or duplicates | Check of completeness and order in the audit |
| **Date** | Actual date of the transaction | To be posted in the right period and the right financial year |
| **Voucher type** | Receipt, payment etc. | So reports show it in the right place |
| **Debit account** | The one debited | Expense up/asset up/liability down |
| **Credit account** | The one credited | Income up/liability up/asset down |
| **Amount** | In figures (and in words) | Both sides should be equal |
| **Description (Narration)** | From/to whom, under which head, based on which bill | Identification months later and audit |
| **Supporting document** | Bill/receipt/resolution | Proof |
| **Signature/approval** | The maker and the approver | Accountability |

A hypothetical sample (**payment voucher**):

| Item | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Voucher no. | PV-0142 |
| Date | 12-09-2026 |
| Dr | Electricity expense ₹18,500 |
| Cr | Bank account ₹18,500 |
| Narration | Office wiring, M/s Sharma Electricals, bill no. 311 dt. 10-09-26, cheque no. 004517 |
| Basis | Bill no. 311, cheque counterfoil |

One thing that is true of almost every voucher: **total Dr = total Cr.** This is the rule of double entry, whose basis is explained in detail in [Debit, credit and double entry](/blog/debit-credit-and-double-entry).

## Types of vouchers

From an accounting point of view there are four basic types, and some more in trading or stock-holding societies:

| Type | When it is made | Common examples (cooperative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Receipt** | Money **came in** | Share capital, loan recovery, interest, admission fee |
| **Payment** | Money **went out** | Salary, rent, electricity, payment to supplier |
| **Contra** | Money moved **between** cash ↔ bank | Cash deposited in bank, cash withdrawn from bank |
| **Journal** | Money neither came nor went; **adjustment** | Depreciation, outstanding expenses, corrections, transfers |
| **Sales** | Goods/service **sold** | Fertiliser, consumer goods, service charges |
| **Purchase** | Goods/service **bought** | Purchase of fertiliser, godown items |
| **Debit note** | Goods bought **returned** or lower price | Return to supplier |
| **Credit note** | Goods sold **came back** or price reduced | Return by customer/member |

Besides these, a **compound voucher** is not a separate "type" but a voucher that has **more than one Dr or Cr** line, such as salary from which TDS and EPF are also deducted. Most accounting software, including SahakarLekha, has a separate facility for it.

> 💡 In many societies, sales, purchase, debit note and credit note are not treated as separate types but are recorded within receipt/payment/journal. From an accounting point of view both ways work; what matters is that a society fixes its method and keeps it **always the same.**

## Which voucher when: five questions

When you can't tell which one to make, ask in this order:

1. **Did money actually come in?** (into cash/bank) → **Receipt**.
2. **Did money actually go out?** → **Payment**.
3. **Did money only move from cash to bank, or from bank to cash?** → **Contra**.
4. **Is it a deal in goods/services** (sold or bought, and it may be on credit too)? → **Sales/purchase** (or record them your way in journal/receipt/payment).
5. **Is it none of these, just an adjustment in the books** (depreciation, outstanding, correction)? → **Journal**.

A simple test: an entry that has **cash or bank** in it is usually a receipt, payment or contra. One that has neither is a journal.

## Accounting entry example for each type

*(All amounts are hypothetical, only for explanation.)*

| # | Event | Dr | Cr | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Receipt:** share capital received in cash from a member | Cash | Share capital | ₹5,000 |
| 2 | **Payment:** electricity bill paid by cheque | Electricity expense | Bank | ₹3,200 |
| 3 | **Contra:** cash deposited in bank | Bank | Cash | ₹40,000 |
| 4 | **Journal:** depreciation on furniture | Depreciation (expense) | Furniture (asset) | ₹5,000 |
| 5 | **Sales:** fertiliser sold for cash | Cash | Fertiliser sales (income) | ₹3,000 |
| 6 | **Purchase:** goods bought on credit | Purchases | Supplier (liability) | ₹40,000 |
| 7 | **Debit note:** goods worth ₹2,000 returned to supplier | Supplier | Purchase returns | ₹2,000 |
| 8 | **Credit note:** customer returned goods worth ₹1,500 | Sales returns | Customer | ₹1,500 |

**Compound voucher (salary, with TDS and EPF):** gross salary ₹20,000; TDS ₹1,000 and EPF ₹1,800 deducted; the rest paid in cash.

| Account | Dr ₹ | Cr ₹ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salary (expense) | 20,000 | — |
| TDS payable (liability) | — | 1,000 |
| EPF payable (liability) | — | 1,800 |
| Cash | — | 17,200 |
| **Total** | **20,000** | **20,000** |

In every row, total Dr and total Cr are equal. This is the check that the voucher was made correctly. (Their step-by-step form with cooperative examples: [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).)

> 🔍 **A misunderstanding:** "In a receipt voucher, cash will be Cr, because money came in." It is the opposite. If money came in, **cash is Dr** (the asset went up). The words Dr/Cr are decided not by "came in/went out" but by the *type* of account. This rule is explained in [the debit-credit article](/blog/debit-credit-and-double-entry).

## Uses of the voucher: who it serves

A voucher is not just a tool for "making entries". It is the unit from which everything else is built:

- **Books and reports:** every voucher is posted to the cash book/ledger, then to the Trial Balance, then to the final accounts (income and expenditure, Balance Sheet). If the voucher is right, everything is right. One wrong voucher spoils the whole chain. (The posting process: [Ledger and posting](/blog/ledger-and-posting-explained).)
- **Audit:** the auditor checks every figure in the books by tracing it to the voucher and its supporting document.
- **Accountability:** the signatures of both the maker and the approver on the voucher show who is responsible for which money.
- **Settling disputes:** in a dispute with a member or supplier, the voucher and its documents are the proof.
- **Control:** voucher-level approval, called Maker–Checker, is the most direct way to prevent wrongdoing. ([Internal control](/blog/internal-control-in-cooperatives).)

> 💡 **For the secretary/accountant:** The voucher is the society's "currency": all your work is recorded in it. So the most valuable habit is that every voucher is made **the same day**, serially, with a clear narration and proof. Societies that do this daily do not have to put in weeks of effort before the audit.

## The life cycle of a voucher

A voucher passes these stages in its life:

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Creation** | The voucher is made from the proof of the transaction: type, Dr/Cr, amount, narration |
| **2. Check/approval** | A second person looks at it; approval for large amounts where the society has decided so |
| **3. Posting** | It is posted in the books: cash book/ledger |
| **4. Effect on reports** | It shows in the Trial Balance and final accounts |
| **5. Safekeeping** | In a serial file with the proof |
| **6. If there is a mistake** | **Do not delete**, cancel it and make a correct new entry |

The sixth stage is the most important. Quietly deleting or overwriting a voucher counts among the biggest objections in the audit, because the record of "what happened" is also wiped out with it. On cancellation the voucher remains, it just shows as cancelled, and the audit trail stays safe.

## What the law says about vouchers — and what it does not

According to the texts of the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act, 1984 and Rules, 1989 that I read:

| Point | Source | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Auditor's access | Section 95(3) | Open access for the person auditing to books, papers, **vouchers**, stock and property |
| Access in inspection | Section 97(1) | Access for the Registrar/authorised person to all books, accounts, papers and **vouchers** |
| Form of books | Rule 78 | Account books and records in the form and manner specified by the Registrar |
| Custody of books and records | Section 49; Rule 36 | List of records and the custodian's signature |

**What I did not find in the law:** in the Act and Rules I did not find any separate definition of "voucher", any count of voucher types, or any compulsory format for a voucher. So in this article, voucher types and formats are **general accounting practice**, not a legal definition. If any special format of the Registrar or an instruction of the higher body applies to your society, that comes first. I have also not verified the statutory period for how many years vouchers should be kept; ask your Registrar/auditor about it.

## Common mistakes

1. **Treating the bill itself as the voucher.** The bill is proof; the entry is the voucher.
2. **A voucher without proof.** "We'll give the bill later" often never comes.
3. **Choosing the wrong type.** Payment instead of contra, which spoils the cash-bank account.
4. **Putting Dr/Cr the wrong way round.** Look at the account type, not "came in/went out".
5. **Vague narration.** "Misc. expenses ₹500" becomes an audit objection; "Stationery, bill #212" gives confidence.
6. **Deleting a voucher.** Cancel it, don't delete it.
7. **Gaps or duplicates in serial numbers.** This is the first thing seen in the audit.
8. **Wrong date or financial year.** This shakes the report figures.
9. **The maker is also the approver.** If Maker–Checker breaks, control breaks.
10. **Making vouchers later, from memory.** Risk of mistakes in all three: date, amount and reason.

In SahakarLekha, receipt, payment, journal and contra are made from the **Vouchers** page; sales and purchase vouchers are made from transactions on the **Sales** and **Purchases** pages, and types like debit/credit notes are also in the system. Entries with several Dr/Cr lines are also made in the **"Expert (multi-line)"** mode of the **Vouchers** page. **Day Book** shows all vouchers of each day in one place. That is, the portal carries the burden of Dr/Cr totalling, posting and reports, and your time goes into choosing the right type, writing a clear narration and attaching proof.

## Three things to remember

- 🧾 The voucher is the society's own book entry; the bill, receipt, challan and bank slip are its proof. Both are needed, and one does not replace the other.
- 🔀 Money came in: receipt; went out: payment; between cash and bank: contra; other adjustments: journal; and in every voucher total Dr = total Cr.
- 🚫 Do not delete a wrong voucher; cancel it and make a correct new entry. But no compulsory voucher format was found in the law; if the Registrar has an instruction, follow that.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a voucher?** — The accounting record of a single transaction, containing the date, Dr account, Cr account, amount, narration and usually a supporting document. Every figure in the books is built from vouchers.
**What is the difference between a voucher and a bill?** — A bill is made by the seller (outside party) and it is proof; a voucher is made by the society itself in its own books, and that is what gets posted in the books.
**How many types of vouchers are there?** — Four basic ones: receipt, payment, journal, contra. In trading societies also sales, purchase, debit note and credit note. One with several Dr/Cr lines is called a compound voucher.
**What is the difference between a receipt voucher and a payment voucher?** — In a receipt, money comes in (cash/bank Dr); in a payment, money goes out (cash/bank Cr).
**When is a contra voucher made?** — When money moves only between cash and bank, such as depositing cash in the bank or withdrawing cash from the bank.
**When is a journal voucher made?** — When there is no cash or bank transaction, such as depreciation, a provision for outstanding expenses or a correction entry.
**Should Dr and Cr be equal in every voucher?** — Yes. Total Dr and total Cr must always be equal; this is the rule of double entry.
**What to do with a wrong voucher?** — Do not delete it. Cancel it and make a correct new entry, so the audit trail remains.
**Does the law have any fixed format for a voucher?** — In the text of the Haryana Act 1984 and Rules 1989 that I read, I did not find a separate format or definition of a voucher. As per Rule 78, books are kept in the form specified by the Registrar; if there is any special instruction from him, follow that.
**How should vouchers be made in a PACS?** — As per the process and types of your PACS's prescribed portal (e-PACS). This article explains the concept; it does not replace that process.

## In the end

The voucher looks small, but the whole of accounting stands on it. A society that makes the voucher of every transaction on time, in the right type, with a clear narration and proof, finds its books become audit-ready on their own, and the secretary and accountant do not have to struggle to correct the books at year-end.
