# Cooperative Society Audit Preparation: A Complete Checklist

A statutory audit is compulsory in the cooperative sector, and every year this is the time when the secretary-accountant is under the most stress. But the truth is — if the accounts have been kept correctly all year, the audit becomes an easy job of a few days. This article gives a practical, detailed preparation checklist, including how to avoid common objections.

## Why does an audit happen?

An audit is an independent check of the society's accounts — it gives members and the cooperative department confidence that the accounts are correct, complete and as per rules. A good audit improves the society's **grading** and increases its future borrowing capacity.

An audit is not just a legal formality — it is a "health certificate" for the society. When an independent, qualified auditor certifies the accounts, members get confidence that their money has been handled properly, and apex institutions/banks get confidence that dealing with the society is safe. So the audit should be seen not as a burden, but as a measure of the society's credibility. (See: [How a cooperative audit works](/blog/how-cooperative-society-audit-works).)

## Types and levels of audit

A society faces several kinds of checks in its life — it is useful to know them:

- **Statutory Audit:** compulsory every year, under the Act — this is the main audit.
- **Internal Check:** the society does it itself through the year (such as monthly reconciliation) — this makes the audit easier.
- **Special/inquiry audit:** for a special reason or on a complaint, when needed.

Of these, preparation for the statutory audit matters most, and the checklist below focuses on it.

## Preparation checklist: documents to keep ready

- ✅ Up-to-date **Trial Balance** (Dr = Cr tallied).
- ✅ **Final accounts** — Trading Account, Income & Expenditure, Balance Sheet, Receipts & Payments.
- ✅ **Bank reconciliation statement** for every account, up to date.
- ✅ All **vouchers** in order, with bills/receipts attached.
- ✅ **Member, share, loan, stock and salary registers**.
- ✅ Copies of **GST/TDS returns** and challans.
- ✅ **Minutes** of general body/board meetings.
- ✅ Last year's **audit report and compliance on objections**.

> 💡 Keep building an "audit file" all year — every month's bank reconciliation, bills for big vouchers, and meeting minutes. At the end you will find everything in one place.

### Why each document is needed

- **Trial Balance:** the starting point of the audit — if it does not tally, the whole check gets stuck there.
- **Final accounts:** the picture of the society for the whole year; the auditor gives an opinion on these.
- **Bank reconciliation:** every bank balance tallied with the statement — without it the bank figures cannot be trusted.
- **Vouchers and bills:** proof of every entry; an entry without a bill is the most common objection.
- **Registers:** member/share/loan/stock/salary — these sub-ledgers must tally with the main accounts.
- **Returns/challans:** proof of compliance.
- **Minutes:** proof of authority for big decisions (for example, whether a big expense was approved or not).

## The most common audit objections — and how to avoid them

| Objection | Cause | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trial Balance not tallied | Wrong opening balance/entry | Reconcile every month |
| Voucher–entry mismatch | Haste, bill missing | Keep bills attached |
| Bank balance not tallied | Reconciliation not done | Do a monthly BRS |
| closing ≠ opening | Wrong year changeover | Close with FY lock |
| Double-counted stock | Closing stock taken twice | Keep a single calculation |

> ⚠️ Putting a mismatched amount into "suspense" postpones the problem, it does not solve it — and next time exactly this becomes an audit objection. Find the root cause and correct it.

### The core rule for avoiding objections

One thing is common in the table above — the root of almost every objection is the "we will do it later" kind of delay. If reconciliation (Trial Balance, bank reconciliation) happens every month, a bill is attached to every voucher right away, and the year is closed properly — most objections never arise. That means the real audit preparation happens not at audit time, but all year. (See: [10 common mistakes](/blog/common-accounting-mistakes).)

## Year-end and FY lock

Before the audit it is necessary to "close" the year properly: the closing stock entry, depreciation, outstanding adjustments, and then **Financial Year lock** — so that no old figure changes during the audit. (See [Common accounting mistakes](/blog/common-accounting-mistakes).)

> 📘 In Haryana this is not just a good habit, it is a rule — under Rule 79 it is compulsory to prepare the Balance Sheet within 3 months of the end of the financial year.

FY lock is needed so that the figures stay stable during the audit — if some old entry changes midway, the auditor's check becomes useless and trust breaks. So after closing the year, locking it, and then making any needed correction properly (through a new entry/adjustment) — this is the right way. (See: [Year-end and FY lock](/blog/year-end-closing-and-fy-lock).)

## What to do during the audit

The society's role during the audit days also matters: give the auditor the documents asked for right away, in an organised way; give straight, honest answers to their questions; and if some shortcoming is pointed out, understand and correct it instead of avoiding it. A society that cooperates with the auditor gets its check done quickly and smoothly. Remember — the auditor is not an opponent, but an independent expert who certifies the society's credibility.

## After the audit — compliance on objections

The audit work does not end when the report arrives. If the audit report has any objection (objection/qualification), **compliance** on it is necessary — that is, correcting the shortcoming pointed out and keeping its reply/record. Objections left unresolved come back next year and lower the society's grading. So as soon as the audit report arrives, make a list of objections, decide the action on each one, and settle them before the next AGM and the next audit. (See: [AGM preparation](/blog/agm-preparation-checklist).)

## Self-check before the audit (mock audit)

If the society does a "practice audit" itself before the auditor comes, nothing surprising is left for the real audit. In the self-check, look at these:

1. **Is the Trial Balance tallying?** (Dr = Cr) — the first and biggest check.
2. **Is the reconciliation of every bank account up to date?**
3. **Does every big voucher have a bill?** — open some random vouchers and see.
4. **Do the registers tally with the main accounts?** — member/share/loan/stock.
5. **Have the previous objections been settled?**
6. **Is the year properly closed and locked?**

Whatever shortcoming shows up in this self-check, correct it before the auditor comes. This half-hour job makes the real audit much smoother.

## Why audit grading matters

At the end of a cooperative audit the society often gets a **grade** (such as A/B/C/D), which shows its financial health and the quality of its records. A good grade has solid benefits: borrowing from apex banks/institutions becomes easier and cheaper, members' trust grows, and the society may get priority in government schemes. On the other hand, a falling grade is a warning that records and management need improvement. So see the audit not just as "pass/fail", but as a guide for improvement — and aim for a better grade every year.

## How a digital system makes the audit easier

Most of the effort in an audit goes into finding and matching documents. A digital system greatly reduces this burden: Trial Balance, final accounts and Receipts & Payments ready in one click; the bill attached to every voucher and easy to find; bank reconciliation and registers tallied automatically; and figures stable at year-end through FY lock. This way the auditor gets the document asked for right away, and the audit is finished in less time instead of days. The audit trail (who changed what) also increases transparency. In short, a clean digital record all year is the best audit preparation.

## Three habits that make the audit easy

- 🗓️ Bank reconciliation + Trial Balance check every month.
- 📎 Bill/receipt attached right away with every big voucher.
- 🔒 FY lock at the end of the year, so the figures stay stable.

## Common mistakes

- **Not reconciling all year** → objections pile up; Trial Balance and BRS every month.
- **Putting off bills for later** → voucher-entry mismatch; attach bills right away.
- **Putting it in suspense and forgetting** → find the root cause and correct it, do not postpone.
- **Not locking the year** → figures change in the middle of the audit; do the FY lock.
- **Not complying on objections** → they come back next year and lower the grade.
- **Last-minute preparation** → keep building an "audit file" all year.

## An example — two societies

To understand the difference, imagine two societies. The first society kept putting off its accounts all year — bills here and there, bank reconciliation never done, Trial Balance not tallying. In the audit month the secretary searches for bills day and night, corrects old entries, and still many objections remain — the grade falls. The second society reconciled every month, attached a bill to every voucher, and closed the year properly. Its audit was finished in just a few days, without stress, objections almost zero, and a better grade. The difference between the two is not talent, but discipline all year. This is the essence of this whole article — audit preparation happens not at audit time, but every month.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the audit of a cooperative society compulsory?** — It is an independent check of the accounts that gives members and the department confidence, and improves the society's grading and borrowing capacity.
**Which documents should be kept ready for the audit?** — Trial Balance, final accounts, bank reconciliation, vouchers (with bills), all registers, returns/challans, minutes and compliance on the last audit report.
**What are the most common audit objections?** — Trial Balance not tallying, voucher-entry mismatch, bank balance not tallying, closing≠opening, stock double-counted.
**Is it fine to put it in suspense?** — No; it postpones the problem, it does not solve it — and it becomes the next objection; correct the root cause.
**Why is FY lock necessary?** — So that old figures do not change during the audit and the check stays reliable.
**How to make the audit easy?** — Monthly reconciliation, bills attached right away, and FY lock at year-end — that is, the right habits all year.
**What to do with objections after the audit?** — Decide the action on each objection and settle it before the next audit, otherwise they get repeated.
**What is audit grading and why does it matter?** — The rating of the society's financial health/records; a good grade means easier, cheaper borrowing, member trust and scheme priority.
**What is a mock audit (self-check)?** — The society's own practice check before the auditor, so that shortcomings get corrected in advance.
**How does a digital system help in the audit?** — Reports and documents in one click, bills attached, automatic reconciliation, FY lock and audit trail — a faster, smoother audit.

## Three things to remember
- 🗓️ Bank reconciliation + Trial Balance check every month — the real audit preparation.
- 📎 Bill attached right away with every big voucher; avoid suspense.
- 🔒 FY lock at year-end, and always comply on every audit objection.

## Conclusion

The audit is nothing to fear — it is the reward for the right habits all year. Ready documents, a tallied Trial Balance and monthly reconciliation — just this much, and the audit becomes easy. For a society that keeps discipline all year, the audit is an easy job of a few days and its credibility grows stronger every year. So do your audit preparation not in the audit month, but every month — this is the easiest and most reliable way.
