# Role of Cooperatives in Rural and Agricultural Development

"Why are cooperative societies important for villages?" — the answer lies in the story of India's rural and agricultural development. Cooperatives changed the countryside by giving access to credit, farm inputs, marketing and banking, and they gave the small farmer organised strength. This article explains, in simple language and with examples, how this impact came about and why it still matters today.

## Why cooperatives became necessary

Before Independence, the farmer was often trapped in the heavy interest of the moneylender — more loans to repay old loans, and this cycle went on for generations. Cooperative societies offered a way of **working together** — cheap credit, fair prices and an assured market, which reduced the middlemen. This "organisation for self-reliance" is the core idea of cooperation — and the village needed it most, where the lone farmer was weak before both the market and the bank.

## Main contributions

- **Cheap and timely credit:** PACS and cooperative banks freed the farmer from the moneylender (for example, KCC crop loans).
- **Farm inputs:** seed, fertiliser and pesticide at the right time and price — fertiliser cooperatives (IFFCO/KRIBHCO) are an example.
- **Procurement and storage:** cooperative purchase of produce and godowns, so the farmer gets the right price in the mandi.
- **Financial inclusion:** banking and savings reaching remote villages.
- **Employment and self-reliance:** income in the village from societies such as dairy, handloom and fisheries.

| --- | --- |
| Credit | Freedom from the moneylender, cheap loans |
| Inputs | Seed and fertiliser on time |

| Savings/banking | Financial access up to the village |

Each point in this table is really the solution to a problem the small farmer struggled with for generations — the debt trap, fake or costly inputs, exploitation by middlemen, and distance from banking. Cooperatives struck at all four at once, and that is what makes them such a powerful tool of rural development.

## Types of rural cooperative societies

In the village, cooperation works in many forms, each meeting a different need:

- **Credit society (PACS):** crop loans and savings at village level — the lowest and most important link of cooperative credit.
- **Marketing society:** collective buying and selling of produce, so the farmer gets a fair price.
- **Dairy cooperative:** milk collection, processing and sale (Anand pattern) — daily cash in the village.
- **Consumer cooperative:** everyday goods at fair prices.
- **Fisheries/handloom/others:** organising small producers of a particular sector.
- **Multipurpose society:** many services in one society (credit + inputs + storage + digital) — a new, self-reliant model.

Different forms, but the same basic principle — pooling resources together and passing the benefit to members.

## The three-tier structure of cooperative credit

The backbone of taking cheap credit to the village is the **three-tier cooperative credit structure**: **PACS** at village level, the **DCCB (District Central Cooperative Bank)** at district level, and the **State Cooperative Bank (Apex)** at state level. Money flows from top to bottom — from the Apex to the DCCB, from the DCCB to the PACS, and from the PACS directly to the farmer. This structure took institutional, low-interest credit to every village and broke the dependence on the moneylender. (See: [Role of NABARD](/blog/nabard-role-in-cooperatives).)

## Financial inclusion — banking up to the village

Compared with big cities, the reach of formal banking in villages has always been weak. Cooperative societies — especially PACS and cooperative banks — bridged this gap: they took savings accounts, loans and payment facilities to remote villages, often where no private bank would go. This connected farmers and rural families to the formal financial system, kept their savings safe, and gave them cheap credit when needed. This "financial inclusion" is a basic step of rural development.

## A farmer's story — what changed

To understand the difference, imagine a small farmer. Before cooperatives: a loan from the moneylender for seed and fertiliser at 3–5% monthly interest; a low price for the produce from the middleman; and no safety for savings. After cooperatives: a crop loan from the village PACS at much lower interest; reliable fertiliser from a fertiliser cooperative; a fair price for the produce through cooperative procurement and godowns; and safe savings in a cooperative bank. The result — his costs fell, his income rose, and he came out of the debt trap. This change on the ground is the real contribution of cooperatives.

## Marketing and storage — a fair price for the farmer

An old problem of the small farmer has been that as soon as the crop was ready he had to sell it in distress, at a low price — because he had neither storage nor the capacity to wait. Cooperative **marketing societies and godowns** changed this: the farmer can keep his produce in a cooperative godown, take a loan against it when needed, and sell when the price is good. Cooperative procurement also gave him an assured, fair-price market in place of the middleman. In this way cooperatives turned the whole "after production" chain — storage, credit, sale — in the farmer's favour. (See: [Inventory and stock management](/blog/inventory-and-stock-management).)

## Employment and the rural economy

Cooperatives are not limited to the farmer — they drive the economy of the whole village. Cooperative activities such as dairy, handloom, fisheries, handicrafts and processing create jobs and income in the village itself, so people do not have to migrate to cities for work. Also, the money that circulates in a cooperative society stays local — one farmer's income goes into another's business — which strengthens the economic cycle of the whole village. This "local money, local development" is a big gift of cooperation.

## Government and institutional support

Many institutions play a role in strengthening rural cooperatives — such as NABARD (which gives refinance and guidance to cooperative banks), the state cooperative department, and various government schemes. Recently the focus on modernising the cooperative sector (such as computerisation of PACS) has also grown, so that village societies can give better, transparent service. A new or existing society should find out about such support and schemes available in its area and use them — confirm exact, current details with the department/bank concerned.

## The role today — beyond the revolutions

The role of cooperatives is not limited to history. Even today — multipurpose PACS, dairy and fisheries cooperatives, women's SHGs, and digital/CSC services — the cooperative sector remains the backbone of the village economy. New initiatives (such as computerisation of PACS and the multipurpose model) are giving a modern form to the same old idea — organising the small producer, linking them with technology and markets, and making them self-reliant. (See: [Cooperatives in the Green and White Revolutions](/blog/cooperatives-in-green-and-white-revolution).)

## Challenges too

For a balanced picture, also understand that rural cooperatives have faced challenges — some societies struggled with weak management, slack recovery, rising NPAs, or political interference. The cure is — transparent working, regular audit, better recovery discipline, and digital accounts. Where a society is strong and transparent, it becomes a boon for the village; where it is weak, it runs at a loss. So to keep the impact of cooperation, good governance is as necessary as good intentions.

## Why the cooperative model fits

For the small village farmer, the cooperative model has proved the most suitable in many ways. A private company's aim is profit, so it often does not reach remote or small farmers (profit is low there). Government schemes do reach them, but flexibility and local participation remain limited. The cooperative model is the path between these two — it is in the members' **own** ownership (so the benefit goes to them), runs according to local need, and is based on the democratic principle of "one member, one vote". That is why no other model has been able to fully replace cooperatives in rural development.

## The link with accounting

All this impact lasts only when the society's accounts are clean — member-wise loans/savings, a correct reserve fund, and reports on time. Transparent books are what keep members' trust, and the biggest difference between a weak society and a strong one is often just this — whose accounts are clean. A simple, Hindi-first digital tool makes this work much easier for a village society. (See: [Principles of cooperation](/blog/cooperative-principles-and-values).)

## Common mistakes (in running a society)

- **Slack recovery** → dues pile up, NPA rises; regular recovery is a must.
- **Weak/verbal accounts** → keep transparent, member-wise accounts.
- **Not building a reserve fund** → a reserve is needed for a bad year.
- **Control in a few hands** → collective, accountable decisions.
- **Ignoring audit/reports** → regular audit and timely reports keep trust.

## Three things to remember
- 🌾 Cooperatives freed the farmer from the moneylender and gave cheap credit.
- 🤝 They took inputs, marketing and banking to the village — "strong together"; jobs in the village itself too.
- 🧾 To keep this impact, clean, transparent books and good governance are needed.
- 🏦 The three-tier credit structure (PACS → DCCB → Apex) is what takes cheap credit to the village.

## In the end
Cooperation has been one of the strongest foundations of India's rural and agricultural development — it freed the small farmer from the grip of the moneylender and middleman by giving access to credit, inputs, markets and banking. This impact continues today and is growing deeper with new initiatives. But its full benefit comes only when the society is transparent, accountable and keeps good accounts. So for every village society, clean books are not just a need — they are the guarantee of its members' trust and its sustainability.

The road ahead also leads back to where this story began — to organisation. As village needs change (digital services, new markets, value addition), cooperatives too are taking a multipurpose, technology-enabled form. But their core will remain the same — organising the small farmer and rural people and, in a transparent way, giving them access to resources and markets. The societies that hold on to this core, and also keep good governance and clean accounts, will remain the most trusted institution of the village in the coming decades too.
