This guide is a tracker on Haryana's new "State Cooperation Policy" — that is, a step-by-step record of what is publicly known so far, which will keep being updated as new information comes in. It is the last part of our [series on DCCBs and the cooperative structure](/blog/dccb-haryana-pacs-harco-bank-credit-structure).

## The first signal — July 2025

According to a report in The Tribune dated 6 July 2025, at the state-level function in Panchkula on International Cooperative Day, Haryana's **Minister of Cooperation, Heritage and Tourism, Dr. Arvind Sharma** said that a new cooperative policy would soon come into force in the country, which would strengthen Union Cooperation Minister Amit Shah's vision of "connecting every household with cooperation", and that Haryana would implement it immediately and make changes to suit the state's needs. According to the report, he also said:

- Earlier, cooperative societies handled only fertilizer, medicines and loans; now **25 kinds of work** are being done, such as Jan Aushadhi Kendras, CSC centres and gas stations.
- So far **141 CM-PACS** (Comprehensive Multi-Purpose Activities Cooperative Societies) have been formed in the state.
- Officers of **Faridabad and Mahendragarh** districts were honoured for 100% computerisation of PACS.

> 💡 On 18 October 2026 the dashboard of the RCS Haryana online portal showed **546 registered CM-PACS** (including 61 women's CM-PACS) and 753 registered PACS. So the number has grown a lot compared with 141 in July 2025 — but the two figures come from different sources (one a news report, one a government dashboard), so do not use the exact increase as the basis for any calculation.

## The decision to make a policy — the meeting of 8 September 2025

According to a PTI report of 8 September 2025, a meeting of the **State Cooperative Development Committee (SCDC)** chaired by Chief Secretary Anurag Rastogi decided that Haryana would make its own "State Cooperation Policy". Senior officers, including Dr. Ashish Kumar Bhutani, Secretary of the Union Ministry of Cooperation, were also present. According to the state government statement, **a committee was to be formed within one week** to draft the policy. We have found no news on whether this committee was formed.

According to the report, the policy will be built on Haryana's needs, taking the experience of other states into account, and will set the path for the expansion and modernisation of cooperative institutions — especially in **rural development, credit, housing and allied sectors**.

## Link with the national policy

This step does not stand alone. The **National Cooperation Policy, 2025** was released on 24 July 2025. According to PIB (10 March 2026), it has 6 strategic pillars, 16 objectives and 83 recommendations, and it encourages states to make or remake their own state cooperation policy. The Union Ministry of Cooperation keeps asking states to make their policies in line with the national policy. Haryana's policy can be seen as part of this chain.

## Lessons from other states

Looking at other states' experience for the new policy was part of the announcement itself — and it is also sensible. India has several well-known cooperative models — such as Gujarat's dairy-cooperative (Amul) model and Maharashtra's sugar-cooperative experience. The lessons from them are usually the same: strong member participation, professional management, transparent accounts and value addition (not just raw material). (For an example, see: [Amul and the Anand pattern](/blog/amul-anand-pattern-dairy-cooperative).)

## Four focus areas — our understanding

The general meaning of the four areas named in the report (this is our interpretation, not the text of the policy):

- **Rural development:** linking cooperative societies to the wider economic needs of the village (inputs, sales, services).
- **Credit:** strengthening the PACS-DCCB-HARCO structure so that cheap, timely capital reaches the farmer.
- **Housing:** affordable housing through housing cooperative societies.
- **Allied sectors:** agriculture-allied activities such as dairy, fisheries and horticulture.

## What is happening on the ground — according to news reports

PTI's report on the same meeting of 8 September 2025 said the following (citing the state government statement):

- The Chief Secretary directed that **34 PACS be made multipurpose (diversify)** across the state.
- The Cooperation Department described a plan to link the **Centre of Cooperative Management, Rohtak** with **Tribhuvan Sahkari University**.
- According to the Registrar of Cooperative Societies, the state's target is to form **477 new M-PACS and 583 new dairy cooperative societies by 2028-29**.
- **95 PACS** received initial approval to run PM Jan Aushadhi Kendras, and 9 societies had already received drug licences and store codes.

Another news report (Indian Cooperative, September 2025) says **518 PACS** had completed digital day-end and online audit, and **338 PACS** were working on the [e-PACS platform](/blog/e-pacs-haryana-erp-day-end-audit-explained). We have not seen these figures in any government document.

> 💡 According to the Schemes page of the Haryana Cooperation Department, **801 PACS/PCCS** of the state are to receive funds under the PACS computerisation scheme (see: [Schemes of the Cooperation Department](/blog/haryana-cooperative-department-schemes-guide)).

## Growing push on digital and compliance

There is a clear trend in these reports — a push for digitisation and transparency. PACS coming onto day-end, online audit and e-PACS show that more digital compliance will be expected from cooperative societies in future. A society that is already digital and audit-ready will be comfortable with these changes; one that is still manual will face more pressure. So it is wise to go digital now, without waiting for the policy to become formal. (See: [What is e-PACS Haryana](/blog/e-pacs-haryana-erp-day-end-audit-explained).)

## For the society — preparing now

Whenever the policy becomes formal, a sensible society can prepare without waiting for it. Practical steps — make your accounts fully digital, keep the record of every member and activity up to date, get the audit done on time, and understand the eligibility and permissions needed for new activities (such as Jan Aushadhi Kendra, CSC). When new opportunities or requirements come under the policy, an organised society will adopt them at once.

## Why a policy matters — the big picture

Why does a clear state cooperation policy matter? Because until now cooperative development has often been scattered across separate schemes and announcements. A unified policy gives all of these one direction, priority and accountability — which sector first, how much budget, in what time frame. This also tells societies clearly where the government's focus is.

## What this means for the society/farmer

For an ordinary society or farmer, the practical meaning is — in the coming years, expect more activities for cooperative societies (such as Jan Aushadhi Kendras), more government support and more digital compliance. A society that is already organised can benefit quickly from every new initiative.
