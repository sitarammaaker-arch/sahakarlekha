The story of cooperation in Haryana is not the story of one village credit society or one procurement centre — it is a journey of nearly eight decades, starting with rural credit and moving through agricultural marketing, fertiliser and seed distribution, foodgrain procurement, dairy, the sugar industry, labour and construction, and now digital multipurpose cooperative business. Understanding this history is not just for information — it helps you understand where the structure that today's PACS, CM-PACS or institutions like HAFED work within actually came from.

## 1947 to 1966: Haryana was not yet a separate state

The history of cooperation in today's Haryana does not begin on 1 November 1966. For about two decades after Independence, the area of present-day Haryana was part of the then Punjab, and cooperative societies here worked under the Punjab legal framework of that period — the Punjab Cooperative Societies Act, 1961.

> 📘 The official history of institutions such as the Haryana State Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Bank gives direct proof of this — its parent institution was registered in October 1966 under the Punjab Cooperative Societies Act, 1961 itself. Early forms of labour cooperatives also appear in this period — societies formed in the early 1960s for earthwork linked to dams and canals, which later spread into skilled construction.

## 1966 — the biggest institutional turning point for cooperation in Haryana

Under the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966, the new state of Haryana came into being on 1 November 1966, and with it a large part of the state's modern cooperative institutional architecture was set up at once — instead of depending on one type of institution, separate institutional chains were built for separate economic needs.

| Sector | Main institutional arrangement | Core purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short-term agricultural/rural credit | HARCO Bank – DCCB – PACS structure | Credit to farmers and rural members |
| Long-term agricultural finance | HSCARDB and district banks | Irrigation, machinery, land development |
| Agricultural marketing and procurement | HAFED and affiliated societies | Inputs, procurement, marketing, processing |
| Sugar industry | Sugarfed and cooperative sugar mills | Sugarcane processing and farmer linkage |
| Labour and construction | Labourfed and primary L&C societies | Employment and construction work |
| Cooperative education | HARCOFED | Training for members, boards and staff |
| Dairy | Dairy Federation/Milk Unions | Milk procurement, processing, marketing |

## HAFED — expansion from marketing to processing

HAFED (Haryana State Cooperative Supply and Marketing Federation) also came into being on 1 November 1966 and is today the state's largest apex cooperative federation. At first its aim was to supply inputs such as fertiliser, seed and pesticides and to market and process produce, but over time procurement of foodgrains like wheat and paddy also became one of its main jobs — in other words, the cooperative system was no longer just a source of credit for the farmer, but became present at every stage, from pre-sowing input supply to post-harvest procurement and further processing and marketing.

## The three-tier credit structure and one important caution

HARCO Bank is the top of the structure developed from November 1966, with HARCO Bank at state level, 19 Central Cooperative Banks at district level, and PACS/PCCS/FSS at village level.

> ⚠️ Different government documents give different numbers for PACS — in some places 804, in others 805 PACS/PCCS/FSS, while the sanctioned number for PACS computerisation is 710. These three figures do not measure the same thing. In any analysis, "total PACS", "PACS/PCCS/FSS" and "PACS included in the computerisation project" must be read separately.

## HSCARDB — the separate story of long-term agricultural investment

In parallel with short-term crop loans, Haryana built a separate arrangement for long-term agricultural investment. The Haryana State Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Bank started with seven Primary Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks, which later grew to 87 and were merged into 19 district-level banks in the 2008 restructuring.

> 📘 This structure has provided long-term capital for productive assets such as irrigation, farm machinery, horticulture, dairy, fisheries and rural godowns — that is, giving the farmer capital for building assets instead of only seasonal crop loans. At the same time, it is equally true that pressure from recovery and accumulated losses has been a structural challenge in long-term credit structures of this kind — this is not an accusation against any institution or person, but a common challenge of long-term cooperative finance.

## Sugarfed and labour cooperation — expansion beyond agriculture

Sugarfed (Haryana State Federation of Cooperative Sugar Mills) was also set up in 1966 to increase the capacity of cooperative sugar mills. Over time this model was no longer limited to making sugar from sugarcane — by-product-based models such as cogeneration of power from bagasse and ethanol from molasses were added, so that the mills earn extra income and reduce losses.

When cooperation is seen as limited to farmers and PACS, the labour cooperative sector is often overlooked. The Haryana State Cooperative Labour & Construction Federation was registered on 24 October 1966, and the early labour cooperatives were formed to organise workers for earthwork linked to dams and canals — a completely different role for the cooperative model: instead of giving the farmer credit or marketing, giving workers collective access to work contracts.

## HARCOFED — building institutions alone was not enough

On the very day Haryana became a state, 1 November 1966, HARCOFED came into being from the division of the then Punjab State Cooperative Union — for member education, cooperative leadership, and training of managing committee members and staff.

> 💡 This design is itself an important signal — a cooperative society cannot be run on capital and registration alone. Since ownership and governance are in the members' hands, member education, board capacity and trained staff are as essential a part of any cooperative institution's economic model as capital.

## Dairy cooperation — towards a producer-owned model

The Haryana Dairy Corporation was formed in 1970 and worked until 1977, after which the Federation took over the dairy business and work began towards a three-tier cooperative structure based on the Anand Pattern. The VITA brand in Haryana grew out of this institutional base — its core idea was that the milk producer should not remain just a supplier of raw material, but become part of cooperative ownership in the procurement-processing-marketing chain.

## 1984 and 1989 — Haryana's own legal framework

After 1966 the institutions became Haryana's, but in the early years the legal framework continued to run on Punjab's old law. The big change came with the **Haryana Cooperative Societies Act, 1984** and the **Haryana Cooperative Societies Rules, 1989** made after it — this gave the state-specific statutory framework covering registration, membership, management, audit, disputes and committee tenure. (The full structure of this Act-and-Rules framework is in a [separate detailed guide](/blog/haryana-cooperative-act-1984-rules-1989-guide).)

## 1990-2020 — more a management challenge than expansion

By the 1980s, institutional structures for almost all major cooperative sectors had been built in Haryana. The period after that was less about forming any new apex federation and more about consolidating and restructuring existing systems — such as merging HSCARDB's 87 primary banks into 19 district banks in 2008. From here an old debate also grew in importance — should a cooperative's success be measured by its membership and geographic reach, or by its financial health, governance and service quality?

## After 2021 — a new era at the national level

On 6 July 2021 the Government of India created a separate **Ministry of Cooperation**, to build a separate administrative, legal and policy framework for the cooperative movement and to strengthen grassroots cooperatives. After this, initiatives such as PACS computerisation, Model Bye-laws, Common Service Centres, Jan Aushadhi, grain storage and business diversification quickly came onto the national agenda, and in 2025 the **National Cooperation Policy** was launched, with six strategic pillars for ten years and direction for states to redraft their own policies.

In this same period, **CM-PACS (Comprehensive Multi-Purpose Activities Cooperative Society)** emerged as Haryana's new experiment — legally and institutionally separate from the traditional PACS (this difference is explained in detail in [What is CM-PACS](/blog/cm-pacs-kya-hai)). PACS digitisation has also moved towards e-PACS/ERP systems, and PACS are no longer limited to credit but are increasingly becoming centres for services such as Common Service Centres, Jan Aushadhi centres and grain storage (full details of the godown scheme are [here](/blog/pacs-godown-grain-storage-scheme-ami-aif-nabard)).

> ⚠️ PACS profitability has been a lasting challenge — various government reviews have repeatedly shown that a large number of the state's PACS are either in loss or barely surviving. For the latest figures, see the Haryana Cooperation Department or PRS Legislative Research's budget analysis, because this number keeps changing over time.

## The biggest lesson from this history

In 1966 the first need was to build an institutional network — a bank was needed, a marketing federation was needed, a sugar federation was needed. By 2026 most of these structures already exist, and the words that now keep appearing in government reviews are — profitability, computerisation, business diversification, financial health. In other words, the next challenge is no longer geographic expansion, but institutional quality.

> 💡 This is just as true for any PACS, CM-PACS or CMS as it is for the big state-level institutions — building the structure was the first stage; making it economically viable, professional and member-centred is the next stage.

## One thread through this journey

If we put all eight decades in one sentence — first **the institutions were built** (around 1966), then **the legal framework became our own** (1984/1989), and now it is the era of **quality and digitisation** (after 2021). Each stage had its own need, and each stage became the foundation for the next. Today's PACS, CM-PACS or HAFED are the result of this gradual development — no institution came from nothing, but from this long institutional journey. That is why knowing the history is useful for understanding the present — it explains why today's systems are the way they are.

## Frequently asked questions

**From when is the history of cooperation in Haryana counted?** — The cooperative movement existed in the present Haryana area even before Independence, but the cooperative institutional structure of a separate Haryana state developed mainly after 1 November 1966.
**What is Haryana's main cooperative law?** — The Haryana Cooperative Societies Act, 1984, together with the Haryana Cooperative Societies Rules, 1989.
**When was HAFED set up?** — Along with the formation of Haryana state, on 1 November 1966.
**Are CM-PACS and PACS the same?** — No; CM-PACS (Comprehensive Multi-Purpose Activities Cooperative Society) should be understood as legally and institutionally separate from PACS (Primary Agricultural Credit Society).
**How many PACS are there in Haryana?** — Different government documents give different figures depending on classification and reference date; so "total PACS", "PACS/PCCS/FSS" and "PACS included in computerisation" must be read separately.

## Three things to remember

- 🏛️ With the formation of Haryana state in 1966, its entire cooperative institutional architecture (HAFED, HARCO Bank, HSCARDB, Sugarfed, Labourfed, HARCOFED, Dairy Federation) was set up together.
- 📜 The 1984 Act and the 1989 Rules gave Haryana its own independent cooperative legal framework — before that, Punjab's old laws applied.
- 📈 The period after 2021 is a shift from quantity to quality — the question now is not "how many institutions were built" but "how lasting and member-centred those institutions are".
