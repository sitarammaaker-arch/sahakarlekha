In Haryana, the whole system of farm credit rests on a three-tier cooperative structure — HARCO Bank at the top, the DCCB (District Central Cooperative Bank) in the middle, and PACS at the bottom. This guide explains this whole chain in detail — as the next part after [our earlier guide on DCCB recruitment](/blog/pacs-employee-dccb-clerk-promotion-haryana-exam).

## Why a three-tier structure — the reasoning

It is natural to ask: why three layers just to give a farmer a loan? The reason lies at the root of cooperation — each layer does the work of its own level best. The village PACS knows the farmer personally (who is reliable, who needs what), but it does not have large capital. The district DCCB handles the capital needs of many PACS and brings banking discipline. And the state's HARCO Bank looks after the balance of the whole state and the link with national institutions (NABARD). So "local knowledge" sits at the bottom and "large capital and regulation" sits at the top — the best mix of both. This division is what has kept the structure standing for decades.

## HARCO Bank — the top tier, working since 1966

The Haryana State Cooperative Apex Bank (HARCO Bank) was set up in November 1966 — so it has been the backbone of the state's cooperative banking sector for about six decades. It has been counted among the best state cooperative banks in the country, and it is registered with DICGC (Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation).

## The three-tier structure — the full picture

According to HARCO Bank's own official information, the structure looks like this:

| Level | Units |
| --- | --- |
| State level | 13 branches of HARCO Bank + 2 extension counters (Chandigarh and Panchkula) |
| District level | 19 District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs) |
| Sub-district/village level | 581 branches, 24 extension counters, 773 PACS/PCCS/FSS units |

This whole network serves about 30.06 lakh members, most of them farmers, rural artisans, farm labourers and small entrepreneurs of rural Haryana.

## A glance at the figures — what they mean

The network figures give a sense of the scale of this structure — 19 DCCBs, 581 branches, 24 extension counters and 773 PACS/PCCS/FSS units, which together serve about 30.06 lakh members. Such a wide reach means a large part of rural Haryana is connected to this cooperative credit system — farmers, artisans, farm labourers and small entrepreneurs. This wide connection is what sets cooperative banking apart from private banks: private banks often choose profitable urban/large customers, while the cooperative structure deliberately goes to the last-mile farmer, where reaching is hard and less profitable. This is its social role.

## An important mismatch — why is the PACS count different in different places?

> 💡 For transparency, this must be said: HARCO Bank describes its network as "773 PACS/PCCS/FSS units", while the live dashboard of RCS Haryana shows [752 registered PACS](/blog/cm-pacs-list-haryana-district-wise-476-registered). This gap is probably because HARCO Bank's figure includes, besides PACS, other categories such as PCCS (Primary Cooperative Credit Societies) and FSS (Farmers Service Societies), while the RCS figure is limited to strictly defined PACS only. Neither figure is wrong — they simply come from different classifications, which shows once again that when comparing cooperative figures in Haryana, you must keep the source and the definition in mind.

## The work of each level — who does what

In the three-tier structure, each layer has its own role:

- **PACS (village level):** Direct contact with the farmer — membership, crop loans (KCC), deposits, fertilizer-seed supply and recovery. This is the face the farmer meets every day.
- **DCCB (district level):** Giving capital and banking support to PACS, supervising them, and balancing cooperative credit across the district.
- **HARCO Bank (state level):** The apex of cooperative banking for the whole state — capital and direction to DCCBs, and the link with national institutions like NABARD.

So even a farmer's small crop loan actually comes through this whole chain — from the village PACS, through the district DCCB, to the state's HARCO Bank and further to NABARD.

## The role of NABARD

Above this whole structure there is one more important link — **NABARD (National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development)**, which gives refinance and policy direction to state cooperative banks. So a large part of the cheap farm capital that reaches the farmer flows along the route NABARD → HARCO → DCCB → PACS. That is why the health of the cooperative credit structure is a matter not only for the state but for national farm finance as well. (See: [The role of NABARD in cooperatives](/blog/nabard-role-in-cooperatives).)

## How does money flow from bottom to top?

In practice, the farmer takes a loan from his PACS; the PACS gets this capital from its district DCCB; and the DCCB gets capital support as needed from HARCO Bank, which is further linked to national institutions like NABARD. That is why any irregularity or delay at the PACS level can affect the whole chain.

## Why PACS is the most important link

The bottom layer of the whole structure — PACS — is actually the most important, because it is the one directly connected to the farmer. If a PACS's accounts are not clean, recovery is weak, or records are incomplete, then lending to it looks risky to the DCCB and HARCO Bank above — and in the end the capital reaching the farmer shrinks. On the other hand, a strong, transparent PACS raises the trust of the whole chain. That is why digital, audit-ready accounts at the PACS level are in the interest not just of that society but of the whole cooperative credit system.

## What this means for the farmer/member

An ordinary farmer does not need to remember this whole structure — for him it is enough to know that his nearest point of contact is his **village PACS**. That is where he gets membership, crop loans, deposits and fertilizer-seed. But it is useful to know that his PACS is not alone — behind it stands a whole state-level and national structure that connects him to cheap, reliable farm capital. This is the strength of the cooperative structure — connecting the small farmer to a large, organised finance system.

## Challenges of the structure

This structure is strong, but not free of challenges. The biggest — weak recovery and NPAs at the PACS level, which reduce the capital capacity of the whole chain. Second — many PACS still keep incomplete/manual accounts, so the upper levels do not get the true picture. Third — the slowness of old systems and the slow pace of digital adoption. Efforts like PACS computerisation are under way to remove exactly these. The direct lesson — the sooner each PACS becomes digital, transparent and disciplined in recovery, the stronger the whole structure.

## Through a farmer's eyes

Rameshwar, a small farmer, wants a crop loan for sowing. He goes to his village PACS, where he is a member. The PACS checks his record and gives the loan — but this money has actually come through a big chain: NABARD → HARCO Bank → his district DCCB → his PACS → Rameshwar. When the crop is sold, he repays the PACS, which in turn repays the DCCB. Rameshwar does not see this whole system — for him there is only his PACS. But this invisible structure saves him from the moneylender's high interest and connects him to cheap and reliable farm capital. This is the real gift of the cooperative credit structure.

## Why accounts matter at every level

All three levels rest on banking records, so clean, timely and matching accounts are needed at every level. At the PACS level, the member-wise record of loans/deposits and recovery; at the DCCB level, PACS-wise transactions; and the consolidated picture above — only when all of these match each other is the audit smooth and the flow of capital steady. Good cooperative software gives exactly this clean, audit-ready record at the PACS level, which becomes the foundation of the upper levels' trust. ## In the end

Haryana's cooperative credit structure is a fine example of how a small village farmer connects to a large, organised finance system — PACS to DCCB, DCCB to HARCO Bank, and further to NABARD. But the strength of this whole chain rests on the lowest link — PACS. The cleaner, more transparent and audit-ready each PACS's accounts, the more reliable the whole system. And when reading figures, always keep the source and definition in mind (such as 773 versus 752) — this is the key to understanding cooperative data correctly.

## The way ahead — strong, digital PACS

The future of this whole structure lies in strengthening the lowest link. Efforts like PACS computerisation, digital day-end and online audit are in this direction — so that every PACS's accounts stay clean, timely and matching with the upper levels. When PACS-level data is reliable, lending to them looks less risky to the DCCB and HARCO Bank, and in the end more and cheaper capital reaches the farmer. So a digital, transparent PACS improves the health not only of itself but of the whole three-tier system — this is the biggest priority for the coming years.

## Three things to remember

- 🏛️ Haryana's cooperative credit structure is three-tier — HARCO Bank (state), DCCB (19 districts), PACS (village level).
- 🔄 Capital is requested from the bottom up — PACS to DCCB, DCCB to HARCO Bank, and further to NABARD.
- 📊 The PACS count can differ by source (773 versus 752) — always check whether the figure includes other categories like PCCS/FSS.

> 💡 In one line — this structure connects the small farmer, instead of to a moneylender, to an organised cooperative finance system linked right up to the national level; and all its strength rests on the clean accounts at the very bottom — the village PACS. That is why digital, audit-ready accounts at every PACS are in the interest of the whole system.
