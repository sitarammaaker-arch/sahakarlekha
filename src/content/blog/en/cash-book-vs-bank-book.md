# Cash Book and Bank Book: The Difference and the Right Use

New accountants often mix up cash and bank — and that is where the trouble starts. The two are separate books, for separate purposes. This article explains **the difference between the cash book and the bank book**, the right use of both, the column layout, petty cash, bank reconciliation (BRS) and common slips — all with examples, in simple words.

## Cash Book

The cash book records all of the society's **cash** receipts and payments in date order, and shows the **cash-in-hand** balance at any time. It is not just a book — it is a *journal* (the transaction is first written here) and a *ledger* (the cash account's balance is formed here) at the same time. That is why it is called a "book of original entry".

> 💡 The cash book balance should equal the cash you can physically count. If there is a difference, check at once — it is the first sign of theft, a slip or an expense without a receipt.

### Types of cash book (column layout)

Traditionally the cash book is kept in three forms — in a digital system this is handled automatically, but understanding the concept is necessary:

| Type | What it shows | When useful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Single-column** | Cash only | A very small society |
| **Double-column** | Cash + bank together | Where there is a bank too |
| **Triple-column** | Cash + bank + discount | Where cash discount is also given/taken |

> 🔍 Many digital systems keep cash and bank in *separate* books (a single-column cash book + a separate bank book), so the matching of both stays clean. This is the method in SahakarLekha.

Another feature of the cash book is that entries are made in **date order** — so the balance on any day comes out instantly, and auditors can check each day's accounts in sequence. This "chronological" quality makes it the most reliable tool for daily cash control.

## Petty cash and the imprest system

Putting small everyday expenses (tea, stationery, auto fare) in the main cash book fills it up. For these a **petty cash book** is kept, often run on the **imprest (advance money) system**:

1. A fixed amount (such as ₹2,000) is given to the petty cashier.
2. They make small expenses and keep a slip for each expense.
3. At the end of the period, the amount spent is topped up again, so the balance always returns to the fixed amount.

This keeps the main cash book clean and small expenses are also recorded with documents.

## Bank Book

The bank book records the deposits and withdrawals of each **bank account**, and shows the bank balance as per the society's records. A society can have many bank accounts — current account, savings account, loan/cash-credit account — and **each has its own bank book**.

> 💡 Keep a separate bank book for each bank account. If you mix two accounts in one book, the matching of either will never come right.

Write the **cheque/UTR/reference number** with each transaction in the bank book — this is what makes matching (reconciliation) with the bank statement easy later.

## Which reports the two books go on to make

The cash book and bank book are not just everyday records — they are the foundation of every major report that follows:

- **Receipts & Payments account:** the summary of the whole year's cash and bank inflows and outflows is made directly from these.
- **Trial Balance:** cash and bank balances come here on the assets side.
- **Balance Sheet:** "Cash in hand" and "Bank balance" show on separate lines.

So if even one wrong entry remains in the cash or bank book, it reaches all three reports above. That is why daily matching and the monthly BRS are so important.

## The main differences at a glance

| Point | Cash book | Bank book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| For what | Physical cash | Money in the bank |
| Balance | Cash in hand | Bank balance (as per books) |
| Check | By physical count | Matching with bank statement (BRS) |
| Negative? | Never (a sign of a mistake) | Normally not |
| Proof | Receipt/slip | Cheque/UTR/statement |

> ⚠️ Mixing cash and bank in one account is the most common slip — then neither the cash matches nor the bank. Keep both separate.

## Money between cash ↔ bank (contra)

When you deposit cash in the bank or withdraw from the bank, it is a **contra** transaction — cash on one side, bank on the other. It creates neither income nor expense; money just moves from one book to the other.

**Example:** The day's cash of ₹40,000 deposited in the bank:
- **Bank Dr ₹40,000**
- **Cash Cr ₹40,000**

Both books stay updated on their own. (Learn more: contra vouchers in the [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).)

## Bank reconciliation (BRS) — why and how

Your bank book balance and the bank statement balance are often not the same — this is not a mistake, it is a timing difference. Matching them is called the **Bank Reconciliation Statement (BRS)**. Common reasons:

- **Issued cheques (uncashed):** You issued a cheque and reduced it in the books, but the payee has not yet put it in the bank.
- **Deposits in transit:** You showed the deposit, but the bank has not yet credited it.
- **Bank charges/interest:** The bank deducted/added it, but it is not yet recorded in your books.
- **Direct credits/debits:** NEFT/ECS/standing instructions that are in the statement but were missed in the books.

Make the BRS every month without fail — it is the strongest tool to catch slips, double entries and fraud. (In detail: [Bank reconciliation (BRS) guide](/blog/bank-reconciliation-guide).)

### BRS — a worked example

Suppose on the 31st your **bank book balance is ₹1,00,000**, but the **bank statement shows ₹1,12,000**. The matching will go like this:

| Particulars | ₹ |
| --- | --- |
| Balance as per bank book | 1,00,000 |
| Add: interest given by bank, not in books | +2,000 |
| Less: bank charges, not in books | −1,000 |
| Add: issued cheque not yet cashed | +12,000 |
| Less: deposit in transit | −1,000 |
| **Adjusted balance = statement balance** | **1,12,000** |

That is, the difference was not a mistake, it was a timing difference. The interest (+2,000) and charges (−1,000) must be entered in the books; the issued cheque and the deposit in transit will settle on their own in the next period.

## One day's example — both books together

Suppose one day at a society:

| Transaction | Cash book | Bank book |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ₹5,000 cash collected from a member | +5,000 | — |
| ₹2,000 stationery bought in cash | −2,000 | — |
| ₹40,000 cash deposited in bank (contra) | −40,000 | +40,000 |
| ₹25,000 paid to supplier by cheque | — | −25,000 |

At the end of the day the cash book balance is matched with physical cash, and the bank book balance (at month-end) with the statement. This discipline is what later makes a correct [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained).

## Cash control — why it is necessary

Cash is the riskiest asset, so discipline around it is necessary:

- **Close daily:** At the end of each day, match the cash book balance with physical cash.
- **Safe limit:** Deposit cash above a fixed limit in the bank the same day — don't keep large amounts in the safe.
- **A slip for every expense:** No payment without a receipt/slip.
- **Division of duties:** The person who handles cash should not alone write the books and do the matching — like [Maker-Checker](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).
- **Surprise checks:** Do a cash count without notice (surprise cash count) from time to time.
- **Not a single safe key:** Keep access to cash limited and write down responsibility clearly, so accountability for any difference can be fixed.

### Cash count slip (denomination sheet)

When closing the day, write down the count of notes like this, so the balance stays certified:

| Denomination | Count | Amount ₹ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ₹500 × 10 | 10 | 5,000 |
| ₹200 × 5 | 5 | 1,000 |
| ₹100 × 8 | 8 | 800 |
| **Total physical cash** | | **6,800** |

This "total physical cash" must match the cash book balance exactly. If there is a difference, find the reason the same day.

## Month-closing routine

At month-end, follow this order:

1. Check that all the month's receipt/payment vouchers have been entered.
2. Match the cash book Closing with physical cash.
3. Make the **BRS** for each bank account; enter any missed interest/charges entries.
4. Make sure this month's Closing = next month's Opening.
5. Only then treat the month's reports (cash book summary, bank summary) as final.

## Cash Book in Haryana: the rules and common slips

**Rule 78** of the Haryana Cooperative Societies Rules, 1989 gives the Registrar the power to issue orders from time to time deciding in what form societies will keep their account books — so the exact column layout of the Cash Book may depend on state/federation instructions, but the basic principles above (cash and bank separate, contra entries, physical matching) apply everywhere.

> ⚠️ The most common slip: typing a number by hand into a formula column such as Cash/Bank Balance (this spoils all the accounts after it), and not matching the month's Opening Balance with the previous month's Closing Balance.

## Common slips — and how to avoid them

- **Mixing cash and bank** → always separate books; money between them through contra.
- **Typing the balance by hand in a formula column** → always let the system work out the balance.
- **Opening ≠ previous Closing** → check the carry-forward every month.
- **Not making the BRS** → treat matching with the statement every month as compulsory.
- **Negative cash balance** → impossible; it means a receipt was missed or a payment was entered wrongly.
- **Leaving out reference numbers** → always write the cheque/UTR/bill number.

## Three things to remember

- 💵 Cash book = physical cash; bank book = money in the bank.
- 🚫 Never mix the two in one account; money between them through contra.
- 🔁 Match the bank book with the statement every month (BRS).

## Frequently asked questions

**Difference between the cash book and bank book?** — The cash book is for physical cash, the bank book for money in the bank.
**Can both be kept in one book?** — They should not be; keeping them separate keeps matching clean.
**Are the bank book and passbook the same?** — No; the bank book is the society's record, the passbook/statement is the bank's. The two are matched.
**Why are the bank book and statement balances different?** — Because of timing differences such as issued cheques, deposits in transit, bank charges/interest; BRS matches them.
**What is petty cash?** — A separate small cash fund for small everyday expenses, often on the imprest system.
**Can the cash balance be negative?** — No; negative cash means an entry was missed or is wrong.
**What if there are many bank accounts?** — Keep a separate bank book for each account, and match each one separately.
**Is the cash book a journal or a ledger?** — Both; the transaction is first written here (journal) and the cash account's balance is also formed here (ledger).
**When in the month should the BRS be made?** — When closing the month, as soon as the statement arrives; a large society may do it weekly too.

## In the end

Keeping cash and bank clearly separate is the first condition of correct accounts. Keep the two books separate, move money between them through contra, match the bank book with the statement every month, and always let the system work out the balance.
