# What is KRIBHCO: another farmers' cooperative fertiliser body

Like IFFCO, **KRIBHCO** is also a farmers' cooperative fertiliser body, but the two are different. This article gives KRIBHCO's introduction, history, work and cooperative nature — all in simple language — and also how to tell it apart from IFFCO.

## What KRIBHCO is

KRIBHCO stands for **Krishak Bharati Cooperative Limited**. It was set up in **1980**. It too is a **multi-state cooperative society**, whose members are cooperative societies. Its headquarters is in **Noida (Uttar Pradesh)**; its main fertiliser plant is at **Hazira** in Gujarat.

## Why KRIBHCO was formed

By the end of the 1970s, the demand for fertiliser in the country was growing fast, and more production capacity was needed to take the Green Revolution forward. Seeing IFFCO's cooperative success, the need was felt for another farmers' cooperative fertiliser body that would raise production and further strengthen the cooperative structure. With this thinking KRIBHCO was set up in 1980 — with the aim that it too would make and distribute reliable fertiliser in cooperative form, owned by farmers themselves. That is, KRIBHCO was also born from the same "farmers' own fertiliser body" spirit as IFFCO. (Background: [Cooperation in the Green and White Revolutions](/blog/cooperatives-in-green-and-white-revolution).)

## What it does

- **Fertiliser production:** mainly urea (and related products).
- **Farmers' interest:** within a cooperative structure, towards the benefit of member societies.
- **Distribution:** fertiliser reaches farmers through the cooperative network.

## What being a cooperative means

Instead of maximising profit for outside investors like a company, KRIBHCO's structure is centred on the welfare of member societies and farmers. This brings a reliable supply of fertiliser at fair prices straight to the farmer. This is the core spirit of cooperation — meeting a shared need together, in the members' interest — and this is what makes KRIBHCO, like IFFCO, a farmers' cooperative body, even though the two are separate organisations.

Being a **multi-state cooperative society** means KRIBHCO's area of work is not limited to one state but spread over many states, and its members are not individuals but cooperative societies. That is, it too is a "society of societies" — another example of cooperative federalism. (See: [The three-tier cooperative structure](/blog/cooperative-society-tiers-primary-central-state-apex).)

## IFFCO and KRIBHCO — how the two differ

| Point | IFFCO | KRIBHCO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Set up | 1967 | 1980 |
| Headquarters | New Delhi | Noida |
| Main plants | Kalol, Kandla, Phulpur etc. | Hazira (Gujarat) |
| Form | Multi-state cooperative | Multi-state cooperative |

> 💡 Both are farmers' cooperative fertiliser bodies, but separate organisations — don't get confused by the names. Both have the same aim (cooperative fertiliser in the farmers' interest), but they are independent institutions, each with its own board, plants and management.

## Why the names get confused

Because IFFCO and KRIBHCO are both multi-state farmers' cooperative fertiliser bodies, both make urea, both are owned by cooperative societies, and both have big plants in Gujarat — the ordinary farmer often takes them to be the same. But they are two completely separate, independent institutions, with different founding years (1967 vs 1980), different headquarters and different management. Treating them as one is as wrong as treating two different banks as one just because both do banking. Knowing this difference is useful when buying or selling fertiliser.

## What this means for a society

For a society selling KRIBHCO fertiliser, this too is ordinary **purchase-sale-stock** accounting. It is important to choose the correct stock and the correct sales/purchase account, so that reports show clearly category-wise.

In practice, for a PACS/farm-input society this means — recording every purchase and sale at once, keeping a correct account of stock (bags/tonnes), watching batch/expiry, and, for subsidised fertiliser, following government guidelines (such as POS entry, where applicable). Also keep in mind that if the same society sells both IFFCO and KRIBHCO fertiliser, it is better to track their stock and sales separately, so that it stays clear how much stock/sales each brand has. (See: [Inventory and stock management](/blog/inventory-and-stock-management).)

## The 1980 era — the background in which it was formed

To understand why KRIBHCO was needed, it helps to understand that era. The Green Revolution (from the mid-1960s) had multiplied wheat and rice yields, but these yields depended heavily on chemical fertiliser. The more grain, the more demand for fertiliser. In the 1970s the country had to depend largely on imports for fertiliser, which were both costly and uncertain. So raising domestic production capacity became a national priority.

IFFCO (since 1967) had already shown that large fertiliser plants could be run successfully in cooperative form. Taking this successful model forward, it was decided to build another large farmer-owned cooperative fertiliser body — so that production capacity would grow further and the country would move towards self-reliance in fertiliser supply. This thinking took shape as KRIBHCO in 1980. That is, KRIBHCO was not just a company, but an answer to that national need — through the farmers' own institution, for farmers.

## Cooperative federalism — "a society of societies"

The idea of "cooperative federalism" is useful for understanding KRIBHCO's structure. Just as a PACS (Primary Agricultural Credit Society) is formed at village level by farmer-members, at a higher level many societies come together to form a bigger society. KRIBHCO is an institution of this higher level — its members are not individuals but cooperative societies. That is, it is a "society of societies".

In practice this means that the village farmer's voice reaches the top through his PACS, and the benefit of the higher institution also comes back down the same way. This structure is the same in both IFFCO and KRIBHCO, and this is what sets them apart from ordinary private companies. The owners of a private fertiliser company are shareholder-investors; the "owners" of these cooperative bodies are the societies whose members are farmers themselves. (For more depth: [The three-tier cooperative structure](/blog/cooperative-society-tiers-primary-central-state-apex).)

## Accounting for fertiliser sales — a simple example

Suppose your PACS ordered 100 bags of urea from KRIBHCO. In the accounts, its effect will show in three places:

1. **Purchase:** the purchase of 100 bags will be recorded as a liability (Creditor) in the account of KRIBHCO (the supplier) and in the purchase account. On payment, the liability will reduce through bank/cash.
2. **Stock (Inventory):** 100 bags will be added to your urea stock. As sales are made to farmers, stock will reduce. At any time, balance stock = opening stock + total purchases − total sales.
3. **Sale:** on a sale to a farmer, income will be recorded in the sales account, and stock will reduce. Profit = sale value − cost value.

If your society sells urea of both IFFCO and KRIBHCO, the biggest precaution is to track the stock and sales of each separately. Otherwise, at month-end it will be hard to find out how much stock of which brand is left and how much of each was sold. In software like SahakarLekha, by recording each product (item) separately, this account stays clear category-wise on its own. (In detail: [Inventory and stock management](/blog/inventory-and-stock-management) and [Basics of purchase-sale accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

> ⚠️ For subsidised fertiliser (such as urea), government rules and a separate POS-entry process apply. Subsidy rates, the DBT process and eligibility change from time to time — always confirm them from the latest guidelines of the Department of Fertilizers/the state.

## How farmers benefit

For a farmer, KRIBHCO being a cooperative gives the assurance that behind the fertiliser he gets there is not profiteering but a farmer-first approach. Reliable quality, fair prices and supply through the cooperative network — all reach the farmer directly. And since the profit finally goes back to member societies (and their farmers), it creates a cycle in which the farmer is both producer and beneficiary. This is the strength of the cooperative fertiliser model.

## Link with fertiliser self-reliance and food security

Fertiliser is directly linked to food security — the more reliable and fairly priced the fertiliser, the better the yield, and the stronger the country's grain stock. Domestic cooperative fertiliser bodies (like IFFCO, KRIBHCO) are an important link in this chain: they not only produce, but through the cooperative network take fertiliser to the last village, where private distributors often hesitate to go. This "reach to the last mile" is the biggest social contribution of the cooperative model.

From a farmer's point of view, this means fertiliser is available right at sowing time, at a fair price, and with trusted quality — all three together. In the private market any one of these can slip (black-marketing, fake fertiliser, or high prices). Since the very purpose of a cooperative body is the members' interest, its focus on all three comes naturally.

## Transparency and member trust

The life of a cooperative body is its members' trust, and trust rests on transparency. Whether it is a national body like KRIBHCO or a small village PACS — the same principle applies to both: clear accounts of every transaction, timely audit, and open reporting before members. Only when members see that their institution's money is being used in the right place and that profit is shared fairly do they take a more active part.

At the level of a small society, this transparency becomes much easier with good accounting software — every purchase, sale, stock and payment is recorded automatically, reports are ready in one click, and members' questions at the AGM can be answered with figures. (See: [AGM preparation](/blog/agm-preparation-checklist) and [Audit preparation](/blog/audit-preparation-checklist).)

## The lesson for a small society

The story of both IFFCO and KRIBHCO gives every small PACS the same lesson: the cooperative model works at every level, small to big, provided it stays built on members' interest, quality and transparency. A village society may not become a national body, but if it follows these same principles — reliable service to members, clean accounts, and fair sharing of profit — it can become just as strong and trusted in its own area.

- 🌱 KRIBHCO is a farmers' cooperative fertiliser body (set up in 1980, main plant at Hazira).

## In the end

KRIBHCO, like IFFCO, is proof that farmers, by organising in cooperative form, can build large production institutions. Its core message is simple — when farmers meet a basic need like fertiliser themselves, through their own cooperative body, they get reliable supply and fair prices, and the profit also comes back to them. Just remember that IFFCO and KRIBHCO are two separate institutions — and if your society sells fertiliser of both, keep their accounts and stock separate and clean.
