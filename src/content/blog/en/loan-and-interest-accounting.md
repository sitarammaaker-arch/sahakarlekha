# Loan and Interest Accounting: A Guide for PACS and Societies

For many cooperative societies — especially PACS — the main work is giving loans to members. This is where the society's earning (interest) lies, and also its biggest risk (non-recovery). So correct accounting of loans and interest decides the society's health. This article explains **loan and interest accounting** — entries, interest calculation, repayment, NPA and common mistakes — in simple language.

## The loan cycle — at a glance

The life cycle of a loan usually runs like this: **sanction → disbursement → interest charged → instalment recovery → fully repaid (or NPA)**. Every stage has its own entry, and correct accounting means all of these are recorded in order and in the right accounts. So first understand the whole cycle, then the entry for each stage will seem easy. The best thing is that this cycle stays the same for every loan — whether the amount is small or large — so once you understand it, the accounts of every loan become easy.

## Giving a loan: an asset is created

When the society gives a loan to a member, the society's cash/bank goes down but a new **asset** is created — "loan recoverable from member".

| Account | Dr | Cr |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Member loan (asset) | ₹50,000 | — |
| Bank/Cash | — | ₹50,000 |

> 💡 A loan is not an "expense" — it is an asset, because the money has to come back. Putting it in expenditure is the biggest mistake.

It is very important to understand this difference: when the society gives a loan of ₹50,000, its money does go down, but it does not go out as an "expense" — it turns into an asset called "member loan", which will come back with interest. If by mistake it is treated as an expense, the society's profit will show unfairly low and the recoverable amount will vanish from the records — both are serious mistakes. So every loan should be recorded in a member-wise loan account, so that on any day you can tell how much is outstanding against which member.

## Sanction and disbursement — the first steps

Before giving a loan the managing committee sanctions it (recorded in the minutes), and the needed documents (application, security/guarantee, eligibility) are checked. Disbursement happens only after sanction, and only then is the "Member loan Dr" entry above made. So keep the sanction reference with every loan voucher — this is the first thing checked in the audit. (Vouchers and documents: [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).)

## Interest: the society's income

Interest is the society's **income**. When interest is charged (becomes due):

| Account | Dr | Cr |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Member loan / Interest receivable | ₹2,500 | — |
| Interest income | — | ₹2,500 |

And when the member repays loan + interest, Cash/Bank is Dr and the loan account is Cr. There is a fine point here — whether interest is recorded "when due" (accrual) or "when received" (cash) depends on the society's policy and rules. Whichever method you choose, keep it consistent, so that income shows on the same basis every year and comparison stays correct.

> 🔍 Calculate interest only once, at the right rate and for the right period. Avoid double entries or estimated interest — it spoils the Trial Balance.

## How interest is calculated

There are two common methods of interest calculation (rates only as examples):

- **Simple interest (simple):** on the principal at a fixed rate and period. For example, on ₹50,000 at 10% a year, 1 year = ₹5,000.
- **On reducing balance (reducing balance):** as instalments are repaid, interest is charged only on the remaining principal — so the total interest works out lower. In most loans this method is considered fair.

> 💡 Which method and rate apply depends on the loan type and rules — all percentages in this article are only examples; confirm the exact rate and method that apply to your society from the rules/bank.

## A repayment example

Suppose a loan of ₹50,000, 10% a year (example rate), and the member repays ₹10,000 principal + ₹5,000 interest together:

| Account | Dr | Cr |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash/Bank | ₹15,000 | — |
| Member loan (principal returned) | — | ₹10,000 |
| Interest income (or Interest receivable) | — | ₹5,000 |

With this the member's loan balance is left at ₹40,000, and the interest becomes the society's income. It is necessary to show the principal and interest parts separately in every instalment — only then do both the loan balance and the income stay correct.

## KCC (Kisan Credit Card) loans

Many PACS give crop loans to farmers under KCC. Its accounting is the same as a normal loan, but keep in mind:

- The sanctioned limit and the actual drawal are different — the record is of the actual drawal.
- When interest support/grant (interest subvention) is received, record it separately.
- Concessional rate for farmers who repay on time — correct use of the rate is necessary.

> ⚠️ Rate-related rules are changed by the government/NABARD from time to time. Confirm the current rate and subvention that apply to your society with the cooperative department/bank — this article only explains the accounting method.

Keep one more thing in mind with KCC and crop loans — the repayment period is set according to the crop cycle (such as after harvest), so their recovery is seasonal. When interest support (subvention) is received from the government, adjust it correctly in the member's account and keep a separate record, so that it is clear how much interest the member paid and how much the government bore. See in detail: [KCC crop loan accounting](/blog/kcc-crop-loan-accounting).

## Repayment schedule (repayment schedule)

Every loan should have a repayment schedule — when and how much each instalment is, and how much principal and how much interest is in each instalment. If this schedule is fixed in advance, the member also knows and recovery monitoring is easier for the society. In a digital system this schedule is made automatically and the loan balance keeps updating with every instalment, so the outstanding amount shows clearly on any day.

## Monitoring recovery and NPA

Giving a loan is easy; the real skill is **recovery**. Every society should watch two indicators:

- **Recovery percentage** = amount recovered ÷ amount due × 100.
- **NPA (non-performing asset)** — a loan outstanding for longer than the set period should be classified as NPA as per rules.

Some practical ways to improve recovery: reminding members before the instalment, regular review of defaulters, incentives for those who repay on time (where rules allow), and strict but fair action on old dues. Recovery is not just about money — it is protecting the society's money, which ultimately belongs to all members.

Rising NPA lowers both the society's grading and its future borrowing capacity.

> ⚠️ The exact rules of NPA classification (which category after how many days overdue) are set by RBI/NABARD and the applicable guidelines and change from time to time — confirm the current rules that apply to your society with the bank/cooperative department. This article only explains the concept.

## The concept of provisioning (provisioning)

When a loan becomes doubtful of recovery, as a precaution a **provision (provision)** is kept against it — that is, treating the possible loss as an expense in advance and setting it aside. With this the Balance Sheet shows a more honest picture (not an inflated profit). The exact rates and rules of provisioning also depend on the guidelines, so match them with the auditor/rules. The core idea is this — do not hide a bad loan, keep preparation for it in advance.

## Penal interest (penal interest)

On late repayment many societies charge extra **penal interest**. Its accounting is the same as normal interest (income), but it is better to keep it in a separate "Penal interest income" account, so that normal interest and penalty show separately in reports. The rate and conditions are set by the bye-laws/rules.

## Keep interest in income but recoveries in assets

A simple map:

| Event | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Loan given | Asset up (loan), cash down |
| Interest charged | Income up, receivable up |
| Instalment received | Cash up, loan/interest receivable down |
| Loan waived/written off | Expenditure (loss) recorded |
| Penal interest charged | Income up (separate account) |
| Provision kept | Expense recorded, against doubtful loan |

This map sums up the whole loan cycle at a glance — every event has a clear accounting effect, and just remembering these makes most entries correct.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a loan as an expense** → a loan is an asset (the money has to come back), not an expense; this is the most common and serious mistake.
- **Not splitting principal and interest** → show both separately in every instalment, otherwise both the loan balance and the income go wrong.
- **Estimated/double interest** → calculate only once at the right rate and period, not by estimate.
- **Ignoring recovery** → keep a regular watch on recovery % and defaulters, do not put it off.
- **Hiding NPA** → classify as per rules and keep provisions; hiding makes the problem bigger.
- **Mixing subvention/penal interest** → keep these in separate accounts, so that reports stay clean.
- **Disbursement without sanction** → every loan should be sanctioned by the managing committee, recorded in the minutes.

## Three things to remember
- 🏦 Loan = asset (the money has to come back); interest = income — never mix these two.
- 🧾 Record principal and interest separately in every instalment, so that balance and income stay correct.
- 📉 Keep regular watch on recovery % and NPA; keep provisions for bad loans as per rules.

## Frequently asked questions
**Is a loan an asset or an expense?** — An asset; the money has to come back from the member, so it goes into the "Member loan" account.
**Where should interest be recorded?** — In the society's income (Interest income); when due, both interest receivable and income go up.
**Difference between simple and reducing-balance interest?** — Simple is on the principal every time; reducing-balance is on the remaining principal, so total interest is lower.
**Why split principal and interest in an instalment?** — So that both the loan balance and interest income stay correct; mixing them spoils the accounts.
**What is NPA?** — A loan outstanding beyond the set period, which is classified as non-performing as per rules (exact rules from RBI/NABARD).
**Why keep a provision?** — Treating the possible loss on a doubtful loan as an expense in advance, so that the Balance Sheet stays honest.
**Is the accounting of KCC loans different?** — The basic method is the same; just record the actual drawal and keep subvention/concessional rate separate.
**How to record penal interest?** — Like normal interest (income), but in a separate "Penal interest" account, so that it shows separately in reports.
**Why is a repayment schedule necessary?** — With it the member knows the instalments and it is easier for the society to monitor recovery and keep the loan balance updated.
**Accounting of loan waiver/write-off?** — It is a loss (expense); write off a loan only after sanction as per rules, not quietly.
**How to improve recovery?** — Through reminders before instalments, defaulter review, incentives for timely payers and fair action.

## Conclusion

The core of loan-interest accounting is — treating the loan as an asset, interest as income, showing both separately in every instalment, and keeping a close watch on recovery. Do not hide bad loans — classify them as per rules and keep provisions. This discipline is what makes the society sustainable and profitable.
