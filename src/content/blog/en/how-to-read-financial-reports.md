# How to Read Financial Reports: From Trial Balance to Balance Sheet

Many board members and secretaries do make the reports, but understanding "what this report is actually saying" feels hard. In reality, every report answers one simple question. This article teaches **how to read the five main financial reports** — from the Trial Balance to the Balance Sheet — in simple words, with examples, and shows how they are linked to each other.

> 💡 Understand one thing at the start: these five reports are not separate papers, they are five views of the same truth. All are made from the same voucher data, so if your entries are right, they match each other on their own.

## 1. Trial Balance — "Are the accounts matching?"

The Trial Balance is a list of the Dr and Cr balance of every account. Its only job is to check that **total Dr = total Cr**. If the two are equal, the entries are mathematically balanced. It is the "raw material" of the final accounts — every other report is made from this list, so it is looked at first.

> ⚠️ A Trial Balance that matches does **not guarantee** that every entry is in the right account — it only tells you that Dr and Cr are equal. A classification mistake can still be there.

**How to read it:** Look at the bottom to see whether total Dr and total Cr are equal. If they are not, there is a one-sided entry or an adding mistake somewhere. If they are, look at each account's balance above — is cash negative, is any expense account unusually large? This is the first-glance check. (In depth: [Understanding the Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained).)

## 2. Trading Account — "How much gross profit from goods?"

For societies that buy and sell, it shows:

> 📘 **Gross profit = Sales − (Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock)**

That is, how much was earned directly by selling goods, before adding expenses. Even better if category-wise sales (fertilizer, consumer goods etc.) show separately.

**Example:** Opening stock ₹20,000 + purchases ₹80,000 − closing stock ₹15,000 = cost of goods sold ₹85,000. If sales are ₹1,00,000, then **gross profit = ₹15,000**.

> 🔍 How to read it: If gross profit looks very low or negative, either the closing stock was counted wrongly, or the selling price is below cost — both are worth checking.

## 3. Income & Expenditure / Profit & Loss — "Total profit or loss for the year?"

The gross profit from the Trading Account comes here, then all **indirect expenses** (salary, rent, electricity, interest) are subtracted and other income is added:

> 📘 **Net profit = Gross profit + Other income − All indirect expenses**

This tells you whether the society made a profit or a loss over the whole year. In a cooperative society it is often called the "Income & Expenditure account", because it is meant for a service-based body rather than for earning profit — but the method of calculation is the same as the Profit & Loss account.

**Example (continuing from above):** Gross profit ₹15,000 + interest income ₹5,000 − salary ₹8,000 − rent ₹3,000 − electricity ₹1,000 = **net profit ₹8,000**.

> 🔍 How to read it: Look at the biggest expense items — has any expense grown unusually from last year? This report becomes the basis of management decisions. The net profit worked out here becomes the basis for [profit distribution](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves) later.

## 4. Receipts & Payments — "Where did the cash come from, where did it go?"

This is a fully **cash-basis** summary — how much money came in and how much went out during the year, whether it was income, capital or loans. In cooperative societies this is a very useful report, because it shows in a plain way where money came into the society's hands during the year and on which items it went — without the complexity of credit/adjustments.

> 🔍 Note: It does not include movements between cash ↔ bank (contra), otherwise the same money would be counted twice.

**Difference from the Income & Expenditure account:** The Income & Expenditure account shows "earnings/expenses" (whether cash was received or not); Receipts & Payments shows only "cash in and cash out". For example — a credit sale of ₹10,000 is income in Income & Expenditure, but not in Receipts & Payments until the money is received. Understanding this difference is the key to reading both. (The basis of the cash basis: [Cash book vs bank book](/blog/cash-book-vs-bank-book).)

## 5. Balance Sheet — "The society's position on one day?"

This is the full picture of the society on one date — what the society has on that day and who has how much claim on it. The other four reports tell you "what happened during the year", while the Balance Sheet tells you "where we stand at this moment". It always rests on one equation:

> 📘 **Assets = Liabilities + Capital**

On the left/one side are assets (cash, bank, stock, loans, building), on the other side liabilities and capital (deposits, borrowings, share capital, reserves). If it "does not match", the problem is often in the opening balance or a double-counted closing stock. (Why this equation always matches: [The accounting equation](/blog/accounting-equation-explained).)

**How to read it:** First see whether both sides are equal. Then on the assets side see where the money is placed (stuck in stock or in cash), and on the liabilities side see how much outside pressure (borrowings/deposits) the society has versus its own funds (shares + reserves). This is the real picture of the society's health.

## Period reports vs one-day reports

Understand one basic difference — some reports are for "a whole period", some for "one date":

| Report | Covers |
| --- | --- |
| Trading Account | The whole year (period) |
| Income & Expenditure / Profit & Loss | The whole year (period) |
| Receipts & Payments | The whole year (period) |
| Balance Sheet | One date (position) |
| Trial Balance | One date (position) |

That is why a Balance Sheet always says "as on 31 March", while Profit & Loss says "for the year ended 31 March". When reading a report, always look at this heading — it tells you whether the figure is for one moment or for the whole year.

## The layout of a Balance Sheet — a glimpse

A simple Balance Sheet looks like this:

| Liabilities & Capital | ₹ | Assets | ₹ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Share capital | 50,000 | Cash & bank | 22,000 |
| Reserve fund | 25,000 | Stock | 15,000 |
| Member deposits | 30,000 | Member loans (recoverable) | 60,000 |
| Net profit | 8,000 | Furniture/building | 16,000 |
| **Total** | **1,13,000** | **Total** | **1,13,000** |

Both sides are equal (₹1,13,000) — this is the practical form of "Assets = Liabilities + Capital". If they don't match, go back and check the opening balances and stock.

## Quick signals for a board member

Even without being an accountant, a board member can sense the health of a report with these simple questions:

- Is there a net profit or a loss? Better or worse than last year?
- How much money is stuck in member loans, and how much of it is recoverable dues?
- Is the stock too high (money tied up in goods)?
- What is the ratio of outside borrowings to own funds (shares + reserves)?
- Does the cash balance match the books or not?

The answers to these questions are in the report itself — you just need to know how to read it. And the best part is that understanding them needs no accounting degree; a little practice and the right questions are enough.

## How the reports are linked to each other

| Report | Answers the question |
| --- | --- |
| Trial Balance | Are the accounts balanced? |
| Trading Account | Gross profit from goods? |
| Income & Expenditure | Total profit/loss? |
| Receipts & Payments | Flow of cash? |
| Balance Sheet | Position on one day? |

> 💡 Gross profit goes from the Trading Account to Income & Expenditure; net profit goes from Income & Expenditure into capital on the Balance Sheet. It is all one chain.

## The whole chain — in one example

Put the figures above together and see how one truth flows through five reports:

1. **Trial Balance** — all accounts balanced (Dr = Cr).
2. **Trading Account** — sales ₹1,00,000 − cost of goods sold ₹85,000 = gross profit ₹15,000.
3. **Income & Expenditure** — gross profit ₹15,000 + other income ₹5,000 − expenses ₹12,000 = net profit ₹8,000.
4. **Balance Sheet** — this same ₹8,000 is added on the capital side, and the closing stock of ₹15,000 comes on the assets side.
5. **Receipts & Payments** — the actual cash in and out during the same period shows separately.

That is, if even one figure is wrong, it will show throughout the chain — that is why correct vouchers are the first and most important condition. And the reverse is also true: if your five reports match each other, that in itself is strong assurance that the year's entries have been right.

## What to watch for while reading

- 🚩 **Negative cash** — impossible; an entry has been missed.
- 🚩 **Trial Balance not matching** — a one-sided entry or an adding mistake.
- 🚩 **Balance Sheet not matching** — opening balance or double-counted stock.
- 🚩 **An unusually large expense** — wrong account or double entry.
- 🚩 **Too much money stuck in stock** — slow sales or wrong closing stock.
- 🚩 **Member loans growing steadily but recovery low** — dues are rising, pay attention.
- 🚩 **Cash balance in Receipts & Payments not matching the books** — an entry or a reconciliation has been missed.

## Three things to remember

- 🔗 The five reports are five views of the same data; with correct entries they match on their own.
- 🧾 A matching Trial Balance = the maths is right; it does not necessarily mean every account is right.
- 📊 Net profit goes from Income & Expenditure into capital on the Balance Sheet — this link ties the whole chain together.

## Frequently asked questions

**If the Trial Balance matches, is everything right?** — No; it only shows Dr = Cr, a classification mistake can still be there.
**Difference between the Trading Account and Income & Expenditure?** — The Trading Account works out gross profit from goods; Income & Expenditure subtracts expenses from it and gives net profit.
**Difference between Income & Expenditure and Receipts & Payments?** — The first shows earnings/expenses (including credit), the second only cash in and cash out.
**Why does the Balance Sheet not match?** — Mostly because of the opening balance or a double-counted closing stock.
**Where does net profit go?** — It is added on the capital side of the Balance Sheet, and later becomes the basis of profit distribution.
**Which report should be seen first?** — First the Trial Balance (is the maths right?), then the Balance Sheet (position), then Profit & Loss (result).
**For which date is the Balance Sheet?** — For one fixed date (such as 31 March); whereas Profit & Loss is for the whole year.
**What should a board member look at?** — Profit/loss, loans/stock stuck, outside borrowings vs own funds, and cash matching.
**For which societies is the Trading Account?** — Those that buy and sell (consumer stores, PACS etc.); in a pure service society it is not necessary.

## Conclusion

Every report answers a question — are they matching? How much was earned? Where did the cash go? What is the position today? Once you understand this link, reports don't scare you, they guide you. Reading reports is not an expert skill — just remember which question each report answers, look at its heading (period or date), and stop at the red flags (negative cash, a Balance Sheet that doesn't match). Once you do this, you will be able to ask questions with confidence in board meetings, and your society's accounts will not stay limited to the accountant — this is the mark of good cooperation.
