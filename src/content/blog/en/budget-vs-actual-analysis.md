# Budget vs Actual: catch the variance in time

Making a budget is only half the job — the real benefit comes when you keep **comparing the budget with the actual figures**. This comparison is called **variance analysis**. This article explains in detail why it matters, how to do it, how to read a variance, and what steps to take on it.

## Why the comparison matters

A budget is a **plan**; the actual figures are the **reality**. The difference between the two (the variance) tells you where the plan went off track, and by how much — and if you find out early, you can correct it early too. If you find out only at the end of the year, the loss has already grown.

In a way, budget vs actual is the society's "steering wheel". Making a budget and forgetting it is like driving a car without looking at the road — you keep drifting from the plan and find out only when it is too late. Regular comparison lets you correct course along the way. That is why good societies do not just make a budget; they also compare it regularly.

## How to read a variance

> 📘 **Variance = Actual − Budget**

- **In income:** actual > budget = good (favourable); less = pay attention (adverse).
- **In expenses:** actual < budget = good; more = warning (adverse).

| Item | Budget | Actual | Variance | Signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sales income | 10,00,000 | 8,50,000 | −1,50,000 | 🔴 Low |
| Salary | 3,00,000 | 3,20,000 | +20,000 | 🟡 High |
| Interest income | 1,00,000 | 1,15,000 | +15,000 | 🟢 Good |

> 💡 Do not get stuck on every small difference — focus on large and repeated variances. This is "management by exception".

### Favourable vs adverse — avoid the confusion

A common confusion is that "actual higher = always good". Not so. It depends on whether the item is income or expense:

- **Income higher (actual > budget):** favourable ✅ — good.
- **Income lower:** adverse ⚠️ — check the reason.
- **Expense higher (actual > budget):** adverse ⚠️ — warning.
- **Expense lower:** favourable ✅ — but if it is far lower, check that a necessary expense has not been put off (such as repairs).

So read every variance in its context — do not blindly assume "more is good, less is bad".

## Types of variance — what caused it

It helps to understand a variance by its source:

- **Volume variance:** say the quantity sold was lower than estimated, so income fell.
- **Rate/price variance:** the quantity was fine, but the price/rate changed.
- **Timing variance:** the expense/income did come, but in a different quarter — so it is not a real variance, only a difference in timing.
- **One-time variance:** some unusual, one-off event (such as a major repair).

Identifying the cause matters, because each cause has a different remedy — there is no need to panic over a timing variance, while a continuing fall in volume is a serious signal.

## What to do when you find a variance

Ask the reason: was the budget itself unrealistic, did expenses really go up, or did income fall? Then decide — cut expenses, speed up recovery, or improve the budget next time. (For the new year's plan, see: [New financial year and budget](/blog/new-financial-year-and-budget).)

A simple order of checks:

1. **Is the variance large?** — If small, leave it; if large, move ahead.
2. **What is the cause?** — Volume, rate, timing or one-time?
3. **Is it in our control?** — Some things (like expenses) are in control, some (like market prices) are not.
4. **What step?** — Expense cut, recovery drive, or revising the plan.
5. **Check next time** — See the effect of the step in the next quarter.

## A detailed example

Read the table above closely. Sales income was ₹1,50,000 lower — this is the largest and most worrying variance; the managing committee should find its cause (did the quantity fall, did the price drop, or was recovery slow?). Salary was ₹20,000 higher — small, but check whether there is any new staff or arrears. Interest income was ₹15,000 higher — a good sign. So the biggest lesson of this quarter is — focus on sales income. The other two variances are small, so spend less energy on them. This is management by exception — the biggest difference gets the most attention.

## Variance in percent — comparison made easier

The variance in rupees alone sometimes gives an incomplete picture — a difference of ₹20,000 is large for a small item, but minor for a large one. So also look at the variance in **percent**:

> 📘 **Variance % = (Actual − Budget) ÷ Budget × 100**

Example: a variance of −₹1,50,000 in sales income, on a budget of ₹10,00,000 = −15%. Salary is +₹20,000 on a budget of ₹3,00,000 = +6.7%. Looking at percentages makes it clear that the 15% variance in sales is the most serious. A good habit is — treat only variances beyond a limit (such as ±10%) as "worth investigating"; this puts your attention in the right place.

## A detailed quarterly table

A society's full quarterly comparison can look like this:

| Item | Budget | Actual | Variance | % | Signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sales income | 10,00,000 | 8,50,000 | −1,50,000 | −15% | 🔴 |
| Interest income | 1,00,000 | 1,15,000 | +15,000 | +15% | 🟢 |
| Salary | 3,00,000 | 3,20,000 | +20,000 | +6.7% | 🟡 |
| Storage/rent | 1,20,000 | 1,10,000 | −10,000 | −8.3% | 🟢 |
| Repairs | 50,000 | 90,000 | +40,000 | +80% | 🔴 |
| Electricity | 60,000 | 64,000 | +4,000 | +6.7% | 🟢/🟡 |

There are two red signals here — sales income (−15%) and repairs (+80%). Even though the rupee difference for repairs (₹40,000) is smaller than for sales (₹1,50,000), its 80% variance is unusual and needs checking (perhaps a large, one-time repair). This is the benefit of looking at both rupees and percent.

## Fixed vs flexible budget

An advanced but useful idea — sometimes a variance arises because the level of activity itself changed. For example, if the society sold more goods than estimated, the purchase cost will also go up — this is not a "bad" variance. A **flexible budget** adjusts the budget to the activity level before comparing, so you can tell exactly whether a variance is about efficiency or just about volume. For a small society a fixed budget is enough, but for a large/trading society this way of thinking is useful.

## How to present variances in the meeting

Variance analysis has value only when it turns into a decision. So present it in the managing committee meeting in a simple, one-page form — only the large variances, their likely causes, and the suggested step. Instead of having the whole long table read out, say "these two items need attention, the cause seems to be this, we suggest doing this" — this way keeps the meeting focused and decision-oriented. (See: [MIS reports](/blog/mis-reports-for-management).)

## How often — the quarterly habit

The budget vs actual comparison should not be done once a year but regularly — usually **every quarter**, and monthly too in a large society. The more often you compare, the sooner a variance is caught and the more time you have to correct it. Make it part of the managing committee's regular meeting, so that decisions are based on figures. (See: [MIS reports for management](/blog/mis-reports-for-management).)

## Quarterly comparison made easy with software

This turns the comparison into a few minutes' work, and the variance and trend of every item are visible at a glance. When it is easy, the society does it regularly — and that regularity is what gives the real benefit.

## Common mistakes

- **Making a budget and forgetting it** → without regular comparison a budget is useless.
- **Waiting until year-end** → catch variances early, otherwise there is no time to correct.
- **Getting stuck on every small difference** → focus on the large and repeated ones.
- **Assuming "actual higher = always good"** → read it in the context of income/expense.
- **Acting without knowing the cause** → first identify the root (volume/rate/timing).
- **Not checking the effect of the step** → look for improvement in the next quarter.

## When the budget itself is wrong

Not every variance means performance is poor — sometimes **the budget itself was unrealistic**. If a large variance appears in the same item every quarter, perhaps the budget estimate itself was wrong. In that case the remedy is not cutting expenses but making **a better budget next time** — based on past actual figures, not guesswork. That is why budget vs actual analysis also improves next year's budget: each year's comparison makes the next year's estimate more realistic. This cycle of "plan → compare → improved plan" makes the society better year after year. (See: [New financial year and budget](/blog/new-financial-year-and-budget).)

## Frequently asked questions

**What is budget vs actual?** — Comparing the budget (plan) with the actual figures, so that variances are caught and corrected in time.
**How do you work out the variance?** — Actual − Budget; in income, more is good (favourable); in expenses, less is good.
**How often should it be done?** — Usually quarterly; a large society monthly too.
**Is every variance a cause for worry?** — No; focus on the large and repeated ones (management by exception).
**What is the difference between favourable and adverse?** — Favourable = in your favour (income higher/expense lower); adverse = against you (income lower/expense higher).
**What should you do first when you find a variance?** — Identify the cause (volume/rate/timing/one-time), then take the right step (expense cut/recovery/plan revision).
**What is a timing variance?** — When the income/expense came in correctly, but in a different quarter — this is not a real variance.
**Why look at the variance in percent?** — A rupee difference without context is incomplete; % tells you which variance is relatively large.
**What if the variance comes in the same item every time?** — Perhaps the budget itself is unrealistic; next time make a better estimate from past actual figures.
**What is a flexible budget?** — A budget adjusted to the activity level, so that volume-driven variances show up separately from efficiency-driven ones.
**Does a small society need this too?** — Yes; even a simple quarterly comparison of a few main items is very useful.
**Who should see the variance analysis?** — The managing committee, in the meeting, as a one-page summary (large variances + causes + suggestions).
**Does variance analysis help with the next budget?** — Yes; each year's comparison makes the next year's estimate more realistic.

## Three things to remember
- 🎯 Variance = Actual − Budget; the earlier the comparison, the earlier the correction.
- 🔎 Focus on large and repeated variances (management by exception); identify the cause, then act.
- 🗓️ Build the habit of a quarterly comparison — do not wait for year-end; look at both rupees and %.

## In the end
Budget vs actual analysis is not a complex technique — it is just a regular comparison of "plan vs reality", which gives the society a chance to recover in time. The rules are simple: compare every quarter, focus on large variances, identify the cause, and act and then check the effect. A society that adopts this habit runs on a plan — not on reaction — and that is the mark of a strong, growing society.

Remember — the aim of variance analysis is not to "catch" or blame anyone, but to keep the society on the right path. When variances are looked at with an open mind, along with their causes, they become a tool for learning and improving — whether it is control over expenses, faster recovery, or a better budget next year. This positive outlook is what makes the whole exercise useful.
