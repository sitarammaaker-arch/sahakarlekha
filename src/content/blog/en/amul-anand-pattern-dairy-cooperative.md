# Amul and the Anand Pattern: the biggest example of cooperative dairying

"Amul" is not just a brand — it is a story of cooperative strength that made India self-reliant in milk. This article explains Amul, the Anand Pattern and the White Revolution together in simple language — and also what every small milk cooperative can learn from it.

## Amul and GCMMF

The Amul brand is run by the **Gujarat Cooperative Milk Marketing Federation (GCMMF)**, based in **Anand, Gujarat**. Its roots go back to **1946**, when farmers in Anand (Kaira district) started their own milk cooperative. Later, in **1973**, GCMMF was formed, which brings milk products to the market under the Amul brand. **Dr. Verghese Kurien** is regarded as the leader of this movement.

## Why this movement began

The Amul story is the story of farmers organising against exploitation. In the 1940s, milk farmers of Kaira (Kheda) district were forced to sell their milk at throwaway prices to private contractors and a large private dairy. The contractor fixed the price, and the farmer had no say. Against this injustice, inspired by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and led by Tribhuvandas Patel, the farmers decided to build their own cooperative dairy — where the price of milk would be fixed not by a middleman but by the farmers themselves, through their own institution.

This idea took shape in 1946, and later, under the technical and managerial leadership of Dr. Verghese Kurien, this small beginning became a national movement. That is, Amul's core message, even before technology, is a social idea — when farmers organise, they can fix the fair value of their own labour.

## The Anand Pattern — a three-tier structure

This model of cooperative dairying works in three layers:
- **Village level:** The village milk cooperative society (farmer members supply milk).
- **District level:** The district milk union (processing, plant).
- **State level:** The state federation (marketing, brand — such as GCMMF/Amul).

| Level | Who | Main work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Village | Milk cooperative society | Milk collection, payment |
| District | Milk union | Processing, quality |
| State | Federation | Marketing, brand |

> 💡 The root of its strength: from the farmer straight to the consumer — fewer middlemen in between, and the profit goes to the farmer members.

## What each level does — in a little more depth

At the **village level**, every morning and evening the farmer brings milk to the milk cooperative society. There the quantity of milk is weighed and its quality (mainly fat and SNF) is tested. The price is fixed on this quality — the better the milk, the higher the payment. This principle of "price according to quality" is the backbone of the Anand Pattern, because it rewards honesty.

At the **district level**, the milk of many village societies comes together, and in a large plant it is processed, pasteurised, and made into milk products (ghee, butter, powder and so on). This is also where quality control and cold chain work happen.

At the **state level**, the federation (such as GCMMF) markets these products, manages the brand (Amul), and passes the profit from large-scale sales back down — to the district unions and finally to the village farmers. In this way the profit does not go into the pocket of an outside investor but returns to the farmer who supplies the milk. This three-tier structure is called the "Anand Pattern", and it became the model for cooperative dairies across the country.

## Link with the White Revolution

To spread this model across the country, **Operation Flood** was run, which is called the **White Revolution** — it took India among the world's largest milk producers.

The strength of Operation Flood was that it took Anand's proven model as a "template" and repeated it in other districts and states of the country. To implement it, the National Dairy Development Board (NDDB) was set up, also led by Dr. Kurien. Gradually thousands of village societies were formed, district unions were set up, and milk began to reach the city consumer straight from the farmer's village. This process turned India from a milk-deficient country into the world's largest milk-producing country. (In detail: [Cooperatives in the Green and White Revolutions](/blog/cooperatives-in-green-and-white-revolution) and [NDDB and the White Revolution](/blog/nddb-white-revolution-dairy-cooperative).)

## What this means for your society

If you run a milk cooperative, the daily accounts — milk collection, fat/SNF-based payment, member-wise records — must be clean. (See: [Basics of dairy cooperative accounting](/blog/dairy-cooperative-accounting-basics).)

In practice this means — each member's daily milk (morning + evening), its quantity and quality (fat/SNF), and the amount payable on that basis must be recorded accurately. At the end of the month (or the ten-day cycle), each member's correct payment must be worked out, and at the society level the total collection, total payment, processing/transport expenses and balance — all must tally. The more transparent these accounts are, the stronger the members' trust will be.

In good accounting software all of this links up automatically — as soon as fat/SNF is entered the amount payable is calculated, the member-wise slip is ready, and at the end of the month reports for the AGM and audit are available in one click. This digital discipline brings even a small VDCS (village dairy cooperative society) to the same transparency as the Anand Pattern.

## Fat/SNF-based payment — a simple example

In a milk cooperative, payment is based not just on litres but also on the quality of the milk. There are two main measures — **fat** and **SNF** (Solids-Not-Fat, that is, the solids other than fat). The higher the fat/SNF, the better the milk, and the higher the price.

Suppose two farmers bring milk on the same day, 10 litres and 10 litres:

- Farmer A's milk: 6.0% fat — good quality, so a higher rate per litre.
- Farmer B's milk: 4.0% fat — lower quality, so a lower rate per litre.

Even though both quantities are equal (10 litres), Farmer A will be paid more, because his milk is better. This principle of "price according to quality" rewards honesty and discourages adulteration. (The rate chart differs in every union/state; this is only an example to explain the principle — take the actual rates from your milk union.)

This is the most delicate part of the accounting — every member's milk for every shift (morning/evening), its quantity and fat/SNF, and the amount payable on it must be recorded correctly. Over a month these become hundreds of entries, so doing it by hand is difficult and error-prone. This is where good software — which calculates the amount payable from the rate chart as soon as fat/SNF is entered — makes the society's work much easier.

## Amul's products and diversification

Amul may have started with liquid milk, but today its range is very wide — milk, curd, butter, ghee, paneer, cheese, ice cream, chocolate, milk powder and many other products. There is a cooperative logic behind this diversification: when the farmer's milk is turned into many kinds of products, its value increases (value addition), and that added value finally returns to the farmer.

This is a lesson for every growing cooperative — instead of selling only raw material, if the society processes it, adds value and sells it, members get a better income. Of course this is a gradual journey, which first needs a foundation of reliable collection and clean accounts — and only then does diversification last.

## Three names, one revolution

Amul's success is not the gift of any one person, but the shared achievement of three names, who should be remembered together:

- **Tribhuvandas Patel** — the farmer leader who organised farmers at the grassroots and laid the foundation of the cooperative.
- **Dr. Verghese Kurien** — the "Father of the White Revolution", who led on technology, management and national expansion.
- **H.M. Dalaya** — the technical mind whose discovery of making powder from buffalo milk made Indian dairying practically possible (since buffalo milk was plentiful in India).

This trio shows that a successful cooperative needs all three things — grassroots organisation, skilled management, and the right technology. Without any one of these, this revolution would have stayed incomplete.

## Women's participation

A big social contribution of dairy cooperation has been that it linked rural women directly to income and decision-making. In many villages milk work is handled mainly by women, and the cooperative's payment reaching them directly gave them an economic voice. From this thinking, women's milk cooperative societies were also formed across the country, where both management and membership are in women's hands. This shows that cooperation is not only an economic tool but also a tool of social empowerment. (See: [The role of women's cooperative societies](/blog/women-cooperatives-role).)

## Lessons for every small cooperative

The Amul story is not limited to one state or one product — it is a blueprint for every cooperative:

1. **Members' interest first:** The profit returns not to an outside investor but to the member farmer.
2. **Reward for quality:** A higher price for the member who supplies honest and better produce — this is what keeps the system healthy.
3. **Transparent accounts:** Correct, timely payment to every member; open reporting by the society.
4. **Organisation in tiers:** Links from village to state, so that even a small society can reach the big market.

A village milk cooperative may not become as big as Amul, but if it runs on these same principles, it can become just as trusted and strong in its own area. (See: [Cooperative principles and values](/blog/cooperative-principles-and-values).)

## In the end

The Amul story shows that cooperation is not an old or weak system — even today, if run properly, it can make farmers the strongest force in the market. Its secret lies not in any magic, but in three simple things — members' interest, reward for quality, and transparent accounts. However small your society is, by holding to these principles it can win its members' trust. And the first step to that trust is — clean, timely, member-wise accounts.
