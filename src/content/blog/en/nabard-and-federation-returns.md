# NABARD and Federation Returns: Reporting by Cooperative Societies

PACS and many cooperative societies don't just have to keep their own accounts — they also have to send **reports/returns** from time to time to **NABARD, the DCCB, the federation** and the cooperative department. But first let us clear up a big misunderstanding: not every cooperative society files returns directly with NABARD. Understanding who reports what to whom, and when, is half the battle. This article makes exactly this easy with examples, so that your society's reporting becomes not a "burden" but the natural result of clean accounts.

## Two separate levels of reporting — understand this first

In the cooperative structure there are two separate streams of reporting, and mixing them up is the most common mistake:

- **Cooperative banks** — State Cooperative Banks (StCB) and District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCB) → come under the purview of **NABARD and RBI**.
- **PACS and general societies** (consumer, marketing, dairy, housing etc.) → report to their state's **Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RCS)**, under the state Cooperative Societies Act.
- PACS data also travels up to **NABARD/RBI** through the DCCB — so correct PACS accounts at the bottom are the root of every report above.

**Understand with an example:** Suppose "Jai Kisan PACS" is a member of its district's DCCB. Jai Kisan PACS does not itself send returns to NABARD — it gives its audit and annual details to the **Registrar**, and the account of its loans/deposits to the **DCCB**. The DCCB (which is a cooperative bank) then, at its own level, sends OSS returns to **NABARD**, which also reflect the consolidated picture of the PACS under it.

> ⚠️ So "NABARD returns" mainly apply to cooperative **banks**; the basic accountability of a PACS or general society is to its **Registrar**. Always confirm the formats and due dates applicable to your society with your cooperative department/federation.

## Whom NABARD oversees, under what authority, and how

NABARD supervises cooperative banks under the authority of **Section 35(6) of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949** — under this it carries out **inspection** of StCBs, DCCBs and Regional Rural Banks (RRBs). This happens in two ways:

- **On-site inspection** — a NABARD team goes to the bank and checks its financial, managerial and operational position and risk profile on the spot. This usually happens annually or at set intervals.
- **Off-Site Surveillance (OSS)** — banks regularly send set financial figures and ratios in **OSS returns** (such as capital adequacy, NPA%, liquidity). NABARD keeps a continuous watch on these from a distance and looks deeper as soon as there are warning signs.

The overall health of institutions like DCCBs is assessed on the **E-CAMELSC** rating model, which mainly looks at — **Capital adequacy (Capital), Asset quality, Management, Earnings, Liquidity** etc. The better the rating, the better the borrowing capacity and credit standing. (See: [NABARD — supervision process](https://www.nabard.org/content1.aspx?id=523&catid=8&mid=8))

**What this means for you:** if your PACS's DCCB gets a weak rating, refinance and borrowing can become costly or difficult — and one big reason for this is often weak recovery and high NPA down at the PACS level. That is, your discipline has an effect all the way up.

## PACS/society: returns and audit to the Registrar (RCS)

Apart from the bank level, every cooperative society's basic statutory accountability is the **annual audit and reporting to the Registrar**:

- An **annual audit** of every society is mandatory — it is done by an auditor on the Registrar's **panel** (a CA on the government list or a holder of a diploma in cooperative accounts); the audit report goes to both the **Registrar and the society**.
- The **time limit depends on the state**. For example, under **Section 81** of the Maharashtra Cooperative Societies Act, 1960, the audit must be done **within four months** of the end of the financial year (that is, by 31 July). (See: [Section 81, Maharashtra Cooperative Act](https://sahakarayukta.maharashtra.gov.in/site/upload/documents/Relevant_extract_of_Section_81_of_Maharashtra_Co-Operative_Societies_Act_1960.pdf))

**The usual sequence of audit and reporting (example):**

1. The financial year closes on 31 March.
2. The society prepares its **final accounts** — Trial Balance, income and expenditure, profit and loss, Balance Sheet.
3. The panel auditor checks the books and raises objections.
4. The society carries out **compliance/resolution** of the objections.
5. The **audit report + classification (A/B/C/D)** goes to the Registrar, and the same is placed before members at the AGM.

> ⚠️ Delay is not cheap: if the audit is not done on time, the AGM gets postponed, the grading falls, and in many states action can even be taken against office-bearers. Confirm your state's exact section, format and date with the cooperative department. (See: [How a cooperative society audit works](/blog/how-cooperative-society-audit-works) and [Audit classification A/B/C/D](/blog/cooperative-audit-classification).)

## What is usually reported

Whether the report goes to the Registrar or up to NABARD/the federation, the figures are usually these — and the source of each is your daily accounts:

- **Member growth** — new members added in the financial year, members removed, total members. Source: member register.
- **Share capital and reserve fund** — total paid-up capital, shares deposited during the year, transfer to the reserve fund. Source: share register + profit distribution.
- **Loan disbursement and recovery%**, and **NPA classification** — how much loan was disbursed, how much recovered, how much outstanding/NPA. Source: loan and recovery books.
- **Borrowings and deposits** — loans taken from the DCCB/bank, members' deposits.
- **Statutory and federation forms by state/institution** — such as monthly/quarterly statements in a set format in some states.

**Example:** if the year's entries in your member register are clean, there is no need to count the "member growth report" separately — it comes out automatically. The same applies to every figure.

## Understand with an example: recovery% and the NPA 90-day rule

The basis of recovery% and NPA reports is RBI's **Income Recognition and Asset Classification (IRAC)** norms. Let us understand them with an example.

**How recovery% is worked out:**
> Recovery% = (amount recovered ÷ amount due) × 100
> Suppose in a year the loan repayment due was ₹10,00,000 and ₹8,50,000 was recovered → recovery% = (8,50,000 ÷ 10,00,000) × 100 = **85%**.

**The NPA 90-day rule (worked example):**
- Under IRAC, if the **interest and/or principal of an account remains overdue for more than 90 days**, it is treated as an **NPA (non-performing asset)**.
- Suppose "Ramprasad" has a KCC/crop loan of ₹1,00,000, with the instalment due date **30 June**. If after this the overdue amount is not recovered for 90 days in a row (that is, until about **28 September**), then **from 29 September this account** will be treated as NPA.
- Now the interest that "accrues" on this NPA **will not be booked as income on an accrual basis** — it will be treated as income only when it is actually **recovered**. That is, protection against fooling yourself by showing profit on paper.

(See: [RBI Master Circular — IRAC norms](https://rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/notification/PDFs/17MIRAC01042022568064ADCFF941B1A5CFEB1E0EABC50C.PDF)) This same discipline keeps both your recovery% and NPA reports correct. (See: [Loan and interest accounting](/blog/loan-and-interest-accounting).)

> 💡 Practical tip: instead of "discovering" NPAs at year-end, look every month — as soon as an instalment nears 90 days, step up recovery efforts. This improves both the year-end report and the grading.

## PACS computerisation: reporting is now standardised

To make reporting easy and uniform, the **Ministry of Cooperation and NABARD** are bringing the country's PACS onto one common ERP software. This centrally sponsored project was approved **by the CCEA on 29 June 2022**, with the aim of computerising about **63,000 functional PACS** (initial outlay ₹2,516 crore, later revised). Its ERP gives every PACS a **Common Accounting System (CAS) + MIS**. (See: [Ministry of Cooperation — PACS computerisation](https://www.cooperation.gov.in/en/computerization-pacs-1))

**Before vs now (example):**
- **Before:** at month-end the clerk added up the books by hand to work out recovery%, NPA and member growth — it took time and errors crept in.
- **Now (CAS+MIS):** the same figures are produced **automatically** from daily entries; a standard report ready to send to the DCCB/Registrar in one click.

> 💡 This is the real benefit of CAS+MIS: a report becomes "as easy as pressing a button" — no separate adding and subtracting. SahakarLekha is built on the same idea — daily accounts right, and reports come automatically.

## An ideal reporting calendar (example)

Every society's calendar will be different, but a practical framework can look like this:

- **Every month:** cash/bank reconciliation (BRS), review of recovery, watch on accounts nearing 90 days.
- **Every quarter:** loan recovery and NPA summary, position of deposits and borrowings, budget vs actual.
- **At year-end (after 31 March):** final accounts → audit → resolving objections → report to the Registrar → AGM.

> 📌 Write down each of your reports and due dates in one place — if you are a bank, the NABARD/RBI OSS dates; if you are a society, the Registrar's audit date.

## Common mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Figures not matching the accounts** — putting separate "estimates" in the report. Avoid it: match every figure with the final accounts.
- **Hiding NPAs or catching them late** — this lowers both the grading and the credit standing. Avoid it: watch the 90-day rule all year.
- **Delay in audit** — both the AGM and compliance get stuck. Avoid it: close the accounts right after 31 March and get time from the auditor.
- **Confusion about whom to report to** — not understanding the difference between a bank and a society. Avoid it: remember the two-level structure explained above.

## Reporting to the federation: why and what

Besides the Registrar and (for banks) NABARD, many societies also give details to their **federation**. A federation is the higher cooperative body to which primary societies are linked — such as a district/state cooperative union, or a sector-based federation (milk union, marketing/consumer federation). There are three common reasons for reporting:

- **Consolidation:** the federation builds an overall picture of its member societies — total members, business, recovery, stock — which it carries further to the government/NABARD/NCDC.
- **Schemes and assistance:** grants, interest subvention or refinance are often decided on these very figures — wrong or late data means direct loss.
- **Coordination:** the federation's coordination in procurement, storage or distribution rests on these reports.

**Example:** a dairy cooperative society gives its district milk union daily/monthly details of milk collection and payments; the union uses this to decide the payment cycle, grants and schemes. Here too the rule is the same — **the figure should come straight from the accounts, not from a separate register.**

> ⚠️ Every federation's forms, frequency (daily/monthly/quarterly) and dates are different — confirm the current format with your federation.

## The foundation of a correct report — correct accounts

All these reports are made only from your **correct daily accounts**:

- Members/shares recorded correctly → member growth and capital reports correct.
- Loans/recovery correct → NPA and recovery% correct.
- Final accounts correct → everything else automatically. (See: [Trial Balance](/blog/trial-balance-explained).)

## Three things to remember

- 📅 Keep a calendar of the due date of every report and audit — NABARD/RBI for a bank, the Registrar for a society.
- 📊 Watch NPA (90-day rule) and recovery% all year — ease at the end.
- 🧾 Every figure must match the final accounts — no separate "estimate".

## Frequently asked questions

**Does every cooperative society have to file NABARD returns?** — No; NABARD mainly supervises cooperative banks (StCB/DCCB) and RRBs. PACS and general societies give reports/audit to their state's Registrar (RCS).
**When does an account become NPA?** — Under RBI's IRAC norms, when interest or principal remains overdue for more than 90 days. After that, income on it is booked only on the basis of recovery.
**How do we work out recovery%?** — (amount recovered ÷ amount due) × 100. For example, ₹8.5 lakh recovered out of ₹10 lakh due = 85%.
**Who does the society's audit and by when?** — An auditor on the Registrar's panel; the time limit is as per the state's Act (example: in Maharashtra, within 4 months of the year-end).
**What effect does PACS computerisation have on reporting?** — Because of CAS+MIS, figures are produced straight from the accounts, so reports are faster, standard and have fewer errors.
**Does a PACS report directly to NABARD or to the DCCB?** — In practice a PACS gives its accounts to the DCCB and its statutory audit to the Registrar; the DCCB then sends OSS returns to NABARD.

## In closing

NABARD/federation reporting is not a burden but the natural result of correct accounts. First be clear about whom you report to — NABARD/RBI if you are a bank, the Registrar if you are a society — then keep clean accounts, watch NPA and recovery all year, and the reports are ready on their own.
