# Contribution of Cooperatives to the Green Revolution and the White Revolution

Cooperative societies played a big role behind the two great revolutions that made India self-reliant in grain and milk — the **Green Revolution** and the **White Revolution**. This article links the two in simple language, with facts and examples — showing how cooperation organised the small farmer and changed the face of the country.

## The Green Revolution (grain)

In the 1960s–70s, grain production rose many times over thanks to improved seeds (HYV — High Yielding Variety), fertiliser, irrigation and technology — this is called the **Green Revolution**. It turned India from a food-grain importer into a country self-reliant in food grains. The role of cooperatives in it was mainly in delivering **inputs and credit**:

- **Fertiliser:** fertiliser cooperatives (IFFCO/KRIBHCO) took fertiliser to farmers.
- **Credit:** PACS and cooperative banks gave timely loans for seed and fertiliser.
- **Storage and procurement:** cooperative godowns and procurement supported the produce.

### How cooperatives made the Green Revolution possible

Improved seed and fertiliser were useful only if they reached **every farmer, at the right time, at the right price** — and this was the real role of cooperatives. A lone small farmer could neither buy fertiliser in bulk nor easily get a loan from a bank. Cooperative societies connected this whole chain: fertiliser cooperatives made and distributed quality fertiliser, PACS gave village-level loans for seed and fertiliser, and cooperative godowns and procurement took the produce to the market. This is how the technology of the Green Revolution reached the ground. (See: [What is IFFCO](/blog/iffco-fertiliser-cooperative).)

## The White Revolution (milk)

**Operation Flood**, which ran from 1970, brought a revolution in milk production — this is called the **White Revolution**. Its basis was the **Anand pattern of cooperative dairying** (a village–district–state three-tier structure), and its leader was **Dr. Verghese Kurien**. Through this, India rose to become one of the largest milk producers in the world. (In detail: [Amul and the Anand pattern](/blog/amul-anand-pattern-dairy-cooperative).)

### Operation Flood — how it ran

The National Dairy Development Board (NDDB) was set up in 1965 at Anand (Gujarat), and Dr. Verghese Kurien was its founder chairman (1965–1998). Operation Flood, launched by NDDB in 1970, is considered the world's largest dairy development programme, and it ran broadly in three phases:

- **Phase-1 (about 1970–1980):** the initial capital was raised from foreign aid (sale of skimmed milk powder and butter-oil donated by the European Community, through the World Food Programme).
- **Phase-2 (about 1981–1985):** a big expansion of milk-sheds and urban markets.
- **Phase-3:** making the cooperative dairy network stronger and self-reliant.

This campaign replicated the Anand pattern (the Kaira District Cooperative Milk Producers' Union, the forerunner of Amul) across the whole country.

### The Anand pattern — from village to market

The root of the White Revolution's success was the three-tier structure of the Anand pattern: the **milk cooperative society** at village level (where the farmer pours milk), the **milk union** at district level (which does processing and marketing), and the **federation** at state level (which handles branding and big markets). This structure removed the middlemen and passed the full benefit of milk directly to the farmer — that was its biggest strength. (See: [NDDB and the White Revolution](/blog/nddb-white-revolution-dairy-cooperative).)

## Fertiliser cooperatives — the farmers' own fertiliser

A big need of the Green Revolution was quality fertiliser reaching every farmer. **Farmer-owned fertiliser cooperatives** (such as IFFCO and KRIBHCO) played an important role in meeting it. Their special feature is that they are not profit-focused private companies but institutions owned by cooperative societies — that is, the farmers are their real owners. So the benefit of fertiliser also finally reaches the farmers. These institutions contributed to all three — fertiliser production, distribution, and teaching farmers its correct use. (See: [What is IFFCO](/blog/iffco-fertiliser-cooperative) and [KRIBHCO](/blog/kribhco-fertiliser-cooperative).)

## The credit structure behind the revolution

To buy improved seed and fertiliser the farmer needed money — and here the **cooperative credit structure** laid the foundation. Cooperative credit in India is three-tier: **PACS** at village level, the **DCCB (District Central Cooperative Bank)** at district level, and the **State Cooperative Bank (Apex)** at state level. This structure took cheap, timely crop loans to the farmer (such as, later, KCC — Kisan Credit Card), so that he could invest in seed, fertiliser and irrigation. So the Green Revolution is a story not only of technology but also of institutional credit — and its backbone was cooperative banking. (See: [KCC crop loan](/blog/kcc-crop-loan-accounting).)

## What the Green Revolution achieved

The biggest contribution of the Green Revolution was taking India towards self-reliance in food grains. In the early years after Independence the country struggled with grain shortages and dependence on imports; the Green Revolution raised yields many times over through the combination of improved seed, fertiliser, irrigation and technology, especially in wheat and rice. The role of cooperatives in this was that of the "delivery link" — wherever the technology and inputs were made, they reached the farmer only through the cooperative network (fertiliser cooperatives, PACS, cooperative banks, godowns). That is why it is said that the cooperative institutional structure was a silent but essential partner in bringing the technical success of the Green Revolution to the ground.

## The shared lesson of both revolutions

Green and White — the two revolutions were in different sectors (grain and milk), but their core thread was the same: **the lone farmer is weak, the organised farmer is strong.** A lone small farmer can neither buy inputs in bulk, nor bargain with a big market, nor arrange processing facilities. But when thousands of farmers join one cooperative society, they gain collective strength — cheaper inputs, better prices, and direct access to the market. This is the real power of the cooperative model, which both these revolutions proved.

## Through a farmer's eyes — what changed

To understand the impact of these revolutions, imagine an ordinary farmer. Before the revolution: he had to borrow from the moneylender at heavy interest for seed and fertiliser; even with a good harvest, the middleman gave a low price in the mandi; and there was no organised way to sell milk. After the revolution: a cheap, timely crop loan from the village PACS; reliable fertiliser from a fertiliser cooperative; a fair price for the produce through cooperative procurement/godowns; and assured, direct payment for daily milk from the village milk cooperative. So his income rose, his risk fell, and he came out of the grip of the moneylender and middleman. This change on the ground is the real achievement of these revolutions.

## The wider picture of the "colour revolutions"

The success of the Green (grain) and White (milk) revolutions inspired cooperative/organised efforts in other sectors too — such as fisheries, horticulture and other farm-based activities, where the same idea of organising small producers to get better income was adopted. The common root of all of these is the same cooperative principle — pooling resources together, bargaining together, and passing the benefit to members. In this way the Green and White Revolutions were not just two events but the inspiration for a whole "organised producer" movement.

## Relevance today

These revolutions are not history; they are still relevant today. Today, "multipurpose PACS", dairy and fisheries cooperatives, and new food-processing initiatives in the cooperative sector are carrying the same idea forward — organising the small producer and making them self-reliant. The lessons the Green and White Revolutions taught — organisation, access to resources, and transparent working — are just as true for every new cooperative initiative today.

## Challenges along with the role

For a balanced picture it is also necessary to understand that the cooperative sector faced challenges — some societies struggled with weak management, slack recovery, or political interference, and in places the benefit did not reach all farmers equally. While the success of these revolutions showed most in strong, transparent, member-centred cooperative institutions (such as Amul), societies with weak governance fell behind. The lesson is clear — the strength of the cooperative model is complete only when working is transparent, accounts are clean, and leadership is accountable. That is why today there is a focus on cooperative reforms (computerisation, better audit, the multipurpose model).

## The link with accounting

Societies in both these streams — whether fertiliser sales or milk collection — last only when daily accounts are clean: stock, member-wise payments and correct ledgers. In a milk cooperative, every farmer's daily milk, payment by fat/quantity, and accurate accounts — this is the life of trust. Transparent books are what keep these societies going for decades. (See: [Dairy cooperative accounting](/blog/dairy-cooperative-accounting-basics).)

## Frequently asked questions

**What was the role of cooperatives in the Green Revolution?** — Mainly in taking fertiliser, credit and storage/procurement to every farmer — the link that brought technology to the ground.
**What is the White Revolution?** — Operation Flood (from 1970, by NDDB), which brought a revolution in milk production through cooperative dairying.
**Who started Operation Flood?** — The National Dairy Development Board (NDDB); its leader was Dr. Verghese Kurien.
**What is the Anand pattern?** — A three-tier cooperative dairy structure (village society → district union → state federation), which began with Amul.
**What is common to both revolutions?** — Organising the small farmer and giving access to resources and markets.
**Is India ahead in milk production?** — Yes; after the White Revolution India became the world's largest milk producer (about one-fourth of global production).
**Why are these revolutions relevant today?** — Their lesson (organisation, access to resources, transparency) applies to every new cooperative initiative today.
**What did the Green Revolution achieve?** — It raised grain yields many times over through improved seed, fertiliser, irrigation and technology and took India towards food-grain self-reliance.
**What is a fertiliser cooperative?** — Farmer-owned institutions like IFFCO/KRIBHCO, which took reliable fertiliser to farmers.
**Were there challenges in the cooperative sector too?** — Yes; challenges like weak management/recovery and unequal benefits — so transparent working is necessary.

## Three things to remember
- 🌾 The work of cooperatives in the Green Revolution — taking fertiliser, credit, storage and procurement to every farmer.
- 🥛 The basis of the White Revolution — cooperative dairying (Anand pattern, Operation Flood 1970, Kurien); India became the world's leading milk producer.
- 🤝 The core mantra in both — organising the small farmer; the strength lies not in technology but in the organised institution.

## In the end
The Green and White Revolutions are the two biggest stories of India's self-reliance — and in both, cooperation was a silent but strong hero. Whether grain or milk, cooperative societies organised the small farmer and gave him resources, credit and markets — and this is what changed the face of the country. This history is an inspiration for every cooperative society today: by organising and working transparently, small people can bring big change.

A final thought — the biggest lesson of these revolutions is that change begins with technology but lasts through institutions. Improved seeds and milch animals existed before too; the real difference came when cooperative institutions took them to every small farmer in a transparent and reliable way. Even today, whether it is new technology or a new scheme, its benefit will reach the ground only when the cooperative institutions delivering it are strong, transparent and accountable — and this is where clean accounts become most important.
