# Primary, Central and State Cooperative Society: The Three-Tier Structure

"What is a central cooperative society?", "What is a state cooperative bank made of?" — these questions come up often. The answer lies in the **three-tier (federal) structure** of cooperation. Once you understand it, the whole system — the flow of credit, membership, and reporting — becomes clear. This article explains this structure in detail with examples.

## The three tiers at a glance

| Tier | Where | Who are the members | Example (credit) | Example (dairy) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | Village/local | **Individuals** (farmers/members) | PACS | Village milk society |
| **Central / District** | District | **Primary societies** | District Central Cooperative Bank (DCCB) | District milk union |
| **State / Apex** | State | **Central societies** | State Cooperative Bank (StCB) | State milk federation |

> 💡 The simple rule: the members of every upper tier are **not individuals, but the societies below**. This is cooperative federalism (federal structure).

## 1. Primary cooperative society

This is the lowest and most grassroots tier. Its members are **individuals directly** — farmers, milk producers, consumers. This is where the real transactions happen: giving loans, taking milk, selling goods. **PACS** (Primary Agricultural Credit Society) is its best-known example.

The primary society is the "face" of cooperation — the ordinary member deals with it directly. Its success or failure touches members directly, so its transparent working and clean accounts matter the most. This is where the democratic principle of "one member, one vote" applies most directly.

## 2. Central / District cooperative society

This is at **district level** and its members are **primary societies** (not ordinary individuals). It gives primary societies funds, processing or coordination. For example, the **District Central Cooperative Bank (DCCB)** gives loans to PACS, and the **district milk union** processes the milk of village societies.

The central tier is like a "bridge" — it connects many primary societies below with the state tier and resources above. A single PACS has neither enough capital nor processing capacity; many PACS together form a DCCB, which gives them collective strength and funds. This "organisation of organisations" is the special feature of the cooperative structure.

## 3. State / Apex cooperative society

This is at the top, at **state level**, and its members are **central/district societies**. It coordinates policy, funds and marketing across the whole state — such as the **State Cooperative Bank (StCB)** or the state **milk federation** (Amul's GCMMF is at this tier).

The apex tier is the "brain" of the whole state's cooperative network — it handles raising funds on a large scale, branding, big markets, and policy coordination, which a single district/primary society cannot manage. For example, in milk, the village society collects the milk, the district union processes it, and the state federation sells it across the country under a brand (such as Amul). (See: [Amul and the Anand pattern](/blog/amul-anand-pattern-dairy-cooperative).)

> ⚠️ Note: all three tiers are not necessary everywhere in every state/sector — in some places there is a two-tier structure too.

## How money and services flow

The best way to understand this structure is to see in which direction money and services flow in it.

- **In credit (loans) — top to bottom:** funds go from the apex (StCB) through the district (DCCB) to the village (PACS), and then to the farmer. (At its root there is often NABARD refinance — see: [Role of NABARD](/blog/nabard-role-in-cooperatives).)
- **In produce/products — bottom to top:** milk/produce goes from the village society to the district union, then to the state federation, and reaches the market, and its price comes back to the farmer.

So in one direction resources (credit/funds) come down, in the other direction products (and their earnings) go up — and this two-way flow keeps the whole structure alive.

## Why this structure — the benefits

The three-tier structure is not built for complexity but out of practical need:

- **Collective strength:** small societies together get big capital, processing and market access.
- **Division of expertise:** village level for collection/distribution, district level for processing/funds, state level for branding/policy — each tier does its own job better.
- **Spreading of risk:** the weakness of one society does not sink the whole system.
- **Democratic control:** control goes from bottom to top (member → primary → central → apex), not imposed from above.

That is why the world's most successful cooperative stories (such as Amul) stand on this federal structure.

## An example — the journey of milk

To understand the difference, look at the journey of milk. In the morning a farmer gives milk to his **village milk cooperative society** (primary), where its fat/quantity is measured and the account is made. This milk goes to the **district milk union** (central), where it is processed and packed. Then the **state milk federation** (apex) sells it across the market under a brand, and the sale money comes back to the farmer through the same chain. Together the three tiers build a system that was impossible for a lone farmer — and this is the practical power of the three-tier structure.

## Three tiers in different sectors

This three-tier structure is not limited to credit or dairy — it is seen in many sectors of cooperation:

| Sector | Primary | Central/District | State/Apex |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Credit | PACS | DCCB | State Cooperative Bank (StCB) |
| Dairy | Village milk society | District milk union | State milk federation |
| Marketing | Primary marketing society | District marketing union | State marketing federation |
| Consumer | Primary consumer store | — / District wholesale | State consumer federation |

Everywhere the basic principle stays the same — individual members at the bottom, society members above, and each tier does its own job. This gives the structure a ready blueprint for taking any cooperative activity from a small scale to a big market.

## Registration and law

The society at each tier is a separate legal entity in itself, and it is registered under the relevant cooperative Act — usually under the state's Cooperative Societies Act (and for societies spread over more than one state, under the Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act). This means the society at each tier has its own bye-laws, its own managing committee, its own general body and its own audit. This autonomy is what makes the structure democratic — no tier is fully subordinate to the one above; it is accountable to its own members (which are the societies below). (See: [Types of societies](/blog/cooperative-society-types-guide).)

## Challenges of the structure

For a balanced picture, also understand that the three-tier structure has its own challenges. Sometimes the problem of one tier (such as a weak DCCB) affects both the tiers below and above; in places coordination between tiers is lacking; and in places political interference or weak management at an upper tier affects the whole system. Because of these challenges there is a focus on cooperative reforms (such as computerisation, better audit, and transparent transactions between tiers). The condition for a strong structure is — every tier should be healthy, transparent and accountable.

## The link with accounting

Each tier is a **separate cooperative institution**, so each tier keeps **its own separate books** and gets its own audit done. The upper institution has to record its transactions with the societies below (loans, shares, grants) clearly, and often has to report to the federation in a set format too. Especially inter-society transactions (between one tier and another) should be recorded correctly and matching in the books of both, otherwise differences come up in audit. So in this structure, transparent, matching accounts at every tier are very important. (See: [NABARD and federation returns](/blog/nabard-and-federation-returns).)

## Common misconceptions

- **"An ordinary person becomes a member of the upper society"** → No; its member is the society below.
- **"All three tiers are necessary everywhere"** → No; in some places there is a two-tier structure too.
- **"Everyone's accounts are in one place"** → No; each tier keeps its own separate books and gets its own audit done.
- **"The upper tier controls the lower"** → The opposite; control goes from the bottom (members) to the top.

## Democracy from the bottom up

The most beautiful thing about this structure is its democratic nature. The village member elects the managing committee of his primary society; the primary societies together elect the leadership of their district (central) union; and the central societies together elect that of the state (apex) federation. So power and control flow **from the bottom up** — the ordinary member's voice starts at the very bottom and gives direction to the whole system. This is completely different from the "top down" control of a private company, and it is what gives cooperation its distinct, people-centred character. (See: [Principles of cooperation](/blog/cooperative-principles-and-values).)

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a central cooperative society?** — A district-level society whose members are primary societies (such as a DCCB).
**What is a state cooperative society?** — The apex society at state level, whose members are central societies (such as the StCB).
**Who are the members of a primary society?** — Individuals directly — farmers, producers or consumers.
**Can an ordinary person become a member of an upper society?** — Usually not; its member is the society below.
**How does credit flow in this structure?** — Top to bottom — apex (StCB) → district (DCCB) → village (PACS) → farmer.
**In which direction do produce/products go?** — Bottom to top — village society → district union → state federation → market.
**Why does each tier keep separate books?** — Because each tier is a separate legal cooperative institution, with its own audit.
**Is there a two-tier structure too?** — Yes; all three tiers are not necessary in every state/sector.
**In which sectors is this structure found?** — In many cooperative sectors such as credit, dairy, marketing, consumer, etc.
**How is each tier registered?** — Under the relevant cooperative Act (state, or multi-state); each tier is a separate entity with its own bye-laws/audit.
**In which direction does control run?** — Bottom to top — members elect the primary, the primary the central, the central the apex.
**What is the main challenge of this structure?** — The effect of one weak tier on the whole system; the cure is transparency, audit and good coordination.

## Three things to remember
- 🪜 Three tiers: primary (individuals) → central/district (societies) → state/apex (societies).
- 👥 The member of every upper tier is not an individual but the society below.
- 🧾 Each tier keeps its own separate books and gets its own audit done; inter-society transactions should be recorded so they match.

## In the end
The three-tier structure of cooperation rests on a simple but powerful idea — **the small come together to become big, but control stays at the bottom.** The village society connects with the member, the district society gives them funds and capacity, and the state society handles big markets and policy — but every upper tier is made up of the societies below. This "federalism" is what makes cooperation democratic and lasting. And there is only one condition for keeping this whole structure healthy — transparent, matching, timely accounts at every tier.

In short, the simplest way to remember this structure is — **three steps, one principle.** Individuals at the bottom, societies in the middle, a society of societies at the top; resources come down from top to bottom, products and control go up from bottom to top. Once you understand this, the whole system of cooperation — the flow of credit, the rules of membership, and the reporting needs — all becomes clear. And the key to keeping every step strong is the same one that holds for all of cooperation — transparent, timely, matching accounts.
