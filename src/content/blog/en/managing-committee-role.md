# Managing Committee: constitution, role and term

The general body meets once or twice a year, but the society runs every day. Who looks after all the work in between? The **managing committee** (Managing Committee / board of directors). This article explains **the managing committee's role, constitution, powers, term and accountability**, all in simple language.

> ⚠️ **Information checked up to 18 October 2026.** The managing committee's working, meetings and minutes are written as good practice. The points on term, reservation and supersession have been matched with Part IXB of the Constitution, the Supreme Court's 2021 judgment and the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act (including the 2013 amendment) — see "Sources" below.

## What the managing committee is
It is **a group of members elected by the general body**, which runs the society's day-to-day work and remains **accountable** to the general body. It is also called the board of directors, the executive or the Board.

In a cooperative society, the real owners are all the members (the general body), but they cannot gather every day. So they choose a few people from among themselves and hand over this responsibility. That is the managing committee. It is just like voters electing MLAs/MPs who take decisions on their behalf. So the managing committee does have power, but it is "borrowed" power: on behalf of the members, in the members' interest.

For this reason, every member of the managing committee should remember that they sit there not for themselves, but as a representative of the whole society. When this spirit stays, decisions stay fair and trust in the society remains.

## General body and managing committee — the difference

| Aspect | General Body | Managing committee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who | All members | A few elected members |
| When it meets | Once or twice a year | Regularly (monthly/as needed) |
| Role | Supreme decisions, policy, elections | Day-to-day running, implementation |
| Power | Supreme | Subordinate to the general body |

So the general body is the "owner" and the managing committee is its "elected management team". Every big policy decision finally belongs to the general body, while implementing it and running things day to day is the managing committee's work. This division is what makes cooperative democracy practical, because thousands of members cannot gather every day, but a small elected team can handle the work every day.

## Constitution (how it is formed)
- The **general body** elects its members on one member, one vote. (See: [Society elections](/blog/cooperative-society-elections).)
- The **number of members and term** are set by the state Act and the society's bye-laws. In Haryana, after 2013, under Section 28(4) the managing committee lasts **5 years** from the election, and the office-bearers' term ends with it.
- Reserved seats for women and SC/ST: in Haryana, since 2013, the proviso to Section 28(1) has "two women members" (details in the [Act guide](/blog/haryana-cooperative-act-1984-rules-1989-guide)).

> 💡 **What the Constitution says, and whom it applies to:** Article 243ZJ of the Constitution says the board will have at most 21 directors; in a society with individual members, one seat will be for SC/ST and two seats for women; and the term of the board and office-bearers will be 5 years. But the Supreme Court in *Union of India vs Rajendra N. Shah* (20 July 2021) held that Part IXB of the Constitution applies only to **Multi-State cooperative societies**. So these rules apply to a state society (such as a PACS) only when the state has put them into its own Act — as Haryana did through the 2013 amendment.

The constitution process broadly goes like this: announcement of election → nomination → voting → result → first meeting of the new managing committee, in which the chairman and other office-bearers are elected. After this, the old committee hands over charge to the new one: records, books, bank authority are all transferred properly. If this "handover" is clean, the new committee can start work without a break.

The purpose of reservation is to ensure participation of all groups in the committee. This too is part of the cooperative values of "equality" and "democratic control". The exact reservation rules differ by state, so check your state Act and bye-laws.

## Role — what it does
- **Decisions:** loan sanction, purchase-sale policy, service rates and admission of new members.
- **Oversight of finances:** making the budget, watching expenses, recommending the reserve fund and profit distribution (final approval is the general body's).
- **Staff:** appointing the secretary and employees, assigning duties and supervising.
- **Compliance:** ensuring timely audit, annual returns, meetings and minutes.
- **Accountability:** placing every big decision and the accounts before the general body.
- **Member service:** hearing members' complaints and suggestions and resolving them on time.
- **Planning:** planning and proposing the society's growth and new services/activities.

> 💡 Simple rule: the managing committee is **not the owner, but a representative**. It works on behalf of the members, in the members' interest, and gives account every year.

### One year's work cycle — an example

A managing committee's year broadly runs like this:

1. **Regular meetings:** month by month, day-to-day decisions like loans, purchases, membership and expenses.
2. **Quarterly review:** watching the financial position, recovery of dues and budget-vs-actual figures.
3. **Year end:** preparing for the audit, making the final accounts, and recommending profit distribution.
4. **General body:** placing the whole year's accounts, report and recommendations before the members; taking their approval. Elections of the new managing committee are also held here (at the end of the term).

So the managing committee takes small decisions all year, and presents the account of all of them in the general body. This cycle keeps the society organised and accountable. (AGM preparation: [AGM preparation](/blog/agm-preparation-checklist).)

### Powers vs limits

The managing committee's power is not unlimited. Some things it can do itself; for some, it needs the approval of the general body/Registrar:

- **Can do itself:** day-to-day decisions, loans/purchases within the set policy, and staff management.
- **Needs general body approval:** approval of the budget and profit distribution, proposal for bye-law amendment, and big policy changes.
- **Cannot do at all:** any decision against the bye-laws or the Act, or violating members' basic rights.

Knowing this boundary is important, because any decision taken outside it can be held invalid later. (See: [What are bye-laws](/blog/cooperative-society-byelaws).)

## Meetings, quorum and minutes

The managing committee works through regular meetings (often monthly). For every meeting:

- **Notice and agenda** should be sent to all members in advance.
- **Quorum** (minimum attendance) must be met, otherwise the decisions of that meeting are not treated as valid.
- **Minutes** of every decision should be written and approved in the next meeting.

Decisions taken without quorum or without minutes can be challenged later, so following the procedure is as important as the decision being right.

### What good minutes look like

Minutes are not just attendance; they are the permanent proof of every decision. Good minutes should have: the date and place of the meeting, members present (confirming quorum), a summary of the discussion on each agenda item, the decision taken (resolution), and who proposed/seconded it. Every big financial decision — loan sanction, big purchase, staff appointment — should be clearly recorded in the minutes, so that there is no confusion later and the audit can trace the root of every payment.

## Accountability and oversight

The managing committee is accountable at two levels:

- **To the general body:** every year, accounts, reports and decisions have to be placed before the general body; members can ask questions.
- **To the Registrar/law:** audit, returns and compliance as per rules.

If the managing committee commits serious negligence or breaks rules, the Registrar has powers under the state Act such as **supersession** — that is, removing the committee and appointing an administrator, as per the set procedure. In Haryana, since 2013, under Section 34(1) this can happen on persistent default, negligence, acting against the society's interest, not holding elections or a deadlock; administrators stay for **at most 6 months** (1 year in a society doing banking); and a society in which there is **no government share, loan, financial aid or guarantee** cannot have its committee dissolved. (Article 243ZL of the Constitution also gives the same framework.) This arrangement is the final protection of members' interests. (See: [The framework of cooperative law](/blog/cooperative-law-framework).)

Besides this, the annual **audit** is an independent check. It examines whether the managing committee handled the society's money as per rules and honestly. And members themselves keep watch by asking questions in the general body, looking at records and voting in elections. In this way, the managing committee is watched from three sides — members, auditor and Registrar — which prevents misuse of power.

## Fiduciary (trustee) duty

Members of the managing committee are like "trustees" of the society. That is, they must always take decisions honestly in the interest of the society and its members, not for personal gain. Avoiding conflict of interest, not using the society's money for private work, and not misusing confidential information are all part of this duty.

**Conflict of interest — an example:** suppose the society wants to buy goods from a managing committee member's own shop. In such a case, that member should stay away from the discussion and voting on that decision, and this should be recorded in the minutes. This keeps the decision fair and no question is raised later. Transparency is the easiest way to carry out the trustee duty.

## Common problems and how to avoid them

- **Decisions without quorum** → check and record quorum at the start of every meeting.
- **Not keeping minutes** → every decision should be written, signed and approved in the next meeting.
- **Crossing the limits of power** → the managing committee should not itself take decisions that fall within the general body's authority.
- **Conflict of interest** → the member concerned should stay away from the discussion and voting on that decision.
- **Not holding elections on time** → ensure elections before the term ends.
- **Not handing over charge cleanly** → transfer records, books and bank authority to the new committee properly.
- **Not informing members** → keep the full accounts and report transparent in the general body.

All of these have one root: procedure and transparency. A managing committee that holds regular meetings, keeps minutes, and gives account openly to the general body has fewer disputes and fewer audit objections. On the other hand, in a society where "everything runs verbally", even small differences turn into big conflicts.

## Link with accounting
The **record of every decision and approval** of the managing committee (minutes) and its financial effect should show clearly in the books — such as loan sanction, capex, reserve fund allocation. This transparency is what helps in the audit. A good rule: the root of every financial decision should be found in some resolution in the minutes. This keeps the answer to questions like "who approved this payment?" always clear. (See: [Basics of cooperative accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

## Frequently asked questions
**What is the managing committee?** — The executive elected by the general body, which runs the society's day-to-day work.
**Who elects it?** — The general body (all members), on one member, one vote.
**How long is the term?** — In Haryana, 5 years from the election (Section 28(4), 2013). In other states, check your own Act; the Constitution's Article 243ZJ with its 5 years applies directly only to Multi-State societies after the Supreme Court's 2021 judgment.
**Can the Registrar dissolve any committee?** — Not in Haryana; a society in which there is no government share, loan, aid or guarantee cannot have its committee dissolved or suspended (Section 34, 2013).
**Is it the owner of the society?** — No; it is the members' representative and is accountable to the general body.
**Difference between the general body and the managing committee?** — The general body is all members (supreme); the managing committee is a few elected members (day-to-day running).
**Can the managing committee take any decision?** — No; big policy and budget/distribution decisions are made with the general body's approval.
**What is quorum?** — The minimum attendance needed to make a meeting valid; without it, decisions are not valid.
**What is supersession?** — On a serious violation, the Registrar removing the committee and appointing an administrator.
**Can members question the managing committee's decisions?** — Yes, in the general body; the committee is accountable to the members.
**What is fiduciary duty?** — The managing committee's duty to always take decisions honestly in the society's interest, not for personal gain.
**What to do in a conflict of interest?** — The member concerned should stay away from the discussion and voting on that decision, and this should be recorded in the minutes.
**Why is handover of charge needed?** — So that the new committee can start work without a break; records, books and bank authority should be handed over properly.

## In the end

The managing committee is the pivot of cooperative democracy: the general body elects it, it runs the day-to-day work, and at the end of the year it gives account. Its power is entrusted by the members, so every decision should be transparent, as per rules and in writing. Only when the managing committee knows its limits, keeps minutes and puts members' interest above everything does the society become healthy and trusted.
