# Cooperative principles and values: the foundation of every society

Every cooperative society rests on the same "character" — a set of shared **values** and the **principles** that put them into practice. This is why a society runs differently from a company. **Cooperative principles and values** are not just textbook ideas — they directly decide how votes are cast in your society, how profit is shared, and how transparent the accounts are. This article explains them in simple language with examples, history and sources — so that secretaries, directors and members know "how" a society should run.

## Values vs principles — what is the difference

- **Values:** the shared beliefs the society stands on — **self-help, self-responsibility, democracy, equality, equity and solidarity**. Along with these come ethical values — **honesty, openness, social responsibility and caring for others**.
- **Principles:** the seven guidelines through which these values enter day-to-day work.

> 💡 In simple words — values tell you "why", principles tell you "how". (Source: [ICA — Cooperative identity, values and principles](https://ica.coop/en/cooperatives/cooperative-identity))

## The ten values — a little deeper

The ICA statement has ten values in all, in two groups — six basic and four ethical:

**Basic values:**
- **Self-help:** members help themselves together — no outsider does it for them.
- **Self-responsibility:** responsibility for the society and its results lies with the members themselves.
- **Democracy:** decisions are collective, by equal votes.
- **Equality:** every member has equal standing and rights.
- **Equity:** fair treatment in proportion to contribution/use (such as patronage).
- **Solidarity:** members stand by each other and by other societies.

**Ethical values:**
- **Honesty, openness, social responsibility, caring for others** — these are what make a society an institution people trust.

> 💡 "Equality" and "equity" are different: equality = equal rights (one member, one vote); equity = fair sharing of benefit in proportion to use.

## Where they came from — a short history

The foundation of modern cooperation was laid in 1844 by the **Rochdale Pioneers** in England, who formed the first lasting consumer cooperative society and set some basic rules. These "Rochdale Principles" were refined from time to time, and **in 1995 at Manchester the International Cooperative Alliance (ICA)** adopted the **"Statement on the Cooperative Identity"** — which records the definition of a cooperative, its values and the seven principles. This is today the shared framework of cooperative societies across the world. (Source: [ICA — Cooperative Identity](https://ica.coop/en/cooperatives/cooperative-identity))

## The seven cooperative principles — in detail

### 1. Voluntary and open membership
Any eligible person can join without any discrimination (gender, social, race, political, religious) and can also leave voluntarily. "Open" here means — the door is open to anyone who can use the society's services and carry out its responsibilities. **Example:** a dairy society cannot refuse membership to any milk producer of the village if he meets the eligibility and the bye-laws; nor can anyone be made a member by force.

### 2. Democratic member control
The society stays in the hands of its members, and in policy decisions **every member has one vote** — no matter how many shares he has taken. **Example:** a member with ₹50,000 of shares and a member with ₹500 of shares — both have an equal single vote in the AGM. (See: [Role of the managing committee](/blog/managing-committee-role).)

### 3. Member economic participation
Members contribute to the capital and keep democratic control over it; profit is used for the society's development, reserves, and member benefit (in proportion to patronage) — not for the profit of outside investors. **Example:** the year's surplus goes first to the compulsory reserve fund, then is shared as member dividend (in proportion to patronage) and to the general/education fund — not to outside investors. (See: [Profit distribution and reserves](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves).)

### 4. Autonomy and independence
The society is an autonomous, self-help organisation controlled by its members. If it takes capital or enters an agreement with the government or any outside body, it does so on the condition that member control and autonomy remain. **Example:** even when taking a government grant or a bank loan, policy decisions must stay with the members/elected board — the outside body must not start running the society.

### 5. Education, training and information
The society trains its members, elected representatives, managers and employees so that they can contribute effectively to its development; and it tells the public, especially young people and leaders, about the benefits of cooperation. **Example:** explaining the bye-laws and their rights and duties to new members, and giving accounting training to the accountant/secretary so that the accounts stay correct. (See: [Rights and duties of a member](/blog/membership-rights-and-duties).)

### 6. Cooperation among cooperatives
Societies serve their members best and strengthen the cooperative movement by working together at local, national and international levels. **Example:** primary societies join district/state federations, which gives them better bargaining, shared storage and refinance — what would be hard alone becomes easy together. (See: [Levels of cooperative societies](/blog/cooperative-society-tiers-primary-central-state-apex).)

### 7. Concern for community
While meeting its members' needs, the society also works for the sustainable development of its community. **Example:** a village society can contribute to local employment, women's empowerment or cleanliness/environment work — profit is not the only goal.

> ⚠️ These principles are not just slogans — they directly shape the **bye-laws, governance and profit sharing**. That is, the effect of each principle shows clearly in your society's day-to-day rules and decisions.

## The seven principles — their effect at a glance

| Principle | What it looks like in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Voluntary and open membership | Membership for the eligible without discrimination; voluntary exit |
| Democratic control | One member, one vote; AGM and elections |
| Member economic participation | Share capital, reserves, patronage dividend |
| Autonomy and independence | Member control intact, no outside pressure |
| Education and training | Member/employee training, education fund |
| Cooperation among societies | Unions/federations, three-tier structure |
| Concern for community | Sustainable development and social responsibility |

## Through the lens of the principles — cooperative society vs company

- **Vote:** in a society, one member one vote; in a company, votes in proportion to shares.
- **Purpose:** a society is centred on serving members; a company is centred on profit.
- **Profit:** in a society, surplus goes to reserves and patronage; in a company, to shareholder dividend.
- **Control:** a society belongs to its members; a company to its large shareholders.

(See in detail: [Cooperative society vs company](/blog/cooperative-vs-company-difference).)

## How this foundation touches the accounts

The principles flow directly into accounting and governance:

- **"One member, one vote"** → the process of the general meeting (AGM) and elections, quorum, resolutions.
- **Member economic participation** → share capital, compulsory transfer to the reserve fund, patronage-based dividend.
- **Education principle** → in many states a part of the profit goes to the **cooperative education fund**.
- **Openness and honesty** → transparent, audit-ready accounts; members' right to inspect the accounts.
- **Autonomy** → the society's own accounts and decisions; no manipulation under outside pressure.
- **Cooperation among societies** → reporting to the federation/DCCB and consolidated accounts.

For these reasons a cooperative society's books do not show only "how much profit was made", but also "how fairly the members were treated". This is why cooperative accounting looks different from a company's — and SahakarLekha is built with this same thinking. (See: [Cooperative society vs company](/blog/cooperative-vs-company-difference).)

## Where these principles show up in India

- **One member, one vote** is written into every state's cooperative Act.
- A part of the net profit compulsorily goes to the **reserve fund** (as per law).
- Contribution to the **education/cooperative education fund** is compulsory in many states.
- **Cooperation among societies** shows up in the three-tier structure (PACS→DCCB→StCB) and in federations.

> 💡 That is, the principles are not just ideals — in India they are also enforced through law and bye-laws.

## The principles work — real examples

- **Amul (dairy):** lakhs of small milk-producer members, each with an equal vote — a living example of "democratic control" and "member economic participation". Village society → district union → GCMMF: the form of the "cooperation among cooperatives" principle.
- **IFFCO/KRIBHCO (fertiliser):** farmers' own cooperative institutions, which supply fertiliser in the members' interest — "self-help" and "concern for community".
- **PACS (credit):** open membership and cheap loans at village level — "voluntary membership" and member service.

One thing is common to all of these — control lies with the members, and the aim is their welfare. That is the real test of the principles.

## How to apply the principles daily — for the secretary/director

Principles are meaningful only when they show up in daily work:

1. **Keep membership open** — admit eligible applicants as per the bye-laws without discrimination, keep records clean.
2. **AGM and elections on time** — follow one member, one vote; record quorum and minutes.
3. **Share profit correctly** — first the compulsory reserve/education fund, then member benefit; nothing arbitrary.
4. **Transparent accounts** — show accounts/reports to members when they ask; audit on time.
5. **Education** — explain the bye-laws, rights and procedures to new members/employees.
6. **Link with the federation** — cooperation and reporting with the district/state federation.

> 💡 With these six habits your society becomes not a "cooperative on paper" but a "cooperative in practice" — and its audit grading also improves.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More money = more votes"** — wrong; every member has only one vote.
- **"The society's aim is profit"** — the aim is member service; surplus is used in the interest of members/community.
- **"Principles are just textbook"** — in fact they apply directly to bye-laws, audit and profit sharing.
- **"Membership is closed to some"** — membership is open and voluntary for every eligible person.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many cooperative principles are there?** — Seven (adopted by the ICA in 1995), given in detail above.
**Difference between values and principles?** — Values = beliefs (why); principles = the way to put those beliefs into practice (how).
**Does putting in more money give more votes?** — No, every member has only one vote — this is democratic control.
**Where did these principles come from?** — From the Rochdale Pioneers of 1844, and formally in the ICA (Manchester) "Statement on the Cooperative Identity" in 1995.
**Do they apply in India?** — Yes; one member one vote, the reserve/education fund and the federation structure in state Acts reflect these very principles.
**What is the difference between equality and equity?** — Equality = equal rights (one member, one vote); equity = fair sharing of benefit in proportion to use/contribution (patronage).
**Can the principles change?** — They are a "living document"; the ICA has refined them several times (the last major revision at Manchester in 1995).
**Which principle matters most for the secretary?** — In practice "democratic control" and "openness/honesty" — because the AGM, elections and transparent accounts come straight from these.
**Does "open membership" mean anyone can join?** — The door is open to every eligible person without discrimination; but the eligibility and bye-law conditions must be met.
**Does taking a government grant end the society's autonomy?** — No, provided the agreement is such that member control and decision-making rights stay with the society.

## Three things to remember
- ⚖️ Control lies with people (one member, one vote), not with money.
- 🤝 The aim is the members' welfare — not the profit of outside investors.
- 🔍 Openness and honesty = transparent, trustworthy accounts.

## In the end
Once you understand the principles and values, everything else — governance, audit, sharing — starts to make sense on its own. Remember the seven principles, bring the ten values into daily decisions, and keep the accounts transparent — this is the real strength of cooperation, and this is what makes a society different from a company and lasting. Only when every member feels that control is in his hands and the accounts are clean does a society truly deserve to be called "cooperative".
