HAFED — that is, the Haryana State Cooperative Supply and Marketing Federation Limited — is not limited to grain procurement alone; it issues tenders for many kinds of work as per its needs. This guide explains the HAFED tender process through a real example — the PEG scheme for godown construction.

## In which areas does HAFED issue tenders?

Different kinds of tenders appear from time to time on the “Tenders” page of HAFED's website. The tenders seen in October 2026 included work such as leasing out HAFED's own warehouse premises and rice mills, e-auction of sunflower, and IT maintenance; the PEG godown tender came in January 2026; and in earlier years there have also been tenders such as transport/labour-cartage.

## HAFED's tender categories — in a little more detail

HAFED's tenders are broadly of these kinds:

- **Transport/labour contracts:** Contracts for carrying goods and for loading-unloading (seen in older tenders).
- **Leasing out its own property:** Leasing out HAFED's own warehouse premises, rice mill or flour mill for a fixed period (seen in the 2026 tenders).
- **Godown construction (PEG scheme):** Getting new covered godowns built by private entrepreneurs, in exchange for a guaranteed lease of 10 years (in detail below).
- **Sale/e-auction and services:** E-auction of produce, contracts for IT or other services.

So HAFED is a large buyer-and-service-provider system, with opportunities for everyone — farmers, transporters, godown owners and millers. Which category will open a tender and when depends on its operational needs.

## Example — the 10-year guaranteed PEG scheme for godown construction

A notice appeared on HAFED's website on 27 January 2026: a two-bid e-tender for getting godowns built under the “10 Years PEG Scheme” for FCI grain storage, on the **GeM portal**, on a Build-Own-Operate basis, with a guaranteed lease of 10 years; last date **13 February 2026** (source 1). According to a consultancy firm's summary (source 3; **matching with the tender document is pending**), its eligibility and conditions were as follows — any individual, firm or company could apply, and:

- The applicant should have their own land or CAP premises, or a registered lease deed of at least 13 years
- If there is no land, a commitment has to be given to buy/lease land within 120 days of tender acceptance — along with an additional bank guarantee of ₹100 per metric tonne
- The minimum godown capacity should be 5,000 metric tonnes, for which about 2 acres of land are needed (1.7 acres extra for each additional 5,000 MT unit)
- An Earnest Money Deposit (EMD) of ₹50 per metric tonne is compulsory
- The land should be connected to a national/state highway by a road at least 22 feet wide, no high-tension line of 11 KV or more should pass over it, there should be no polluting industry, brick kiln or petrol pump nearby, and the site should not be flood-affected

> 💡 The conditions above are an example from a specific old tender (last date 13 February 2026 as per HAFED's notice), so that readers understand the general structure of a HAFED tender — this tender is now closed. This is not an advertisement for any current open tender, but a case study to explain the process.

## Basic tender terms — EMD and bank guarantee

To understand a tender, you need to know two terms:

- **EMD (Earnest Money Deposit):** A security amount that the applicant deposits with the tender — it shows the application is serious. It can be forfeited if the applicant does not take up the work or breaks a condition; otherwise it is returned. In the example tender (as per the consultancy firm's summary) it was ₹50 per metric tonne.
- **Bank guarantee:** A written guarantee given by a bank that the applicant will fulfil their commitment; if they do not, HAFED can recover that amount from the bank. In the example, there was a condition of an additional bank guarantee of ₹100 per metric tonne if the applicant had no land.

Both these arrangements are common in government/cooperative tenders — their purpose is that only capable and serious applicants come forward.

## How to take part in a tender — general steps

Every tender has its own conditions, but the general process is usually like this:

1. **Portal registration:** Registration, and (where applicable) a digital signature, on the portal where the tender is (such as GeM for the PEG tender).
2. **Reading the tender document:** Carefully reading the eligibility, capacity, time limits and conditions.
3. **Documents and EMD:** Preparing the documents asked for and the EMD/bank guarantee.
4. **Submitting the bid:** Uploading the bid online before the last date.
5. **Evaluation and allotment:** After technical + financial evaluation, allotment to the eligible lowest/best bid.

> ⚠️ The steps above are for general understanding — the exact process, eligibility and dates of any tender should be treated as final only from that tender's official document.

## Applicant's checklist — before taking part

- [ ] Registration and digital signature (where applicable) on the relevant portal (GeM or the government e-tender portal).
- [ ] Tender document read in full — eligibility, capacity, time limits, penalty conditions.
- [ ] Land/lease (where applicable) and other eligibility documents ready.
- [ ] Arrangement of EMD and (if needed) bank guarantee.
- [ ] An honest assessment of financial capacity — construction/operating cost versus guaranteed income.
- [ ] Bid uploaded before the last date (avoid the last moment).

A practical tip — before bidding, work out the cost and benefit with a cool head. The guaranteed rent looks attractive, but you know the real profit only after deducting construction cost, interest and maintenance.



## Transparency in tenders and common mistakes

With government/cooperative tenders going online (e-tender), transparency has increased — bids stay sealed, open at a fixed time, and the evaluation standards are written in the document beforehand. Even so, new applicants often fall into these mistakes:

- **Bidding at the last moment:** Missing the bid because of a slow portal or upload failure — always submit ahead of time.
- **Incomplete documents:** If even one compulsory document/certificate is missing, the bid is technically disqualified.
- **Not reading the conditions fully:** Not reading the capacity, time limits or penalty conditions carefully — it costs dearly later.
- **Financial over-estimation:** Overstating income and understating cost — a realistic calculation is needed.

Avoiding these mistakes is half the success — the rest depends on eligibility and the right price.

## Advice for small entrepreneurs and societies

Tenders are not a game only for big companies. Small entrepreneurs and cooperative societies too, with the right preparation, can take part in tenders such as godown construction or transport. Start with a small capacity, first understand the full process of one tender, and move ahead with experience. For a cooperative society, this is also a chance to connect members to a new, lasting income activity — provided an honest assessment of risk and cost is done first. If needed, take guidance from an experienced person or the cooperative department.

## Why godown construction matters for the cooperative sector

Storage capacity is directly linked to the farmer's interest — if there are not enough godowns, the crop spoils in the open and the farmer has to sell early. Schemes like PEG encourage private and cooperative entrepreneurs to build godowns, which increases the state's total storage capacity. For a cooperative society too, a godown can be both a lasting source of income (rent/storage charges) and a service to members. (See: [Godown/storage scheme](/blog/pacs-godown-grain-storage-scheme-ami-aif-nabard).)

## An imaginary calculation — is the godown profitable or not

Suppose an entrepreneur is thinking of building a 5,000 MT godown. He adds up the total 10-year income from guaranteed rent, and deducts from it — the cost of land/lease, construction cost, interest on the bank loan, insurance, and yearly maintenance. What is left is the real profit. Often the guaranteed rent looks attractive, but after deducting interest and maintenance the margin turns out thin — especially if land has to be bought. That is why this cool calculation of "cost versus guaranteed income" is necessary before bidding. (This is only a framework for thinking, not any real figure — the actual numbers depend on the tender document and market rates.)

## If a society enters a tender — the accounting side

If a cooperative society enters a godown construction or transport tender, it should keep separate, clean accounts of this new activity — capital cost (godown construction), EMD/bank guarantee, rent income, maintenance expense — all separately. This keeps it clear whether the new activity is economically beneficial or not, and the audit also stays smooth. (See: [Basics of cooperative accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

## After winning the tender — agreement and monitoring

Winning the tender is not the end, it is the beginning. After allotment there is a formal agreement, which sets out the obligations, time limits, quality standards and penalty conditions. In cases like godown construction, the progress of construction and the standards are monitored, and if the work is not completed in the fixed time, the bank guarantee may be forfeited or a penalty imposed. So the applicant should honestly assess not just the bid but also the capacity for execution — because fulfilling the conditions of the agreement is the real test. This same discipline also builds credibility for future tenders.
