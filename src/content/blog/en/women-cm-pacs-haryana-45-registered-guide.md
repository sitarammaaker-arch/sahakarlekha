Within Haryana's CM-PACS expansion drive, a separate category is also emerging — women CM-PACS. This guide brings together and explains their current number, the picture of women's cooperative participation at the national level, and the schemes available for women-centred cooperative societies. For background, see our [guide on the number of CM-PACS](/blog/cm-pacs-list-haryana-district-wise-476-registered).

## Latest figure — registered women CM-PACS

According to the Performance Dashboard on the official online portal of RCS Haryana, on 18 October 2026 the state had **61 registered women CM-PACS** (Registered Women CM-PACS) on record — about 11% of the 546 registered CM-PACS in total. This number is growing fast: Wayback Machine copies of the same dashboard recorded 16 out of 367 on 11 June 2026 and 57 out of 529 on 8 October 2026. This is a separate, specially marked category, which shows that at the policy level women-led cooperative societies are being tracked separately.

## What does "61" mean

The figure of 61 may look small, but seen in context it is notable. 61 out of a total of 546 CM-PACS being in the women's category (~11%) shows that roughly one in every nine or ten CM-PACS is women-centred. For comparison, among all the cooperative societies in the country, women-only societies are about 2.52% — although the base of the two figures is different (one is only CM-PACS, the other all kinds of societies), so this is not a direct comparison, only a rough idea. And most importantly — **marking and tracking it separately** at the policy level shows that women's participation is not being left to "it will happen on its own", but is being promoted deliberately. Since this is a real-time figure, the number may go up further — so always see the latest position on the portal.

## The national picture — where do women stand in the cooperative sector?

According to an article by two NITI Aayog officers (Kurukshetra magazine, January 2023; the authors' personal views), of India's 8,54,300 cooperative societies only 2.52% are entirely of women (women-only) — the article took this figure from the International Cooperative Alliance (ICA). According to another report (ORF, May 2025, citing 2023 figures of the Ministry of Cooperation), of 24,264 women's cooperative societies only 10,806 were working — that is, more than half were not working — and the male-female ratio in cooperative membership was about 74:26. Against this background, Haryana's 61 registered women CM-PACS can be seen as a positive step, even if it is still at an early stage.

## The bigger picture of government support

Women's cooperatives are getting support at several levels — at the Centre, a separate Ministry of Cooperation and NCDC's women-centred schemes; in the state, separate tracking of the women's category in initiatives like CM-PACS; and on the ground, the wide base of the SHG network. Only when all three — policy, finance and grassroots organisation — come together do women's societies become sustainable. Just making a scheme or just registering a society is not enough; the real impact comes when a women's group gets capital, training and market access — all three together. So an interested women's group should gather information from all these sources (ARCS, NCDC, SHG federations) and move ahead with a concrete plan.

## Provision for women's representation in the PACS model bye-laws

According to bye-law 10(3) of the Ministry of Cooperation's [PACS model bye-laws](/blog/pacs-model-bye-laws-haryana-explained) (sent on 5 January 2023), every PACS board has a reservation for **2 women members and 1 Scheduled Caste/Scheduled Tribe member** — there is no limit on the number of members for this. It applies in a state only when that state/PACS has adopted these bye-laws. This shows that women's participation is not limited only to forming separate women-centred societies, but is also being included institutionally in mainstream cooperative governance.

## National schemes available for women's cooperative societies

Two schemes of the National Cooperative Development Corporation (NCDC) are important for women's cooperatives. Under **Swayam Shakti Sahakar**, NCDC gives money to cooperative credit institutions (PACS, district/state cooperative banks, SHG federations, etc.) so that they in turn give working capital or term loans to women's self-help groups (SHGs) — this requires the borrowing institution to be at least 3 years old, to have positive net worth and no cash loss in 3 years, so a new CM-PACS may not meet its conditions yet. Under **Nandini Sahakar**, women's cooperative societies (or those in which at least 50% of members are women) can take term loans, margin money and working capital; on term loans, subject to timely repayment, there is a 2% interest subvention for new/innovative activities and 1% for others. Haryana's women CM-PACS may be in a position to take the benefit of these schemes — see the details in the [NCDC cooperative credit schemes guide](/blog/ncdc-cooperative-credit-schemes-nandini-yuva-chini-mill), although confirmation of state-level implementation is needed separately.

## Why women's cooperatives matter

Women-led cooperative societies are not just a matter of figures — they connect rural women directly to economic participation, income and decision-making power. When a woman is herself a member and leader of the society, she gets not only income but also confidence, social identity and collective strength. In India, dairy cooperatives (like the Amul model) have shown that when women organise, the economic health of both the family and the village improves — because a woman's income often goes directly into children's education, nutrition and household savings. With this thinking, tracking the women's category separately in the CM-PACS expansion is a meaningful step. (See: [Role of women's cooperative societies](/blog/women-cooperatives-role).)

## How a women CM-PACS is formed

The legal framework of a women CM-PACS is the same as other CM-PACS — that is, the same minimum members, bye-laws and registration process, only with women prominent in the leadership/membership. A women's group wanting to form one has to follow the same path: gather enough interested members, prepare documents (Aadhaar, PPP, etc.), adopt the Model Bye-laws, and apply online to the ARCS. (Full process: [CM-PACS registration guide](/blog/cm-pacs-registration-process-haryana).) Women linked to self-help groups (SHGs) often form a natural base for this, because they already have the habit of saving and working together.

## Activities in which women CM-PACS can shine

The multipurpose structure of CM-PACS opens several suitable paths for women's societies, especially those where women's traditional participation is already strong:

- **Dairy and milk collection:** the most natural area for rural women — milk collection, processing and sale.
- **Food processing and packing:** value-addition activities like pickles, papad, spices and bakery.
- **Handicrafts and tailoring:** collective production and shared market access.
- **Consumer store / fair price:** supply of everyday goods at village level.

In these activities, women's societies not only earn income but also connect other women of the village to employment and markets — that is, one society becomes an economic support for many families.

## An example — from SHG to women CM-PACS

Suppose in a village a self-help group (SHG) of 15 women is already running savings and small loans. They have all three — experience of working together, mutual trust and the habit of saving. This same group can move ahead and get registered as a women CM-PACS: by preparing documents (Aadhaar, PPP), adopting the Model Bye-laws, and applying to the ARCS. After registration, once they meet the eligibility conditions, they can take a term loan from an NCDC scheme like Nandini Sahakar and start a milk collection or processing unit. A small start, but with clear accounts and a sustainable activity — this is the model that turns "getting formed" into "getting going". (This example is only to explain the path; confirm the exact eligibility and process from the official source.)

## Challenges — and their solutions

The national figures (2.52% women-centred; more than half of women's societies not working) also tell a bitter truth — women's societies do get formed, but surviving is hard. The common reasons behind this are — lack of capital, no experience of accounts and compliance, lack of market access, and the pressure of household responsibilities. The solutions lie there too: capital from schemes like NCDC's, less compliance burden through simple digital accounts, training, and starting with a small, sustainable activity. "Forming" is easy; real success lies in "keeping it running".

## The way ahead

If the goal stays only "forming more women CM-PACS", there is a risk of repeating the national experience (more than half inactive). The real direction should be from "numbers" towards "quality and sustainability" — that is, making every society that is formed active, profitable and self-reliant. This needs — regular training (accounting, compliance, management), easy capital (NCDC schemes), market linkage, and handholding (guidance in the early years). Where this support is available, a women CM-PACS becomes not just a registration number but a source of lasting income and dignity for the women of the village. Haryana's 61 societies are the first step in this direction — the real test is in seeing them flourish.

## Accounts and records — the foundation of sustainability

The biggest need of a women CM-PACS — clean, simple accounts. When every member's share/deposit/loan, and the income and expenditure of every activity, are recorded separately and correctly, the society passes the audit and also keeps the members' trust. Good, Hindi-centred cooperative software is a big help here, especially when the women running the society have little formal experience of accounting. Clean records are the foundation on which NCDC loans, government support and market trust rest. (See: [Basics of cooperative accounting](/blog/cooperative-accounting-basics).)

## Frequently asked questions

**How are women CM-PACS different from ordinary CM-PACS?** — It is a category tracked separately by RCS Haryana, where women's leadership/membership is prominent; the legal framework is the same as other CM-PACS.
**Which national schemes are available for women's cooperative societies?** — NCDC's Swayam Shakti Sahakar (loans to women's SHGs through cooperative credit institutions) and Nandini Sahakar (loans to women's cooperative societies, with interest subvention) are the two main schemes.
**What is the position of women-centred cooperative societies in India?** — According to an article by NITI Aayog officers (Kurukshetra, 2023, ICA figure), only 2.52% of all cooperative societies are entirely of women, and according to ministry figures (in the ORF report) more than half of women's societies were not working.
**How to form a women CM-PACS?** — The legal framework is the same as other CM-PACS — enough members, documents, Model Bye-laws and an online application to the ARCS; SHGs often make a good base.
**What is the biggest challenge?** — Forming is easy, keeping it running is hard — lack of capital, experience and market access; the answer is scheme loans, simple digital accounts and training.
**Is women's representation on the board compulsory?** — The model bye-laws (bye-law 10(3)) reserve 2 women and 1 SC/ST member on every PACS board; Section 28 of the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act has also required two women members on the committee since 2013.
**In which activities do women CM-PACS do well?** — Activities like dairy, food processing/packing, handicrafts-tailoring and consumer stores, where women's participation is already strong.
**Can an SHG become a women CM-PACS?** — Yes; an SHG already has savings, trust and experience of working together, which makes a natural base.
**What is the significance of the number "61"?** — ~11% of 546 CM-PACS being in the women's category, and its being counted separately on the dashboard, shows a policy priority; the comparison with the national 2.52% is only a rough idea because the base of the two is different.
**What is needed for sustainability?** — Capital (NCDC schemes), clean digital accounts, training and market access — all four together; just registering is not enough.
**Does this figure keep changing?** — Yes; "61" is the position on the live dashboard of RCS Haryana on 18 October 2026 — always confirm the latest number from the portal.

## In the end

Haryana's 61 registered women CM-PACS (18 October 2026) are a small but meaningful start — especially against the national background where women-centred cooperatives are still very limited. The real question goes beyond increasing the number — making them sustainable, which needs capital (NCDC schemes), clean accounts, training and market access. Where these four things come together, women's cooperatives become not just a figure but a force that changes the economic picture of the village — and a capable women's society brings many other women of the village forward with it. And remember — this "61" is a real-time figure; always confirm the latest number from the RCS Haryana portal.
