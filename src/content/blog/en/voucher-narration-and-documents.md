# Voucher narration and documents: the key to audit-ready books

Two societies can have the same figures — but in the audit, the difference comes from how **clean and backed by evidence** their entries are. There are two keys to this: a good **narration** and the right **supporting documents**. This article explains both.

## Their importance grows with time

On the day you make an entry, you remember everything — which payment, for what, to whom. But six months or a year later, when the auditor comes or a member asks a question, the figure alone ("₹2,000") tells you nothing. That is when the narration and document you added that same day are useful. So these two habits are really a clear message left for "the future you" — and they become the society's collective memory, which stays even when office-bearers change.

## What a narration is and why
A narration is a short note written with every voucher that explains the transaction in simple words — for what, from/to whom, and any reference (bill/cheque number). This article explains **how to write a good narration and which documents to keep** — with examples, samples for each type and the audit view.

> 💡 A good narration = books that anyone can understand, even months later, without asking. This is the biggest help for the auditor and for new staff.

**Weak:** "Payment ₹2,000"
**Good:** "Office rent June — to landlord Shri X, cheque 004512"

### The three parts of a good narration

A good narration answers three questions:

1. **For what?** — the purpose of the transaction (rent, salary, fertiliser purchase).
2. **From/to whom?** — the other party (landlord, employee, supplier).
3. **Which reference?** — bill/receipt/cheque/UTR number and period.

If all three are there, anyone understands the entry at once, even months later. So keep these three questions in mind while writing a narration. (Voucher basics: [Voucher entry guide](/blog/voucher-entry-guide).)

### Sample narrations by type

| Transaction | Good narration |
| --- | --- |
| Receipt | "Share capital from member Ramkumar — receipt #212" |
| Payment | "August electricity bill — UHBVN, bill #4471" |
| Contra | "Day's cash deposited in bank — SBI, slip #08" |
| Journal | "March depreciation — on furniture" |
| Purchase | "50 bags of fertiliser — M/s XYZ, bill #991" |

Note — every sample has all three: purpose, party and reference. This habit makes the books "speak".

## Supporting document
This is the evidence attached to the voucher — bill, receipt, invoice — which shows that the transaction really happened and for the stated amount. While the narration tells "what and why", the document gives proof that "this is true". Only when both are together does an entry become fully trustworthy — a narration without a document is an incomplete claim, and a document without a narration is an incomplete story.

> ⚠️ Entries without evidence are the most common audit objection. Always keep a bill/receipt with every large voucher — a receipt is better even for small payments.

### Which document for which transaction

| Transaction | Required document |
| --- | --- |
| Purchase | Supplier's bill/invoice |
| Sale | Copy of the sale receipt/invoice |
| Payment | Bill + proof of payment (cheque/UTR) |
| Salary | Salary slip and attendance record |
| Cash expense | Seller's receipt or slip |
| Bank transaction | Bank slip/statement |
| Managing committee decision | Copy of the relevant minutes |
| Loan sanction | Application, sanction resolution and documents |

Treat documents as compulsory for large or regular transactions; keeping a slip even for very small cash expenses is a good habit. Where no bill is available (such as wages), take the payee's signature on a self-declared voucher. A simple rule — the larger the amount, the stronger the document needed; and for a transaction that happens again and again, fix its documentation method in advance, so you don't have to run around every time.

### Good vs weak narration — more examples

| Weak | Good |
| --- | --- |
| "Expense 500" | "Stationery purchase — bill #212, M/s ABC" |
| "Deposit 10000" | "Member loan recovery — Ramkumar, receipt #45" |
| "Bank 50000" | "Cash deposited in bank — SBI, slip #08" |
| "Correction" | "JV/210 correction — wrong account, corrected by JV/215" |

The difference is clear — a weak narration leaves questions, a good narration gives answers. Writing just a few more words makes the entry clear forever.

## What the two give together
| Without | With |
| --- | --- |
| "What was this entry for?" | Clear at once from the narration |
| "Did this really happen?" | Proven by the document |
| Objection in audit | Smooth audit, fewer objections |

## Narration for corrections and cancellations

When correcting a mistake or cancelling a voucher, the narration becomes even more important — because this is where the auditor asks the most questions. Never write just "correction"; write the reason and the reference. For example:

- **On cancellation:** "PV/312 cancelled — amount entered wrongly (₹500 instead of ₹5,000), new correct entry PV/318".
- **On adjustment:** "Bank charges, as per statement, August — found in BRS".

A clean narration like this shows the correction was honest, not an attempt to hide something. (Don't delete a wrong voucher, cancel it — [10 common mistakes](/blog/common-accounting-mistakes).)

## When to use a self-voucher

For some expenses no outside bill is available — such as daily wages, small gifts, or internal adjustments. In such cases make a **self-voucher** with the full narration and, where possible, take the payee's signature or thumb impression. It is not as strong as an outside bill, but it is far better than "no evidence" and remains acceptable in audit, provided the narration is clear and the amount is reasonable.

## Link with segregation of duties

Narration and documents become even stronger when they come with **approval**. One person makes the voucher (adds the narration + document), another checks and approves it — this is maker-checker. This not only catches mistakes, but also builds trust because two people have looked at every entry. In a digital system this approval mark is recorded automatically.

## Two pictures of the same books

See how the same ₹8,000 rent payment looks in two societies:

- **Society A:** narration "Payment 8000", no bill. The auditor asks — whose, for what? Finding the answer takes hours, and an objection is recorded.
- **Society B:** narration "June rent — landlord Shri X, cheque #004512", with the rent receipt and a copy of the cheque. The auditor is satisfied at a glance and moves on.

The figure is the same in both, but the experience is worlds apart — the difference is only these two habits.

## How to store documents and for how long

It is not enough to just keep documents; they must be kept in order — only then will you find them at once when needed:

- **In order:** file them in order of voucher number/date, so the evidence for any entry is found quickly.
- **Digital copy:** scan the bill/receipt and attach it to the voucher; the risk of paper tearing/getting lost goes down.
- **Period:** keep documents safe for several years (the exact period depends on law/bye-laws and tax rules — confirm with your auditor).
- **Backup:** take regular backups of digital records, so the data stays safe even if there is a technical problem.
- **Security:** keep access to sensitive documents limited, so there is no tampering.

In a digital system documents stay attached directly to the voucher, so at audit time no time is lost searching for "where is this bill?".

## Digital vs paper documents

| Aspect | Paper | Digital (scan) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk of loss | Higher (tearing, termites) | Lower (backup possible) |
| Finding | Flipping through files | Attached directly to the voucher |
| Sharing | A copy must be made | Can be sent at once |
| Space | Fills up cupboards | No physical space |

The best way is a mix of both — keep the original paper documents safe as per the rules, and attach their scanned copy to the voucher. This meets both day-to-day digital convenience and the legal requirement.

## Small habit, big benefit

Writing the narration and attaching the document takes only a few seconds per entry — but the benefit lasts all year: clean reports, easy audit, fewer disputes, and records a new office-bearer can understand. On the other hand, putting them off saves a few seconds but later brings hours of searching and audit objections. So treat this not as "extra work" but as "saving for the future" — this discipline turns ordinary books into audit-ready books.

## Through the auditor's eyes

On every large transaction the auditor looks at three things: is the **narration** clear, is the **document** present, and has **someone approved** it (maker-checker). If all three are there, the entry passes without questions; if even one is missing, an objection arises. So keeping clean narrations and documents really means making the audit easy in advance. ## Common mistakes

- **Blank/one-word narration** → single words like "payment", "expense" are useless; write a full sentence with purpose-party-reference.
- **Not keeping documents** → treat the bill/receipt of every large voucher as compulsory, otherwise an audit objection is certain.
- **Habit of writing later** → write the narration at the time of entry, don't leave it to memory, otherwise it stays incomplete.
- **Misuse of "miscellaneous"** → putting large expenses under a vague head brings audit objections; always choose a clear head.
- **Keeping documents scattered** → file them in order of voucher number/date, otherwise they can't be found when needed.

## Frequently asked questions
**Why is a narration necessary?** — It is what makes the entry understandable and auditable.
**Is a one-word narration enough?** — No; a short sentence with purpose + party + reference is far better.
**Is a receipt needed even for small payments?** — Yes, it is better to keep one — it helps in the audit.
**What should a good narration contain?** — Three things: purpose, the other party, and reference (bill/cheque number and period).
**Which document for which transaction?** — A bill for purchases, a slip for salary, a statement for the bank, minutes for decisions.
**What if no bill is available?** — Take the payee's signature on a self-declared voucher (such as for wages).
**How long should documents be kept?** — For several years; the exact period depends on law/tax rules and the bye-laws — confirm with your auditor.
**Is a digital copy valid?** — Scanning and attaching it to the voucher is a good habit; also keep the original document safe as per the rules.
**What to write on a cancelled voucher?** — Not just "correction"; write the reason and the reference of the new entry, so the audit trail stays clean.
**When should the narration be written?** — At the time of entry itself; if put off for later, the narration stays incomplete or wrong.
**How is maker-checker related to narration?** — The maker adds the narration/document, the checker approves — all three together make the entry strong.

## Three things to remember
- 📝 A clear narration on every entry — purpose + party + reference.
- 📎 A bill/receipt with every voucher, stored in order.
- ✅ Narration + document + approval = audit-ready, trustworthy books.
