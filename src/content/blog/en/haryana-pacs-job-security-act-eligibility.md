To give "permanent-like" service security to employees who have long worked in Haryana on contract, ad-hoc or outsourced basis, the state government has brought a special law — the **Haryana Contractual Employees (Security of Service) Act, 2024**. The question is whether employees working in PACS, CMS and other cooperative societies come within its scope — and this is where there is the most confusion. This guide makes everything clear: the Act's conditions, the "Government Organization" dispute, the courts' stand and the practical steps for an employee.

## The basics of the Act

> ⚠️ **Information checked up to 18 October 2026.** The Act, its 2025 amendment and the 2025 Rules were read from Gazette copies; court judgments from Indian Kanoon copies (see "Sources" below).

This Act (Haryana Act No. 17 of 2024) was published in the Gazette on 6 December 2024. It is **deemed to have come into force from 14 August 2024**, and the eligibility cut-off date ("appointed date") is **15 August 2024** (Sections 1(1), 2(a)). The aim is clear — to protect employees who have long worked on contract in government and semi-government bodies from sudden removal, and to give them continuity of service and better pay within a set framework.

## Three conditions of eligibility

To get the benefit of the Act, an "eligible contractual employee" must meet these conditions together:

- The employee is engaged on contract and their monthly remuneration is up to ₹50,000 — or they are deployed through Haryana Kaushal Rozgar Nigam (HKRN) under the Deployment of Contractual Persons Policy, 2022 (the Act does not state a pay limit for this case) (Section 3(i)).
- They have completed at least **5 years** of full-time service by 15 August 2024. After the 2025 amendment, a year is counted when payment was made for at least 240 days in that year (Section 3(ii), Explanation 2).

> ⚠️ These people do not come within the scope of this Act (proviso to Section 3): employees engaged in Centrally Sponsored Schemes, whose cost is paid fully or partly by the Central Government; those working on honorarium; part-time employees; persons who had turned 58 on the appointed date; and employees already removed before the Act came into force. Regular/permanent employees are not "contractual employees" anyway. As per the 2025 Rules, a person who marries a second time while their spouse is alive is also ineligible (the government can give an exemption).

## The biggest question: does a "cooperative society" come under it?

This is the point with the most confusion. **Section 2(f)** of the Act defines "Government Organization" (that is, the body to which this Act applies) — it lists departments, boards, corporations and "authority", but **the words "Co-operative Society" are not directly present in this definition.**

The plain meaning is — this Act does not apply automatically, without any additional legal clarification, to a registered cooperative society (whether PACS, CMS or any other). Under **Section 39** of the Haryana Cooperative Societies Act 1984, every registered society is a "body corporate" in itself — a body with its own separate legal identity, perpetual succession, seal, and the power to hold property, enter into contracts and sue. Usually its employees are governed by its own service rules (Staff Service Rules), meaning it is not part of any government department. This is why including societies in the definition of "Government Organization" is not a self-evident matter.

## What being a "body corporate" means

The practical meaning of being a "body corporate" under Section 39 is that the society is an independent legal person — it has its own seal, its own property, its own contracts, and its own service rules for its employees. It is not "a part of the government" like a government department, but a separate body owned and controlled by its members. That is why it is not easy for a government-employee law to apply to it automatically — it must first be shown that the government has so much control over that particular society that it falls within the definition of "Government Organization". (More on this "control vs supervision" principle: [Writ jurisdiction and cooperative societies](/blog/cooperative-society-writ-jurisdiction-article-226).)

## Direction from court judgments

Some 2026 judgments of the Punjab and Haryana High Court (Justice Harpreet Singh Brar) have given partial direction on this ambiguity. These judgments concern employees of **HAFED** — that is, the Haryana State Cooperative Supply and Marketing Federation Limited, which is itself a cooperative body:

- **Dinesh Kumar v. State of Haryana (CWP-5144-2026, 27 February 2026):** The petition of security guards deployed in HAFED through HKRN was allowed. Treating HAFED as a "Government Organization", the court asked the state to add it to the job-security portal and asked HAFED to issue service-security orders within 8 weeks. HAFED did not contest this; the judgment does not contain a separate analysis of Section 2(f).
- **Yoginder Singh v. State of Haryana (CWP-8381-2026, 19 March 2026):** On the basis of the same judgment, the court said that the Dinesh Kumar judgment operates "in rem", that is, it applies to all similarly placed employees of HAFED.

Keep in mind — these judgments are for HAFED; they do not give blanket coverage to every cooperative society. In some cooperative bank cases (such as **Rajat Deswal v. Jind Central Cooperative Bank, CWP-13135-2026, 30 April 2026**) the court told the bank to pass a reasoned order on the employees' claim within 3 months in the light of the Dinesh Kumar judgment — that is, it left the decision to the bank and did not itself declare coverage.

In a separate case — **Chhabilla v. State of Haryana (CWP-24118-2025, 15 December 2025)** — a driver deployed through HKRN had been removed on the ground that "there is no vehicle". The court set aside the removal order, because no show-cause notice was given, there was no hearing, and HKRN's instructions and the 2022 deployment policy were not followed. This shows that however the "Government Organization" question is settled, due process in termination of service is required in every case.

> 💡 That is, the courts have so far not laid down any general rule like "all cooperative societies come under this Act". Relief was given in HAFED's case, but for other bodies each case is being looked at separately.

## What "in rem" means

One term that causes confusion here — "in rem". In simple words, a judgment is "in rem" when it is not limited to just the two parties but applies to all similar people in that category. In the HAFED case (Yoginder Singh, 2026) this meant that the judgment is not limited to the employee who filed the petition, but will apply to all similarly placed eligible employees of HAFED. But this "in rem" effect is also limited to HAFED — it does not automatically extend to the employees of every other cooperative body. So an employee of any other society will have to get their case examined separately.

## What you get if the benefit applies

The main benefits an eligible contractual employee gets under this Act are as follows:

| Benefit | In brief |
| --- | --- |
| Service security | Right to keep working until the age of retirement (Section 4); subject to discipline rules (Section 7) |
| Remuneration | Consolidated pay based on the entry pay of the pay level of the relevant post, not less than the pay on the appointed date; increase with DA on 1 January and 1 July (Section 5) |
| Seniority-based increase | 5% of entry pay (5 to 8 years of service), 10% (8 to 10 years), 15% (more than 10 years) — First Schedule |
| Other benefits | Health cover under PM-JAY "Chirayu", gratuity, maternity benefit, and ex-gratia/compassionate appointment under HKRN — First Schedule |

## A lesson for the society (employer) too

This issue is not only the employee's — it matters for the employer society too. The cooperative bank driver case gives a clear lesson: even if the dispute over the definition of "Government Organization" is not settled, **due process in termination of service is required in every case.** That is, when removing any contractual worker/employee, the society should follow the procedure, notice and records as per rules — otherwise the termination may be set aside on the ground of a procedural flaw alone. So societies should keep their employee records, service rules and termination procedure clear and documented.

## The body that has to decide this — an ongoing process

Since the question of whom to treat as a "Government Organization" has come up for many kinds of bodies (not limited to cooperative societies), according to a news report (The Tribune) the state government has formed a high-level committee chaired by the Additional Chief Secretary (Finance), which will decide the list of bodies that fall within the word "authority" in Section 2(f). We have not seen the government order for this, and the report does not mention cooperative societies separately. Until a clear, general guideline or notification comes on this, every cooperative society and its contractual employees will have to examine their particular case separately — based on the body's structure, government control and the courts' current stand.

> ⚠️ This Act is still going through a phase of interpretation. Before claiming eligibility, an employee of any cooperative society should get clarity on their particular case from their ARCS/DRCS office or a legal adviser — simply saying "we too are a government-like body" is not enough to make a claim.

## What an employee should practically do

If you are a contractual/ad-hoc employee of a cooperative society and think you may be eligible, move step by step instead of making a hasty claim:

1. **Check the conditions:** engaged on contract and remuneration up to ₹50,000 (or HKRN deployment), and 5 years of full-time service by 15 August 2024 (payment for at least 240 days each year) — are these met?
2. **Check the exceptions:** are you in the category of a Centrally Sponsored Scheme, part-time/honorarium, or aged 58+ on the appointed date?
3. **Look at the body's character:** what level of government control is there over your society — this will decide whether it is close to a "Government Organization" or not.
4. **Collect documents:** appointment letter, proof of service, pay records — whatever proves eligibility.
5. **Take advice:** clarity on your particular case from the ARCS/DRCS or a legal adviser.

That is, the claim should be based on facts and documents, not just on assumption — this is the right and successful route.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does every PACS/CMS contractual employee automatically come under this Act?** — No; the words cooperative society are not directly in the "Government Organization" definition of Section 2(f), so it does not apply automatically.
**Does this mean a cooperative society's contractual employee will never get this benefit?** — Not necessarily; in 2026 the High Court held employees of HAFED (which is itself a cooperative federation) to be within the scope. For other bodies, decisions are being made case by case for now.
**Does this Act apply to a regular/permanent cooperative society employee?** — No; it is only for eligible contractual/ad-hoc/outsourced employees; regular employees are governed by their separate Service Rules.
**What is the minimum service needed for eligibility?** — At least 5 years of full-time service by 15 August 2024; each year counts when payment was made for at least 240 days in it.
**What does an "in rem" judgment mean?** — A judgment that is not limited to the petitioner but applies to all similar people in that category; this effect of the HAFED judgment (Yoginder Singh, 2026) is limited to HAFED.
**What difference does being a "body corporate" make?** — The society is an independent legal body (Section 39), not a part of a government department — so a government law does not apply automatically.
**What first step should an employee take?** — Check the three eligibility conditions and the exceptions, collect documents, and get clarity on your case from the ARCS/DRCS or a legal adviser.
**Can a society remove an employee without due process?** — No; whatever the coverage dispute, due process (notice/records) is required in termination of service, otherwise the termination may be set aside.

## Three things to remember

- ⚖️ The words "Co-operative Society" are not directly included in the "Government Organization" definition of Section 2(f) — coverage does not come automatically.
- 🏛️ The courts have so far taken a case-by-case approach — no single uniform rule applies to all cooperative societies.
- 📋 For eligibility you need these conditions together: engaged on contract and remuneration up to ₹50,000 (or HKRN deployment), and 5 years of full-time service by 15 August 2024.

## In the end

The Haryana Contractual Employees (Security of Service) Act, 2024 is a big relief for employees who have long worked on contract — but for cooperative society employees its scope is not yet fully clear. The most important thing to understand is that a cooperative society is an independent "body corporate" (Section 39), so this Act does not apply to it automatically — coverage depends on the body's structure and government control, and on case-by-case court interpretation. So the right route for an employee is — first check the three eligibility conditions and the exceptions, collect documents, and before making a claim get clarity on your particular case from the ARCS/DRCS or a legal adviser. Facts and documents, not assumptions, are the basis of a successful claim.
