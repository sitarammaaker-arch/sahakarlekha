# How to do depreciation: a simple explanation

A society's assets — furniture, computers, buildings, vehicles — grow old over time and lose their value. Showing this falling value as an expense every year is **depreciation (ghisai)**. It is necessary for correct profit and a correct Balance Sheet. This article explains **how to do depreciation** — why, which method, the entry, examples and common mistakes — in simple language.

## Why depreciation is necessary
If furniture worth ₹60,000 keeps showing at ₹60,000 year after year, the Balance Sheet becomes false and profit looks inflated. By reducing a little of the value every year, the **real position** is shown.

Behind this is a basic accounting idea — the **matching principle**: the cost of an asset that is used for several years should also be spread over those same years, not shown in one year. So furniture worth ₹60,000 is not fully an "expense" in the first year; instead its wear shows as an expense a little every year. This keeps each year's profit correct and comparable. If you expense the whole cost in the first year, that year's profit will look unfairly low and the other years' profit too high — which is not the real picture.

## Which assets it applies to

Depreciation applies to **durable (capital) assets** that are used for more than one year — furniture, computers, vehicles, machinery, buildings. Land usually does not carry depreciation (it does not wear out), and day-to-day expenses (electricity, rent, stationery) are out of the question — they are already expenses of the same year. This difference between capital and revenue expenditure is important. (See: [10 common mistakes](/blog/common-accounting-mistakes).)

## Two common methods
- **SLM (Straight Line):** An **equal** amount is reduced every year.
- **WDV (Written Down Value):** A **fixed % on the remaining value** is reduced every year (more in the early years, less later).

| Aspect | SLM | WDV |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basis | Original cost | Remaining value |
| Amount each year | Equal | Keeps falling |
| Suited to | Assets with steady wear (furniture, buildings) | Assets that age fast (computers, vehicles) |

> 💡 Which method and rate — this depends on the asset type and the rules that apply. All percentages in this article are only **examples**; confirm the exact rate and method that apply to your society from **your rules/auditor**, because these change with the law and the asset type. Once you choose a method, do not change it from year to year.

## What is included in the "cost" of an asset

The "cost" on which depreciation is charged is not just the purchase price — it includes all necessary expenses to make the asset ready for use: transport, installation, and initial setup. For example, furniture of ₹55,000 + ₹5,000 transport/assembly = total cost ₹60,000, and depreciation will be charged on this ₹60,000. So at the time of purchase, do not treat these expenses as a separate "expense" but add them to the cost of the asset.

## What is useful life

Every asset has an estimated "useful life" — that is, how many years it will be used. A computer has a short life (it becomes outdated fast), while a building has a very long life. The method and rate depend largely on this: the shorter the life, the faster the depreciation. That is why WDV (fast) is often chosen for computers/vehicles and SLM (steady) for buildings/furniture.

## Example — furniture of ₹60,000, SLM 10%
₹6,000 will be reduced every year (here 10% is only an example rate):

| Year | Opening value | Depreciation | Remaining value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ₹60,000 | ₹6,000 | ₹54,000 |
| 2 | ₹54,000 | ₹6,000 | ₹48,000 |
| 3 | ₹48,000 | ₹6,000 | ₹42,000 |

## Example — WDV 10% (for comparison)

Now see the same ₹60,000 furniture at WDV 10% — 10% on the **remaining value** every year:

| Year | Opening value | Depreciation (10%) | Remaining value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ₹60,000 | ₹6,000 | ₹54,000 |
| 2 | ₹54,000 | ₹5,400 | ₹48,600 |
| 3 | ₹48,600 | ₹4,860 | ₹43,740 |

Note — in SLM an equal ₹6,000 was reduced every year, but in WDV the amount kept falling (₹6,000 → ₹5,400 → ₹4,860). This is the basic difference between the two methods.

**Which one to choose when?** SLM suits assets that wear at a steady pace (buildings, furniture) — here it makes sense to show an equal expense every year. WDV suits assets that age fast at first (computers, vehicles) — here showing more depreciation in the early years is closer to the real position. But the most important thing — whatever the choice, fix it once for each asset class and keep it consistent; changing the method again and again makes reports incomparable and raises questions in the audit.

## Salvage (residual) value

Often some value (scrap/salvage value) remains even at the end of an asset's life — like the amount received on selling an old vehicle. In SLM it is often deducted in the calculation: depreciation per year = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life (years). For example ₹60,000 cost, ₹6,000 residual, 9 years life → per year (60,000 − 6,000) ÷ 9 = ₹6,000.

## How to make the entry
One **journal entry** every year:

| Account | Dr | Cr |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Depreciation expense | ₹6,000 | — |
| Asset (furniture) | — | ₹6,000 |

With this the expense goes to the Income & Expenditure Account, and the value of the asset falls in the Balance Sheet.

## Effect on reports — double

Depreciation is a unique expense, because it touches two reports at once:

- **Income & Expenditure Account:** Depreciation is an expense, so it reduces profit.
- **Balance Sheet:** The value of the asset falls, so the assets side becomes smaller.

But note — in depreciation **no cash actually goes out** (it is a "non-cash" expense). The money went out when the asset was bought; depreciation only spreads that cost over the years. That is why it does not appear in the Receipts & Payments Account, but shows in both the Income & Expenditure and the Balance Sheet. (How reports link up: [How to read financial reports](/blog/how-to-read-financial-reports).)

## Depreciation and profit distribution

Since depreciation reduces profit, it also affects profit distribution — "profit" worked out without charging correct depreciation is inflated, and a dividend paid on it reduces the society's real capital. So always charge depreciation before distribution, so that the "distributable profit" is real. (See: [Profit distribution and reserve fund](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves).)

> 🔍 If you show "total depreciation" (accumulated) separately, both the original cost of the asset and the total depreciation so far stay clear.

### Two ways — direct reduction vs accumulated

The entry can be made in two ways:

- **Direct reduction:** As above, reduce the asset account itself every year.
- **Accumulated depreciation account:** Keep the asset at original cost, and keep adding the depreciation to a separate "accumulated depreciation" account. The Balance Sheet shows original cost − accumulated depreciation = remaining value.

The second way is more transparent, because it shows both the original cost of the asset and the total depreciation so far — separately and clearly. For example, if the Balance Sheet shows "Furniture ₹60,000 (−) accumulated depreciation ₹18,000 = ₹42,000", the reader at once understands how old the asset is. That is why bigger societies often adopt the accumulated-depreciation method.

## Asset register

Keep a separate record of every fixed asset — purchase date, cost, method, rate, each year's depreciation and remaining value. This "asset register" is the first thing asked for in the audit, and it shows how much depreciation has been charged on which asset. In a digital system this register and the depreciation schedule are generated automatically, so the details of every asset are available in one click and the year-end journal entry is also ready.

## On selling/removing an asset

When an asset is sold or removed, compare the sale price with its remaining book value:

- **Sale > remaining value** → profit (income).
- **Sale < remaining value** → loss (expense).

This profit/loss shows in the Income & Expenditure Account, and the asset and its accumulated depreciation are removed from the books. Recording this is necessary, otherwise an asset that has been sold stays in the books and keeps the Balance Sheet wrong.

**Example:** Suppose a vehicle's remaining book value is ₹40,000 and it was sold for ₹45,000 → ₹5,000 profit (income). If the same had sold for ₹35,000 → ₹5,000 loss (expense). In both cases the vehicle and its accumulated depreciation are removed from the books, and cash/bank goes up. This "asset disposal" entry should be clearly visible in the audit.

## Common mistakes

- **Not charging depreciation** → both assets and profit look inflated, and distribution is also wrong.
- **Changing the method again and again** → year-to-year comparison breaks; keep one method consistently for each asset class.
- **Treating capital as expense** → expensing a durable asset fully in one year is wrong; treat it as an asset and charge depreciation.
- **Not removing a sold asset** → a ghost asset stays in the books and the Balance Sheet stays wrong.
- **Not keeping an asset register** → reconciling accounts in the audit becomes hard.
- **Not adding transport/installation to cost** → the real cost of the asset looks lower, and depreciation is also wrong.
- **Charging depreciation on land** → land does not wear out; depreciation is usually not charged on it.

## Three things to remember
- 🗓️ Charge depreciation **every year at year-end** — do not forget.
- 📐 Use one method (SLM/WDV) consistently every year.
- 🧾 A separate record for every asset — purchase date, cost, rate and remaining value.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is depreciation?** — Showing the falling value of a durable asset as an expense every year, so that profit and the Balance Sheet stay correct.
**Difference between SLM and WDV?** — In SLM an equal amount every year; in WDV a fixed % on the remaining value, so the amount keeps falling.
**Which assets does it apply to?** — Durable capital assets (furniture, vehicles, machinery, buildings); usually not on land.
**Which rate should be applied?** — It depends on the law and the asset type; confirm the exact rate from your rules/auditor (the % in this article are only examples).
**What is the entry?** — Depreciation expense Dr, asset (or accumulated depreciation) Cr.
**What if depreciation is not charged?** — Both profit and assets will look inflated, and an audit objection will arise.
**What to do on selling an asset?** — Compare the sale with the remaining value, record the profit/loss, and remove the asset from the books.
**Does cash go out in depreciation?** — No; it is a non-cash expense, so it does not appear in Receipts & Payments, but it affects profit and the Balance Sheet.
**What should be added to cost?** — Along with the purchase price, necessary expenses like transport and installation; do not treat these as separate expenses.
**What is an accumulated depreciation account?** — Keeping the asset at original cost and adding depreciation to a separate account, so both the cost and total depreciation show clearly.
**Why depreciation before distribution?** — Profit without depreciation is inflated; a dividend paid on it reduces real capital.

## In the end
Depreciation is the key to correct profit and a correct Balance Sheet. Charge it every year at year-end, keep one method consistently, make a record of every asset, and remove sold assets on time. In a digital system the **depreciation schedule** is generated automatically and the journal entry is ready, so this job takes only minutes.
