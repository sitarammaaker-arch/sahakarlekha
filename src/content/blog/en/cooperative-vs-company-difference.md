# Cooperative Society vs Company: The Key Differences in Simple Words

A new society's secretary or director often wonders — "Why don't we run like a company?" Once you understand the **cooperative society vs company** difference, the whole logic of cooperative accounting, audit and governance becomes clear. Both are separate legal entities, and limited liability applies to both — but their paths are completely different on control, purpose, tax and profit-sharing. This article explains each aspect plainly, with examples, the legal basis and sources.

## The biggest difference: who holds control

- **In a cooperative society**, control rests with *people* — **each member has one vote**, no matter how many shares they hold.
- **In a company**, control rests with *money* — the more shares, the more votes.

**Example:** In a society, a member with ₹50,000 in shares and a member with ₹500 in shares — both have one vote each. In a company, the one with more shares has more control. The direct effect is that no single wealthy person can take over a society — the strength lies in the number of members, not in capital. This is the "soul of cooperation". (See: [Cooperative principles and values](/blog/cooperative-principles-and-values).)

## The second difference: purpose

- A society's purpose is the **welfare of its members** — fair prices, cheap loans, an assured market.
- A company's purpose is usually to **maximise profit for investors**.

**Example:** A dairy society's goal is that the member-farmer gets a fair and timely payment for milk; a private dairy company's goal is that its investors get the maximum profit. Both buy milk, but "for whose benefit" — that is the real difference.

## Point-by-point comparison

| Point | Cooperative society | Company |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Law | State Cooperative Act / MSCS Act 2002 | Companies Act, 2013 |
| Registrar | Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RCS/CRCS) | RoC (MCA) |
| Minimum members | Usually 10 (varies by state) | Private 2, Public 7, OPC 1 |
| Control | One member, one vote | Votes in proportion to shares |
| Purpose | Member welfare | Investor profit |
| Owners | Members (who are also users) | Shareholders |
| Management | Elected managing committee | Board of Directors (Board) |
| Shares | Transferred under society rules, not in the market | Traded in the market (if listed) |
| Profit | Reserve + member welfare/patronage | Shareholder dividend |
| Tax | Section 80P deduction (as per eligibility) | Normal company rates |

## Legal basis and registration

- A **company** is formed under the **Companies Act, 2013** and comes under the **Registrar of Companies (RoC), Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA)**.
- A **cooperative society** is formed under the **State Cooperative Societies Act** (or the **MSCS Act, 2002** for multi-state) and comes under the **Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RCS/CRCS)**.
- **Minimum members:** Forming a society usually needs **at least 10 members** (this may vary by state); whereas a private company is formed with 2, a public company with 7 (a one-person company with 1).

(See: [Central Registrar (CRCS) — FAQ](https://crcs.gov.in/faq) and [Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA)](https://www.mca.gov.in). For a new society: [How to form a cooperative society](/blog/how-to-register-a-cooperative-society).)

## Legal status and liability

The good news — on this point both are the same:

- **Separate legal status:** After registration, both can hold property in their own name, enter into contracts, and sue or be sued.
- **Limited liability:** A member's/shareholder's liability is usually limited to their contribution/shares.

The difference is not in status, but in **control and purpose** — and that decides everything that follows.

## The difference in capital and shares

- **A company's shares** are usually freely transferable (traded in the market in a listed company).
- **A society's shares** are not sold in the market; their transfer/refund is subject to the society's bye-laws and rules.
- In a society, the purpose of capital is not control but the member's **participation** — so "one member, one vote" stays in place, no matter how much capital someone has put in. This is why a society cannot raise large capital quickly, but it builds the collective strength of many small people.

## Member vs shareholder — the difference in role

- **A society's member** is usually **also a user** — the farmer who takes a loan, the milk producer who supplies milk, the consumer who buys goods. That is, the owner and the customer are the same person.
- **A company's shareholder** need not be a customer — they are often just an **investor** who wants a return.

That is why a society's decisions centre on "what benefit for the member", while a company's centre on "how much return for the investor".

## Management and governance

- A **company** is run by a **Board of Directors**, elected by shareholders (votes in proportion to shares).
- A **society** is run by an **elected managing committee**, chosen by members on **one-member-one-vote**; big decisions are taken in the **general body meeting (AGM)**. (See: [Role of the managing committee](/blog/managing-committee-role).)

**Example:** In a company, a person holding 51% of the shares can dominate the board alone; in a society with 100 members, each has an equal single vote — no one person can become the "owner". This democratic safeguard is the identity of a society.

## The difference in tax — Section 80P

- A **cooperative society** gets a special tax deduction under **Section 80P** on certain activities (such as credit to members, marketing of agricultural produce, cottage industry). Note — institutions controlled by RBI (cooperative banks) are generally an exception to this.
- A **company** pays normal company tax rates (no cooperative-specific deduction like 80P).

(See: [Income Tax Department — Section 80P](https://www.incometaxindia.gov.in/w/section-80p-58) and a simple explanation of [Section 80P](https://cleartax.in/s/section-80p). Check exact eligibility with your CA.)

> ⚠️ A common misunderstanding — "A cooperative society cannot earn a profit". It can; the profit is simply shared among or kept for members as per cooperative rules, not as profit for investors.

## What happens to the profit

- **Society:** A part of the net profit (surplus) goes to the mandatory **reserve fund** and (in many states) to an **education fund**; the rest goes to member welfare/patronage dividend — and the dividend often has a rule-based cap. (See: [Profit distribution and reserves](/blog/profit-distribution-and-reserves).)
- **Company:** Profit goes mainly as **dividend to shareholders** and to reserves, in proportion to shares.

## Example: the same profit, two paths

Suppose a surplus/profit of ₹1,00,000 is left over in the year. Its path will differ in the two structures:

- **In a cooperative society:** First, as per the rules, a part goes to the **reserve fund** (and in many states to the education fund), then the rest goes **to members in proportion to their use (patronage)** — that is, whoever did more business with the society gets more benefit; not based on how many shares they hold. The dividend often has a rule-based cap too.
- **In a company:** The same ₹1,00,000 goes mainly as **dividend to shareholders in proportion to their shares** — whoever holds more shares gets more.

This one example shows the difference in the "soul" of the two — in a society, profit is linked to **use**; in a company, to **capital**.

## How this affects accounting

Because control and purpose differ:

- A society has separate arrangements such as the **annual general meeting, elections, mandatory reserve fund, dividend cap, and Registrar audit**.
- Accounts are made according to this structure — member-wise shares/loans, patronage, reserve transfers.
- This is why ordinary business/company software does not fully fit a society.

> 💡 That is why, for a society, choose software built for societies. (See: [How to choose software for a society](/blog/choosing-cooperative-accounting-software).)

## When each structure is right

- A **cooperative society** when the purpose is the **shared, democratic service** of a group (farmers, milk producers, consumers, employees) — where every member is equal.
- A **company** when the goal is to run a **profit-focused business by raising capital** and control/profit in proportion to investment is acceptable.

> 💡 Both have their own advantages — the choice depends on your purpose, not on one being "better".

## Advantages and limits of both

**Cooperative society — advantages:** democratic (one-member-one-vote), centred on member welfare, tax relief like Section 80P, the collective strength of small people, access to government cooperative schemes.
**Limits:** raising capital is slow (shares not in the market), decisions take time (democratic process), Registrar control and annual compliance.

**Company — advantages:** raising capital is easy (shares/investment), quick decisions (board), large-scale expansion.
**Limits:** control rests with capital (the small stakeholder falls behind), purpose is profit-focused, no cooperative-specific tax relief.

> 💡 That is, where a group needs shared service and equality, a society; where big capital and fast expansion are needed, a company.

## Common confusions / myths

- **"A society means a government body"** — No; a society is an autonomous, member-controlled body (even if it has links with or schemes from the government).
- **"Profit is forbidden in a society"** — No; earning a profit is fine, only the sharing is rule-based (reserve + patronage).
- **"A company is always better"** — No; for a group's shared service and equality, a society is often better.
- **"The audit of both is the same"** — No; a society's annual audit is done by an auditor from the Registrar's panel within the cooperative framework, a company's by a CA/appointed auditor under the Companies Act. (See: [Types of cooperative societies](/blog/cooperative-society-types-guide).)

## The growing importance of cooperatives in India

To give cooperatives a separate identity in India, the **Ministry of Cooperation** was formed in 2021, and the **National Cooperative Policy** came in 2025 — with the goal "Sahakar se Samriddhi" (prosperity through cooperation). That is, alongside the company model, the cooperative model is also being strengthened, especially for rural and small producers, where "an equal vote for all" and "member welfare" matter more. (See: [Types of cooperative societies](/blog/cooperative-society-types-guide).)

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a cooperative society a company?** — No; it is a separate body formed under cooperative law (State Act/MSCS Act), not under the Companies Act.
**Do more shares give more control?** — In a company, yes; in a society, no — there every member has only one vote.
**How many members are needed at minimum to form a society?** — Usually 10 (may vary by state); a company is formed with 1–7.
**Can a society keep a surplus/profit?** — Yes; it is shared or kept for reserves and member welfare as per the rules.
**What is the difference in tax?** — A society may get a Section 80P deduction as per eligibility; a company pays normal company tax rates.
**Is liability limited in both?** — Yes, in both the liability of a member/shareholder is usually limited to their contribution.
**Can a society be converted into a company?** — In some cases there are procedures for institutional conversion, but this is complex and depends on the law — take advice from a CA/Registrar.
**On what basis is the dividend given?** — In a society, in proportion to the member's use (patronage) (within the rule cap); in a company, in proportion to shareholding.
**Can a society's shares be sold in the market?** — No; a society's shares are not traded in the market, their transfer/refund is subject to the bye-laws and rules.
**Is the registrar of the two different?** — Yes; a company comes under the RoC (MCA), a cooperative society under the Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RCS/CRCS).

## Three things to remember
- 👥 Control rests with people (one member, one vote), not with money.
- 🎯 The purpose is member welfare, not maximum profit.
- 🧾 That is why cooperative accounting, tax (80P) and governance differ from a company.

## In the end
Understanding the "cooperative vs company" difference is understanding the soul of cooperation — control with people, purpose as member welfare, and profit in rule-based sharing. Both are valid and useful bodies; only their paths differ. The day you understand this difference clearly, the society's AGM, elections, reserve, patronage and 80P — all start to make sense. This is what decides how your society's accounts, tax and governance should be kept — and this is what makes it different from a company and lasting.
